00

Backpropagation is a leaky abstraction

Frameworks make loss.backward() feel like magic: stack differentiable blocks, call one method, and parameters receive .grad. In practice, things do not “just work.” Wrong shapes, bad broadcasting, fused-op mismatches, dead nonlinearities, and mistaken “fixes” (clipping the wrong tensor) show up as silent slowdowns or wrong training dynamics.

The leaky abstraction: you can still break the system if you treat autograd as a black box. Debugging and good initialization require knowing how gradients flow — the same lesson as building Value graphs in micrograd, now at tensor granularity.

Those failure modes — saturated activations, dead units, vanishing/exploding signals in deeper nets (especially as you stack depth before we even reach recurrent models), and subtle implementation bugs — are exactly why you cannot treat autograd as a black box. This chapter is the hands-on companion: you re-derive the flows for a concrete MLP.

One failure mode worth keeping in the front of your mind: code that looks like responsible engineering but confuses tensors. Suppose someone wants to cap how hard outliers can pull the optimizer, so they clamp the loss scalar at a maximum. That is not gradient clipping. Clamping the loss at an outlier can drive that example’s gradient toward zero, effectively telling the network to ignore the very cases that often carry the most signal. The intended tool is a norm cap on .grad (or equivalent), not a ceiling on the forward loss. The underlying mistake is treating autograd as a black box: without knowing what depends on what, it is easy to “fix” the wrong tensor.

What autograd gives you

Correctness (usually), speed, and composability across thousands of ops — indispensable for research and production.

What it can hide

Why a layer stops learning, why a bias seems unused, why numerical noise appears in a “dead” branch, and whether your custom op’s backward matches the forward.

01

Why manual tensor backprop still matters

You will not ship production training loops with handwritten d_loss/d_param for every layer. You will use the same mental tools when you debug, implement a custom autograd.Function, or prove a fused kernel matches the expanded graph.

  • Debugging: When loss plateaus, inspect activations and ask whether local derivatives squash the signal.
  • Understanding: The softmax+cross-entropy backward is a famous example where the analytic expression is simpler than the full decomposed graph.
  • Avoiding bugs: += for multi-use nodes, sum where broadcast replicated, and shape checks catch most mistakes before they hide inside a stack of modules.

Historical note: Roughly a decade ago, handwritten backward passes were routine; today autograd is standard. The skill shifted from “write every backward” to “know enough to verify any backward.”

Concrete history makes that shift tangible. Geoffrey Hinton and Ruslan Salakhutdinov’s 2006 Science paper on deep belief nets and restricted Boltzmann machines arrived in an era when you spelled out updates yourself. Training stacks in the wild often used contrastive divergence and related estimators — not always classical backprop through every layer — and gradients were manipulated inline in loops. Research code commonly lived in MATLAB: not a general-purpose language in the modern sense, but a scientific computing environment with a convenient tensor type, plots, and a debugger — and everything ran on CPU.

By the mid-2010s, NumPy and Python had taken over for many projects. A typical paper pipeline (image–text alignment, object-level “fragmented” embeddings, contrastive losses resembling later CLIP-style training) would implement both the forward loss and the backward pass by hand, append regularization terms explicitly, then cross-check against a numerical gradient estimate to prove the analytic code matched finite differences within tolerance. That workflow was boring, error-prone, and educational: you internalized which tensors were reused, where sums accumulated, and why a single missing chain-rule branch could silently pass a weak test.

Then

Hand-derived d_loss/d_param, explicit regularization, gradient checkers against finite differences, and framework-free or minimal-framework training loops.

Now

Composable autograd over thousands of ops — but the same bookkeeping errors (+=, sum axes, broadcast) still appear inside custom layers and fused kernels.

The MLP (same architecture as before)

We backprop through the same character-level MLP from Chapters 03–04 — embedding lookup, batch norm, tanh, and a softmax NLL head. See those chapters for the full forward pass, layer shapes, and the pipeline emb → hprebn → bnraw → tanh → h → logits → softmax → NLL → mean.

Shape anchor for the walkthrough: With batch size B = 32, vocab V = 27, and hidden width nh = 64, logprobs and logits are 32×27. Hidden activations h are 32×64. The embedding lookup packs a 32×3×10 block when three ten-dimensional character vectors are concatenated per example — the same logical tensor can be viewed as 32×30 after view for the first linear layer.

Batch normalization usually makes the first linear bias redundant (affine BN already shifts activations). In the exercise setup, a non-zero b1 is kept anyway: it is “spurious” in the sense that you could delete it in a cleaner design, but it forces you to prove you can still route gradients correctly into an extra parameter that batch norm could have absorbed.

03

Removing loss.backward()

PyTorch can compute all gradients with one call. For this exercise, you zero param.grad yourself, run forward with explicit intermediates, fill d_* tensors from loss downward, then assign param.grad = d_param (or accumulate). The only “black box” left should be efficient tensor kernels — not the graph semantics.

Exercise arc: (1) backprop through every atomic op and cmp to autograd; (2) fuse cross-entropy+softmax into one d_logits; (3) fuse batch norm similarly; (4) plug into the full training loop.

Exercise 1 is the microscope: retain every intermediate (retain_grad()), name matching d_* tensors, and verify each edge against PyTorch. Exercise 2 is the shortcut you actually want in libraries: collapse the decomposed softmax + negative log-likelihood into a single analytic d_logits — same mathematics, far fewer round-trips. Exercise 3 repeats that idea for batch norm: one forward “block,” one backward formula derived on paper, then tensored across features. Exercise 4 drops the assembled backward into a real training loop, compares to autograd for a short run (often ~100 steps) until cmp shows only tiny float drift, then trains end-to-end under torch.no_grad() with manually maintained d_param buffers copied into .grad or zipped directly into the optimizer step.

After the manual backward completes, copy each d_param into param.grad inside torch.no_grad() (or bypass .grad entirely and zip manual tensors with parameters in the optimizer step). The point is to signal to PyTorch that no reverse-mode tape is required for this block — tensors are dumb arrays with efficient BLAS-backed matmuls, and the calculus is yours.

04

Naming gradients: d_ tensors and retain_grad()

For each forward tensor x, store d_x = ∂loss/∂x with the same shape as x. Call x.retain_grad() on intermediates when you want PyTorch’s reference x.grad for cmp().

Initialization trick: tiny random biases (not all zeros) can unmask shape bugs — an all-zero bias sometimes makes wrong gradients look accidentally plausible.

The cmp(s, dt, t) helper prints three numbers: exact bitwise equality of your manual tensor dt against t.grad, approx via torch.allclose (tolerance for fused paths and reorderings), and max|Δ| — the largest absolute elementwise gap. When something is wrong, max|Δ| is usually the fastest clue.

Broadcast backward always pairs with a sum because forward broadcast reuses the same storage across many uses — gradients arriving at those reused slots must add. A column vector replicated across 27 columns in a forward multiply becomes, in backward, a sum across those 27 incoming sensitivities so the column’s shape is restored.

05

Manual backward: cross-entropy at logprobs

Suppose loss = -mean(logprobs[range(B), Yb]). Only B entries of the (B, V) tensor participate. Mean introduces a factor 1/B.

∂loss/∂logprobs[i,j] = 0 if j ≠ Yb[i]; else −1/B

Beachhead strategy: derive the scalar case for three plucked values -(a+b+c)/3; ∂/∂a = -1/3. Generalize to batch size B.

Expand the beachhead explicitly. The loss is loss = -mean(logprobs[range(B), Yb]). Algebraically, mean divides by B, and the outer minus sign flips the sign. For three scalars a, b, c drawn from three rows’ correct columns, loss = -⅓(a + b + c) = (-⅓)a + (-⅓)b + (-⅓)c, so ∂loss/∂a = -⅓ (and similarly for b, c). With B = 32 instead of 3, every selected log-probability carries ∂loss/∂logprob = -1/32.

Indexing detail: Yb holds the correct class index per row. For each batch row i, you read column Yb[i] — e.g. row 0 might pluck column 8, row 1 column 14, row 2 column 15, and so on. That yields exactly B numbers that feed the mean; the other B×(V-1) entries of logprobs never influence the loss, so their partial derivatives are zero. Implementation: d_logprobs = zeros_like(logprobs), then d_logprobs[arange(B), Yb] = -1.0 / B.

Sanity check: When the decomposed forward matches autograd’s graph, cmp('logprobs', d_logprobs, logprobs) reports exact=True, approx=True, and max|Δ|=0.0 — your first proof that naming and shapes line up.

06

Backward through log and the softmax stack

logprobs = probs.log() gives d_probs = (1/probs) * d_logprobs elementwise. Low probability on the correct class amplifies the gradient — the model is pushed harder to fix confident mistakes.

Stable softmax forward: subtract row max, counts = exp(norm_logits), probs = counts * (1/counts.sum(-1, keepdim=True)) (or counts * counts_sum**(-1) to avoid a division backward quirk in some versions). Backward through multiply + broadcast + sum mirrors micrograd’s “multi-use node → sum gradients.”

Why counts_sum ** (-1) instead of 1 / counts_sum? For this pedagogy notebook, ** -1 is used because some PyTorch builds exhibited awkward numerical behavior on the backward of plain division for this subgraph. The forward value is identical; the backward path differs at the implementation level.

Toy picture for probs = counts * count_sum_inv with counts shaped 3×3 and count_sum_inv shaped 3×1: PyTorch first replicates each entry of the column across all 27 (here 3) columns, then multiplies elementwise. Backward through the multiply alone gives d_count_sum_inv = (counts * d_probs).sum(1, keepdim=True) — the local derivative w.r.t. the replicated column is the other multiplicand counts, and replication’s backward is the sum across columns, analogous to micrograd’s rule: one variable, many uses, gradients add.

For counts = exp(norm_logits), the local derivative of exp is exp itself; you already computed counts in the forward pass, so d_norm_logits = counts * d_counts reuses that tensor.

Through norm_logits = logits - logit_maxes, the minus sign routes d_logits = d_norm_logits.clone() and d_logit_maxes = -d_norm_logits.sum(1, keepdim=True) because logit_maxes is a 32×1 column broadcast across 27 classes. That is not the end of the story for logits: logit_maxes = logits.max(1, keepdim=True).values creates a second branch. Scatter d_logit_maxes back with a one-hot mask of argmax indices (e.g. F.one_hot(..., num_classes=V) scaled by the column), and add into d_logits with +=.

Subtracting the row max only stabilizes softmax, so the ideal sensitivity of the loss to logit_maxes is negligible — yet when you decompose the graph into explicit max, subtract, exp, and normalize, finite arithmetic leaves tiny residuals, often on the order of 1e−9 or 1e−10 in max|Δ| for that tensor. A fused cross_entropy kernel typically never materializes this branch at all; the piecewise backward is still correct, just more verbose.

Broadcasting backward rule: if forward replicated a tensor along a dimension, backward sums along that dimension (often keepdim=True so shapes align with the parameter).

probs = counts * count_sum_inv

Elementwise multiply: local derivatives swap roles. d_count_sum_inv = (counts * d_probs).sum(1, keepdim=True).

Column vector reused across columns → backward sums across those columns. Another vocabulary for the same rule: a forward sum acts as a router — every summand receives the full upstream gradient (scaled by local 1s); replication’s backward is the inverse pattern.

07

Backward through logits = h @ W2 + b2

Write a tiny 2×2 example: D = A @ B + C with C broadcast as rows. Differentiate w.r.t. entries of A: each a_ij appears in multiple outputs → sum contributions. You recover the compact rules:

dA = dD @ Bᵀ  dB = Aᵀ @ dD  dC = sum(dD, dim=batch)

First-principles derivation (2×2 case). Write D = A @ B + C_broadcast, expanding entries so d₁₁ = a₁₁b₁₁ + a₁₂b₂₁ + c₁ (first row of A dotted with first column of B, plus the broadcast bias row). The scalar a₁₁ appears in both d₁₁ and d₁₂ when you write the full grid — so by the chain rule, ∂L/∂a₁₁ = (∂L/∂d₁₁)(∂d₁₁/∂a₁₁) + (∂L/∂d₁₂)(∂d₁₂/∂a₁₁) = (∂L/∂d₁₁)b₁₁ + (∂L/∂d₁₂)b₁₂. Stacking every a_ij recovers a matrix product: the gradient w.r.t. A is dD @ Bᵀ. Symmetric reasoning gives dB = Aᵀ @ dD. For the broadcast bias row c₁, c₂ copied down each column of D, each c_k touches an entire column of D; routing gradients backward is summing down that column — hence dC = dD.sum(0) (with the exact reduction axis chosen to kill the batch dimension).

Shapes must match: d_h is (B, nh), d_W2 is (nh, V), d_logits is (B, V). If you know two shapes, often only one matmul orientation is legal — that is the formula.

Practical secret: You do not need to memorize dA vs dB transposes. Fix d_h’s shape to 32×64, d_logits to 32×27, and W2 to 64×27. There is only one valid contraction order that multiplies those pieces into a 32×64 tensor: d_h = d_logits @ W2.T. For d_W2, the unique 64×27 contraction is h.T @ d_logits. For b2 (length 27), the only reduction of a 32×27 sensitivity that yields length 27 is d_logits.sum(0).

Backward through tanh

Let h = tanh(hpreact). In terms of the output h, the local derivative is 1 - h² (not sech²(z) evaluated at pre-activation, though they are equivalent). Chain: d_hpreact = (1 - h**2) * d_h.

Reference tables often quote d/dz tanh(z) = sech²(z), which is correct but easy to misapply at 2 a.m. The algebraically equivalent form 1 − tanh²(z) lets you read the local slope directly from the tensor you already stored as h. If you expand the definition of tanh and differentiate carefully, you recover the same factor; a picture search for the derivative of tanh is a fast sanity check when symbolic engines return the hyperbolic secant form first.

d/dz tanh(z) = 1 − tanh²(z)

09

Backward through batch normalization (the hardest layer)

Forward (training): per-channel mean/variance across batch, optional Bessel correction 1/(B−1) for variance, ε for stability, then bnraw = (x−μ)/σ, hpreact = gain * bnraw + bias. Gain and bias broadcast like any row-vector scale/shift.

Variance estimator nuance: training code in some stacks uses biased 1/B while running stats use 1/(B−1) — a train/test mismatch worth knowing when you debug BN. The exercise code uses 1/(B−1) consistently so denominators stay aligned.

Sum ↔ broadcast duality: a sum in forward (e.g. reduce batch dimension) becomes replication/broadcasting in backward, and replication in forward becomes sum in backward — the pattern you already saw in softmax normalization.

Think of a forward sum as a funnel: many children feed one parent; backward is a sprinkler that returns the parent’s gradient to every child (scaled by local derivatives). Conversely, broadcast replication in forward is one parent pretending to be many children; backward must fold those paths back with a sum.

Through bndiff2 = bndiff**2 followed by variance reduction, bndiff is a multi-use node: one path feeds bnraw = bndiff * bnvar_inv, another path feeds the variance. The first partial d_bndiff from the normalization head is therefore incomplete until the variance branch contributes; the second installment uses += exactly like softmax’s two uses of counts. If you cmp too early, you want the checker to fail — it confirms the test harness is not lying.

For the mean’s backward through bndiff = hprebn - bnmean, the immediate assignment d_hprebn = d_bndiff.clone() is again only part of the answer: bnmean itself depends on hprebn, so a second branch adds (1.0/B) * d_bnmean after summing routed gradients into d_bnmean. Until both pieces land, hprebn looks wrong on purpose.

To implement sum backward without hand-building a replicate tensor, multiply by a ones tensor of the child shape and let broadcasting do the expansion — for softmax’s count_sum backward, d_counts += torch.ones_like(counts) * d_count_sum (equivalently, an explicit expand/broadcast_to).

Backward through the embedding layer

emb = C[xb] gathers rows. Backward deposits d_emb into d_C at the same indices. Repeated characters in the batch mean the same row is used many times → accumulate with +=.

Concretely, xb might be 32×3 integer indices into a table C of shape 27×10 (27 characters, 10-D embeddings). The forward packs a 32×3×10 tensor of plucked rows. A row of xb might read [1, 1, 4] for one training example: three context characters identified by those ids, used to predict the next token. Backward routes each d_emb[k, j, :] to d_C[xb[k,j], :]; duplicate ids in the batch (the 1 appears twice in the example) mean two slices add into the same row of d_C, hence += rather than assignment. A double Python loop over batch and time is clear; a vectorized scatter-add is faster in production kernels but not required for understanding.

12

Common bugs and the gradient-clipping confusion

Real bug pattern: code that intends to clip gradients instead clips the loss scalar. Clamping outliers on the loss can zero their contribution’s derivative and distort optimization — the author likely confused “limit gradient norm” with “limit loss value.”

The failure mode is subtle because both ideas sound like “stability.” A clipped loss changes which examples contribute curvature: once an example’s loss hits the ceiling, variations that should nudge parameters no longer do. Gradient clipping, by contrast, leaves per-example losses untouched and only rescales the aggregate update step when the norm of parameter gradients grows too large. The tensors live in different spaces; the fixes are not interchangeable.

Remember: clipping loss is not the same as torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_. They operate on different tensors with different meanings.

  • Forgetting += when a tensor is used twice (softmax branch, bndiff, embedding rows).
  • Using sum(..., keepdim=False when the target param still needs a broadcast dimension.
  • Trusting “almost zero” branches (e.g. logit_maxes) — numerically tiny gradients are expected; they reflect shift invariance of softmax.
13

Efficiency: fusing operations

Decomposed backward is pedagogical; production uses fused forward+backward for softmax+cross-entropy and for batch norm. The analytic shortcut for mean NLL + softmax:

d_logits = (softmax(logits) − one_hot(Y)) / B  (rows sum to 0)

Intuition: every row’s gradient is a “force” pulling down wrong-class mass and pulling up the correct class; total pull cancels so the row sums to zero.

Analytic derivation sketch: For one example, write loss = −log p_y with p_y the softmax probability at label y. Let L_i be logits. Expand p_y = exp(L_y) / Σ_j exp(L_j) and differentiate ∂loss/∂L_i by cases — i = y vs i ≠ y. The quotients simplify; you obtain p_i − 1 on the label coordinate and p_i elsewhere, which is exactly softmax(L) − one_hot(y) as a row vector. A batch mean introduces an extra factor 1/B on every row.

Mechanical picture: Visualize softmax(logits) as the forward probabilities. Subtract 1 at each row’s true class index (implemented as -= 1 at (arange(B), Yb) after cloning probs), then divide by B. Row-wise, the entries act like opposing tensions: negative mass on incorrect classes, positive correction on the true class, arranged so the row sums to zero — total downward pressure on wrong logits balances the upward pull on the right one. The magnitudes scale with how wrong the probabilities were: a confidently wrong class suffers a large downward gradient; a nearly perfect one-hot forward pass yields a nearly zero row.

If forward probabilities were already ideal (a hard 1 on the label, 0 elsewhere), the fused row of d_logits (before the 1/B scale) is all zeros — no push, no pull, nothing to learn from that example at that step.

Fused batch-norm backward (paper symbols, pencil work)

Align notation with the original batch-normalization paper for a single feature dimension: input x_i, batch mean μ, variance σ², normalized x̂_i = (x_i − μ)/√(σ² + ε), and affine output y_i = γ x̂_i + β. Given upstream ∂L/∂y_i, backward through the affine scale first: ∂L/∂x̂_i = γ · ∂L/∂y_i (for the exercise that collapses gain/bias, set γ = 1 mentally).

Unlike the vectors x and x̂, the moments μ and σ² are scalars per feature with enormous fan-out: many arrows leave each moment node in the compute graph. Backward therefore sums contributions from every x̂_i that depended on them. A productive order is topological, right-to-left: x̂ → σ² → μ → x, the same discipline as sorting a micrograd graph.

For ∂L/∂σ², sum over i: each x̂_i depends on σ², so accumulate Σ_i (∂L/∂x̂_i)(∂x̂_i/∂σ²). For ∂L/∂μ, count carefully: μ influences every x̂_i and also influences σ², which then influences every x̂_i again. With batch size B = 32, one mental picture is 32 paths through the x̂ branch plus 1 path through σ² — 33 arrows to sum for the mean’s global gradient. When you expand ∂σ²/∂μ for the definition of σ² as the centered second moment, the term cancels exactly in the special case where μ is the empirical average of the x_i; the second chunk of the mean’s gradient vanishes, leaving a compact expression.

Finally, each scalar input x_i feeds three visible dependencies: μ, σ², and its own x̂_i (which depends on all x only through μ and σ²). Chain-rule contributions add three grouped terms. When you substitute earlier partials, rename summation indices (i vs j) so you do not collide with the free index of ∂L/∂x_i. After algebra, factors of (σ² + ε)^{-½} and (σ² + ε)^{-³⁄₂} appear; one trick is to factor (σ² + ε)^{-³⁄₂} as three copies of (σ² + ε)^{-½} and distribute them across terms — pieces recombine until x̂_i shows up cleanly in the numerator.

Vectorizing that closed form to a B×C hidden layer means the entire derivation runs independently per channel (e.g. 64 neurons): each column shares the same scalar algebra but uses its own μ, σ², x̂ statistics. Every reduction must use the correct keepdim so broadcasts line up — getting the fused BN backward into one tensor expression is its own shape puzzle even after the symbols are correct.

14

Dead neurons, saturated tanh, and depth

As covered in Chapter 04, when |h| → 1, 1−h² → 0 shrinks gradients through that unit; if an entire column sits in the flat tail, you have a dead neuron for that step. ReLU fails differently — always-negative pre-activations give exactly zero gradient — and depth compounds vanishing/exploding effects that shallow nets forgive more easily.

Diagnostic habit: histogram h.abs() > 0.99 across batch×hidden; look for entire columns “always saturated” vs healthy mix.
