Attention & Transformer Language Models

From recurrent bottlenecks to parallel self-attention — how the attention mechanism solved RNN limitations and gave rise to the transformer architecture that powers today's large language models.

1 Playground

Recurrent neural networks process sequences one token at a time, creating bottlenecks and vanishing gradients. The attention mechanism lets each position attend directly to all previous positions — and when combined with multi-head attention, residual connections, and feed-forward layers, it becomes the transformer: the architecture behind GPT, Claude, and every modern LLM.

00

Overview: From RNNs to Transformers

The transformer architecture, introduced in the 2017 paper "Attention Is All You Need" by Vaswani et al. at Google, has become the dominant paradigm for language modeling. Before transformers, recurrent neural networks (RNNs) were the standard — but they suffered from fundamental limitations that attention and the transformer architecture addressed.

Historical Context

Attention was originally introduced around 2014 (in work by Bahdanau et al.) as an add-on for RNNs in machine translation — a way to alleviate the bottleneck of compressing the entire prefix into a single hidden state. A few years later, researchers realized that recurrence might not be necessary at all: the transformer paper dropped recurrence completely and moved to self-attention as the core mechanism.

Since 2017, no architecture has dethroned the transformer. LSTMs, GRUs, and other RNN variants each had their moment — but the transformer has remained the foundation for nearly a decade. Most advances in language modeling have come from scaling up this architecture: more layers, more parameters, and more data.

The original transformer was introduced at NeurIPS 2017 by researchers at Google under the now-iconic title Attention Is All You Need (Vaswani et al.). The paper’s core claim is architectural: a model built entirely from attention can replace recurrence (and, for their setup, convolutions) for sequence transduction while training faster through parallelism. In their words: “We propose a new simple network architecture, the Transformer, based solely on attention mechanisms, dispensing with recurrence and convolutions entirely.”

Earlier, Bahdanau et al. (2014) (“Neural Machine Translation by Jointly Learning to Align and Translate”) introduced attention as an add-on to recurrent encoder–decoder models for machine translation. That line of work targeted the bottleneck of compressing a source sentence into one vector. The step from there to the transformer was to ask whether recurrence itself was still necessary once attention could directly connect positions.

Reading the lineage: 2014 attention-on-RNNs → 2017 self-attention-only transformer. The transformer did not appear in a vacuum; it dropped the recurrence that attention had originally been designed to augment.

01

Limitations of Recurrent Neural Networks

RNNs route the entire prefix through a single hidden state at each step. That design creates three problems attention was built to fix: vanishing or exploding gradients over long spans, a bottleneck that compresses an ever-longer prefix into one fixed-size vector, and no parallelization across time steps during training. How those failures show up in the forward pass, the next-token loss at every position, and BPTT is developed in Chapter 03.

Wider recurrent states (state-space models such as Mamba) ease the bottleneck but still funnel the past through a sequential update path. Attention instead gives each position direct, weighted access to earlier representations — the mechanism the rest of this chapter develops.

RNN along time

Ht must wait for Ht−1. One forward sweep along the sequence is inherently sequential. Training uses all positions’ losses, but the hidden-state recurrence is still a chain.

Self-attention within a layer

All Q, K, V rows for the layer can be built from known inputs in one shot; attention outputs at different positions do not wait on each other’s hidden states the way RNN steps do. (Causal masking only limits which keys each query may use — it does not reintroduce an RNN-style recurrence across depth-at-time.)

Key insight: Residual connections help with vertical scaling (depth) in neural networks, but they don't address the horizontal scaling problem in RNNs — the sequential dependency between time steps. Attention is the solution for that.

02

Attention: A Solution to the Bottleneck

The high-level intuition behind attention: instead of forcing all relevant information into a single hidden state, let the model look at previous hidden states when needed. At each time step, before predicting the next word, the model can "attend" to H1, H2, ..., Ht — shortcut connections to earlier positions so that information doesn't have to be compressed into one vector.

In the original Bahdanau attention (2014), this was used with RNNs for machine translation: the decoder could attend to all encoder hidden states. The transformer later dropped recurrence entirely and built the entire model from self-attention — where we attend over the same sequence we're processing.

Attention as Soft Lookup

Think of attention as a soft information retrieval mechanism. At each position, a query is compared against keys from allowed earlier positions; matching keys receive higher softmax weight, and the output is a weighted sum of their values. Bahdanau attention (2014) introduced this pattern to give RNN decoders shortcuts to earlier states; in transformers, the same idea lets each position retrieve detail from the prefix without routing everything through one vector.

03

Query, Key, and Value Formulation

At each time step, we project the input (word embedding or previous layer's hidden state) into three vectors: Query (Q), Key (K), and Value (V). Each uses a different linear layer:

Qi = WQ · xi    Ki = WK · xi    Vi = WV · xi

The same weight matrices WQ, WK, WV are shared across all time steps. Only the input changes.

Why Separate Q, K, and V?

A common question: why not use the same vector for everything? If we used the same vector as both query and key, the dot product of a vector with itself would typically be highest — we'd always assign the most attention to the current position. Separate projections allow the model to focus on faraway positions when needed. They also add parameters, which helps as we scale up.

Dot-Product Attention

Geometrically, a dot product measures alignment between vectors (length-weighted cosine when both are unit norm). In attention, we do not hard-code linguistic roles; we train the linear maps WQ and WK so that pairs (Qi, Kj) with large dot products tend to correspond to positions j whose information is useful for the task at position i — for language modeling, useful for predicting the next token. High score therefore means “this key’s value should be mixed in strongly,” not “these words are synonyms in a dictionary sense.”

We compute attention scores as the dot product between the query at the current position and each key:

scorei,j = Qi · KjT

A higher dot product means that position j is more relevant to the prediction at position i. These scores are unbounded and hard to interpret, so we apply softmax to turn them into a probability distribution — the attention weights. We then take a weighted sum of the value vectors. A common question: why have a separate value vector? We could just weight the inputs directly. The value projection adds parameters and flexibility — the model can transform what it retrieves before summing, and this was found to improve performance.

Hi = Σj attention_weighti,j · Vj

Concrete weights: a three-token prefix

Take the third position of a prefix and suppose softmax over keys {1, 2, 3} yields weights 0.30, 0.50, 0.20 on positions 1, 2, and 3 respectively. Then the self-attention output at that position is literally a vector weighted sum of the value vectors:

H3 = 0.30 · V1 + 0.50 · V2 + 0.20 · V3

Each scalar multiplies an entire vector (broadcast over dimensions); the result has the same dimensionality as each Vj. That H3 is then passed through the usual output projection and, at the top of the network, into the vocabulary softmax — the same pattern as RNN language models, only the construction of Ht changed.

At the second position, only keys 1 and 2 are legal under causal decoding. Example weights might be 0.30 on position 1 and 0.70 on position 2, giving H2 = 0.30 V1 + 0.70 V2. The mass on any future position is exactly zero after masking — not “small,” but zero — so the future cannot silently leak in through a tiny weight.

At the first position, causal attention has only one legal key — the token itself. The softmax puts 100% of the mass on that position, so H1 collapses to V1 (up to numerical details). Later positions are where mixing across the prefix actually happens.

Scaled Dot-Product Attention

In practice, we divide the dot product by √dk (where dk is the key dimension) before softmax. Without scaling, when dk is large, dot products can grow large and push softmax into regions where gradients are tiny. Scaling keeps the scores in a reasonable range.

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax(QKT / √dk) · V

04

Self-Attention

In self-attention, the queries, keys, and values all come from the same sequence. We're attending over our own input — each position can look at every other position (subject to masking, which we'll cover next). This is in contrast to cross-attention, where Q comes from one sequence and K, V from another (e.g., decoder attending to encoder outputs).

The key advantage: no dependency between Ht and Ht−1. To compute H3, we only need Q3, K1..3, and V1..3 — all of which can be computed in parallel from the input embeddings. We can compute all hidden states in a layer with just two matrix multiplications: QKT, then softmax and multiply by V.

Parallelization: This observation — that self-attention has no sequential dependency — is what made the transformer possible. Training is much faster than RNNs because we can compute an entire layer in parallel. At test time (inference), we still generate one token at a time, so the speed difference is less pronounced — but training efficiency was the breakthrough.

Parallelism across positions in a layer does not mean every query “sees the future.” For autoregressive training we still apply a causal mask. What parallelizes is the batched linear algebra: once embeddings (and position information) are known for an entire training sequence, every row’s allowed dot-products, mask, softmax, and weighted values can be scheduled as wide matrix operations on a GPU. There is no hidden-state recurrence of the form Ht = f(Ht−1) inside that self-attention layer, so you are not forced to finish timestep t−1 before starting timestep t the way an RNN core would be.

Long-range access is the other side of the coin. In the unmasked (or already-masked) setup, nothing in principle stops position 9,999 from assigning large weight to position 2 — the model is not required to squeeze token 2’s contribution through 9,997 sequential updates. That flexibility is exactly what the RNN bottleneck made difficult.

Training vs. inference: With the full target sequence in the batch, every position in a layer can be computed in parallel under a causal mask (the shared next-token objective is covered in Chapter 03). At inference the model appends one token at a time — the outer loop resembles an RNN even though attention over the growing prefix is not recurrent. Teacher forcing and exposure bias return in Chapter 08 and Chapter 09.

05

Multi-Head Attention

Instead of one set of Q, K, V projections, we have multiple heads — each with its own WQ, WK, WV. Each head computes attention independently, and we concatenate the outputs and pass them through a final linear layer to produce the layer output.

Why Multiple Heads?

The intuition: different heads can learn to focus on different linguistic properties. One head might focus on syntax (e.g., attending to all verbs). Another might focus on named entities or specific topics. Some heads might always put high attention on the last few words (recent context is often crucial); others might look further back. We don't constrain the model — we just give it the capacity to specialize.

In practice, it's hard to interpret what each head does. The number of heads is a hyperparameter (often 8, 16, or 32). Each head can be computed in parallel — there are no dependencies between heads.

The original authors motivate multi-head attention informally: with several projection triples (WQ(h), WK(h), WV(h)), each head can specialize — one might track syntax-like regularities (e.g., attending to verbs), another might track named entities or numeric spans, another might emphasize recent tokens (often crucial for local coherence) while a sibling head looks further back. These are not hand-assigned roles; the model discovers patterns that minimize next-token loss. Some heads do end up strongly position-focused (last token, last two tokens); others spread mass more evenly. Interpretation remains partial and fragile, but the capacity argument is clear: one head must be a jack-of-all-trades; many heads can partition labor.

Engineering detail: implementations often perform one large linear projection from the model dimension to the concatenated Q, K, V slots for all heads, then reshape and slice into per-head matrices. That is mathematically equivalent to separate matrices per head but can be faster on hardware. After attention inside each head, the head outputs are concatenated and multiplied by WO, mixing heads and typically re-projecting to the model width.

Single-head self-attention

One (WQ, WK, WV) triple. Simpler, fewer parameters per layer, but only one attention pattern family at that depth.

Multi-head self-attention

Multiple independent attention maps in parallel, then concatenation and WO. More parameters, more representational routes, and empirical gains on most large-scale language tasks.

Hyperparameters: How many heads, how wide each head, how many layers — there is no closed-form “optimal” recipe. Large labs sweep many configurations; practitioners often choose powers of two for head counts out of hardware convenience, then validate on dev loss or downstream tasks.

The Computation

For each head h, we compute headh = Attention(Qh, Kh, Vh). We concatenate all heads and multiply by WO (the output projection):

MultiHead(Q, K, V) = Concat(head1, ..., headH) · WO

06

Causal Masking for Autoregressive Generation

When we compute QKT, we get a full matrix — each query attends to all keys. But for language modeling, we must not look at the future. When predicting the word at position i, we only have access to positions 1 through i. Including future positions would be cheating — we'd be using the answer to predict the answer.

The Mask

We apply a causal mask (also called a triangular or autoregressive mask): set all positions j > i to −∞ before softmax. After softmax, those positions get probability 0. The result is a lower-triangular attention matrix — each row has nonzero weights only for positions up to and including the current one.

In implementation: we add a mask matrix (0 for valid, −∞ for masked) to the scores before softmax, or use a "masked fill" that sets masked cells to −∞.

Why −∞ (or a large negative sentinel), not zero, before softmax? Writing the softmax as pi = exp(zi) / Σj exp(zj), a masked position needs its numerator exp(zmasked) to go to 0, which requires zmasked → −∞, not a finite zero logit. If you merely zero out a forbidden cell but leave other logits finite, that cell still gets positive probability mass. Setting the logit to negative infinity drives its softmax output to exactly zero, removing any influence on the weighted sum of values. Libraries such as PyTorch expose row-wise softmax over a chosen dimension; causal masking is applied per query row so each position’s weights form a proper distribution over allowed keys.

softmax(z)i = exp(zi) / Σj exp(zj)   (row-wise over keys j for each query row)

Matrix form: three steps for the whole sequence

Pack all queries as rows of Q, keys as columns of K (equivalently KT as the right-hand factor), values as rows stacked into V. For a length-n sequence, the raw score matrix is n × n; for 1,000 tokens it is 1,000 × 1,000 per head before masking. Then:

  1. Scores: multiply Q by KT (and apply the 1/√dk scale in scaled dot-product attention).
  2. Mask + softmax: force upper-triangle (future) cells to −∞, then take row-wise softmax so each row sums to 1.
  3. Mix values: multiply the attention matrix by V; row i is Hi.

Only two large matrix multiplications (scores, then values) materialize all positions’ outputs for that layer — the reason self-attention is so friendly to accelerators when sequence length is manageable.

Why excluding future keys matters: Value vector Vj is a function of token j’s input. If position 2 could attend with nonzero weight to position 3’s key/value while training to predict the token after position 2, the network could cheat by reading ahead — in the extreme, putting 100% weight on the very next token and copying information it should not know at that timestep. Causal masking forces the model to respect information availability at each autoregressive step.

When Do We Need Masking?

For decoder-only language models (GPT-style), we always use causal masking — we're predicting the next word at each step. For encoder models (e.g., BERT-style), we might not mask — we're not doing next-word prediction; we might be classifying the entire sequence (e.g., sentiment). In that case, looking at the full sequence is fine. Encoder-decoder models use different masking for encoder vs. decoder.

Real systems mix both ideas. In an assistant, the user prompt tokens are often given in full — the model is not guessing them — while generated continuation tokens still require causal structure among new positions. Implementations therefore use structured mask patterns beyond a single pure lower triangle: prompt tokens may attend bidirectionally within the prompt, while generation remains autoregressive. The unifying question is always: which positions are legitimate inputs at the point where a representation is used?

07

The Transformer Architecture

The transformer is a deep network of stacked blocks. Each block contains:

  1. Masked multi-head self-attention — the Q, K, V projections, multiple heads, causal masking, concatenation, and output projection.
  2. Residual connection — add the block input to the attention output before the next step.
  3. Feed-forward layer — a linear layer (often with ReLU), typically with a hidden dimension 4× the model dimension.
  4. Another residual connection — add the previous output to the feed-forward output.

Papers and codebases often call each repeat unit a block or layer even though, inside it, there are many linear maps, softmaxes, and nonlinearities. When someone says “a 32-layer transformer,” they mean 32 such stacked blocks, not a single attention softmax.

In practice, layer normalization is applied (before or after the residual) — we're omitting it here for simplicity, but it's standard in modern implementations.

Normalization (omitted above): Full transformer stacks also place LayerNorm (and sometimes dropout) around attention and FFN sublayers in specific orders (Pre-LN vs Post-LN). The functional story — attention mixes tokens, residuals preserve paths, FFN processes each position — remains the same; normalization stabilizes scale for deep stacks and large learning rates.

Residual Connections

Residual connections (skip connections) add the input of a layer to its output: output = F(input) + input. This creates a shortcut for gradients — they can flow directly backward without passing through all the layer's computations. This helps with the vanishing gradient problem in deep networks. Both paths (through the layer and through the shortcut) receive gradients during backprop.

The idea was popularized for very deep convolutional nets by He et al. (2015), “Deep Residual Learning for Image Recognition.” In a transformer block, suppose the sublayer produces an update such as Z(ℓ) from attention on the ℓ-th block’s input. A residual tie adds the input of that sublayer back in (dimension must match — model width is fixed by design). Stacking L blocks means the loss at the top can reach early representations either by walking backward through every attention map and FFN, or by hopping through additive shortcuts that skip whole interior chains. During backprop, gradients split across branches; the skip path is not a separate “free prediction” — parameters inside F still learn, but optimization is far less brittle when a near-identity route exists.

x(ℓ+1) = x(ℓ) + Fℓ(x(ℓ))   (schematic residual around sublayer Fℓ)

Positional Encoding

Self-attention by itself has no information about position. If we shuffled the tokens, we'd get the same attention pattern. We must inject position information. The simplest approach: add a position embedding to each token's embedding before the first layer. P1 is added to the first token, P2 to the second, and so on. Sinusoidal, rotary (RoPE), and bias-based schemes are compared in Chapter 06.

Without this injection, self-attention over a bag of embeddings behaves like a permutation-equivariant operator on unordered positions: reorder inputs, reorder outputs the same way — no notion of “second word in the sentence.” Learned absolute position vectors (table lookup by index) are simple but not the only choice; they are sometimes described as a baseline that later positional schemes improve upon, especially for length generalization.

Same word, different positions: Consider "my favorite LLM is really the best LLM" — "LLM" appears twice. Without position information, their keys and values would be identical. Adding position embeddings makes them different: the first "LLM" gets P2, the second gets P7. In deeper layers, the inputs are already different (previous layer's hidden states), so representations diverge further.

Encoder vs. Decoder-Only

The original transformer had both an encoder (bidirectional, no causal mask) and a decoder (causal mask, plus cross-attention to encoder). GPT-style models are decoder-only — just stacked decoder blocks with causal masking. No encoder. This is the dominant architecture for language modeling today.

Training vs. Inference

At training time, we have the full sequence — we can compute all positions in parallel. At inference time, we generate one token at a time. We don't know the next word until we've predicted it. So we compute Q, K, V only for the current position and all previous positions. To avoid recomputing, we use a KV cache — we store the keys and values of all previous tokens and reuse them at each step. Only the new token's K and V need to be computed. The cache grows as we generate longer outputs.

Refining that picture: at decode time you usually need the query only for the current final position (the newly appended token) when producing the next distribution, whereas all prior positions’ keys and values must remain available for attention. Those older K/V tensors do not change when you append a fresh token at the end, so caching them avoids recomputing every layer’s projections for the entire prefix on each step. Across layers and heads, the cache can grow to gigabytes for long contexts — motivating offload to CPU, compression, quantization, and fused kernels in production systems.

Training (parallel forward)

The full sequence is present in the batch. Masked self-attention fills an entire n × n score matrix (then mask) in one kernel launch per layer because no hidden state waits on a previous timestep’s RNN-style recurrence.

Inference (autoregressive decode)

Future tokens do not exist yet. Each step extends the prefix by one; attention runs over all known tokens. Without a KV cache, work would repeat from scratch; with caching, only the new token’s projections append to stored K/V.

Where parameters pile up: output projections and FFNs

In wide models, most parameters often sit not in the raw Q/K/V slices alone but in the post-attention projection WO and especially the position-wise feed-forward networks (the “MLP” sublayer), which expand to ~4× the model dimension and back. A toy counting argument: if you concatenate 32 heads each of dimension 100, the concatenated vector has dimension 3,200; projecting back to a hidden size of 1,000 is already a multi-million-entry matrix. Real stacks multiply that by dozens of layers on each side of attention. This is why scaling laws track parameter count so closely with FFN width and depth.

Pre-training: Transformers are typically pre-trained on massive text corpora to minimize the next-word prediction loss. The resulting models can then be fine-tuned for specific tasks. Scaling — more layers, more heads, more parameters, more data — has driven most of the progress in LLMs.
