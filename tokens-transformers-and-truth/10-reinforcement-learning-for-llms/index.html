Reinforcement Learning Basics

In reinforcement learning, an agent interacts with an environment. At each step, the agent observes a state, takes an action, and receives a reward. The agent's behavior is defined by a policy — a mapping from states to distributions over actions. The goal is to maximize expected cumulative reward.

Language Generation as an RL Problem

Autoregressive language generation fits this framework naturally. The state is the prompt x plus the tokens generated so far. The action at each step is choosing the next token from the vocabulary. The reward is typically computed only at the end of the sequence — after the full response y is produced — by a reward model that assigns a scalar score to the pair (x, y).

The Objective

We want to maximize the expected reward over prompts from our dataset and responses sampled from our current model:

J(θ) = Ex~D, y~pθ(·|x) [ R(x, y) ]

Here pθ is our policy (language model), R is the reward function (often a frozen reward model), and D is the prompt distribution. We cannot enumerate all possible responses, so we approximate this expectation by sampling one or a few responses per prompt.

Reward model: The reward function is typically a frozen model trained on human preferences (see Chapter 9 for Bradley–Terry training and best-of-N). Given (x, y), it outputs a scalar score. During RL, we do not update the reward model — we use it only to score responses.

Key insight: The reward is not differentiable with respect to the model parameters. The response y is produced by sampling — a discrete, stochastic process. We cannot backpropagate through it. Instead, we differentiate through the probability that the model assigns to the sampled response.

What reward models are judged on

When people iterate on reward modeling, they care about more than raw agreement with crowd workers on generic chat. Benchmark-style suites probe whether a model assigns higher scores to factually grounded answers than to confident hallucinations, whether it respects precise instruction following (length constraints, formatting rules, multi-part checklists), whether it ranks correct mathematics above wrong reasoning, and whether it reflects safety norms (for example, refusing harmful requests). Even strong public checkpoints are imperfect: they may still prefer a worse answer part of the time, and correctness-style tasks remain a weak point relative to human judgment on open-ended prose.

Human labels, model labels, and fragility: Preference data are noisy—different annotators often disagree, and difficult prompts rarely admit a single objectively correct ranking. Training a reward model treats every label as equally trustworthy, so the network may latch onto shallow cues (verbosity, polish, a single grammar slip) that predict the dataset instead of true quality. Scaling with LLM-synthesized preferences is attractive for coverage, but it stacks the teacher model's biases on top of the same risk: the reward signal can become easier to game because it is one more approximation removed from humans.

Open reward checkpoints

Published artifacts are often mid-sized language models fine-tuned on large preference datasets. They make the training recipe inspectable and reproducible, which is why leaderboards can compare them directly.

Proprietary judges and API scores

Commercial systems prompted to emit scalar scores may appear next to open models on plots, but the largest, most heavily engineered reward stacks inside major labs typically never enter the same public evaluations. Treat leaderboard numbers as a slice of the ecosystem, not a universal ordering of "true" reward quality.

Verifiable tasks ahead: When a task has an objectively checkable answer (arithmetic, string equality on a solution), a learned reward model is often unnecessary—a cheap verifier or exact match can provide the scalar signal. The RL machinery (policy gradient, advantage, clipping) still applies; only the source of R(x, y) changes.

01

REINFORCE and the Policy Gradient Theorem

The REINFORCE algorithm (Williams, 1992) provides a way to compute the gradient of the expected reward with respect to the policy parameters. It uses a simple mathematical trick — the log probability trick — to rewrite the gradient in a form we can estimate from samples.

The Gradient

For a fixed prompt x, the expected reward is:

J(θ) = Σy pθ(y|x) · R(x,y)

The gradient with respect to θ involves differentiating through pθ(y|x). We multiply and divide by pθ(y|x) (multiplying by 1) and use the identity:

∇θ log pθ(y|x) = (1 / pθ(y|x)) · ∇θ pθ(y|x)

This lets us rewrite the gradient as an expectation:

∇θ J(θ) = Ey~pθ(·|x) [ R(x,y) · ∇θ log pθ(y|x) ]

Adjust the reward to see how the gradient pushes probability up or down. Positive reward increases probability; negative reward decreases it.

The REINFORCE Algorithm

Given a prompt x, we (1) sample a response y from our current model, (2) compute the reward R(x,y) using our frozen reward model, and (3) update the model parameters in the direction of R(x,y) · ∇θ log pθ(y|x). If the reward is high, we increase the probability of that response. If it is low (or negative), we decrease it.

Supervised fine-tuning

The training distribution over targets is effectively anchored to reference text from humans or teachers—a nearly one-hot target token at each step. There is always a concrete y* whose log-likelihood we increase, even if the model would rarely sample that sequence on its own.

Policy-gradient RL on a reward

There is no fixed reference completion. We draw y ~ pθ(·|x), learn whether it was good or bad only through R(x, y), and nudge mass toward or away from that sample. The optimization target is defined by the interaction of sampling and scoring, not by a stored gold sequence.

REINFORCE itself predates large language models by decades; the same score-function gradient identity has served robotics, games, and control. For LLMs it simply reuses the machinery already built for pre-training and SFT—autoregressive log-probabilities and backprop through softmax logits—with the scalar reward inserted as a multiplier on those familiar gradients.

REINFORCE Step-by-Step

Sampling and Batch Size

We cannot enumerate all possible responses. In practice, we sample one to perhaps 16 or 32 responses per prompt to compute the update. More samples give a better estimate of the expected reward but increase decoding cost. Best-of-N sampling (Chapter 9) is the inference-only extreme — no weight update. RL training aims to make a single sample reliably high-reward.

Token-Level Decomposition and Credit Assignment

The log probability of a sequence decomposes into a sum of token-level log probabilities via the chain rule:

log pθ(y|x) = Σi log pθ(yi | x, y1, ..., yi-1)

The reward is received only at the end of the sequence. This creates a credit assignment problem: which tokens contributed to the high or low reward? In simple algorithms like REINFORCE and GRPO, we broadcast the same sequence-level reward to every token position. Each token's gradient is scaled by that reward. PPO uses a more sophisticated approach: a value function predicts token-level expected rewards, giving a different baseline at each position — so tokens earlier in the sequence can receive different advantage estimates than later ones.

Why the log trick? A naive Monte Carlo average of R(x,y) · ∇θ pθ(y|x) with y ~ pθ is biased — it includes an extra factor of pθ(y|x) because we sample from the policy. Dividing by pθ(y|x) corrects this and yields the log trick. Without it, we would not get an unbiased estimate of the true gradient.

Write the naive Monte Carlo average of R(x,y) ∇θ pθ(y|x) with y ~ pθ. Its expectation expands with an extra weight pθ(y|x) inside the sum over outcomes—it is not the gradient of J(θ) = Ey~pθ[R(x,y)]. Multiplying and dividing by pθ(y|x) before differentiating is exactly the correction that turns the estimator into an unbiased policy-gradient sample: reward times ∇θ log pθ(y|x).
02

Baseline Subtraction and Advantage

Using the raw reward in the policy gradient leads to high variance. If a sample gets reward 2, we increase its probability — but what if the model usually gets reward 10 for that prompt? A reward of 2 is actually worse than usual. We want to compare each sample to a baseline.

The Advantage

The advantage is defined as:

A(x, y) = R(x, y) − B(x)

where B(x) is a baseline that may depend on the prompt. If the advantage is positive, the sample is better than usual — we increase its probability. If negative, it is worse than usual — we decrease its probability. Using the advantage instead of the raw reward reduces variance and improves learning.

Baseline Strategies

  • ✗ Mean batch reward: Use the average reward over the entire batch as the baseline. The drawback is that different prompts have different difficulty — comparing across prompts is noisy.
  • ✓ Group statistics: For each prompt x, sample multiple responses (e.g., 8, 16, or 32). Compute the mean (and optionally standard deviation) of rewards over those responses. Use that as the baseline for each sample from that prompt. This gives a prompt-specific baseline and is used in GRPO.
  • ✓ Trained value function: Train a separate model (a critic) that takes the prompt and a partial response and predicts the expected final reward. This provides token-level baselines — different at each position — and is used in PPO/RLHF. The value function is trained with L2 loss and updated alongside the policy.

The value function is more expressive but requires an extra large model in memory. GRPO avoids this by using group statistics, trading some accuracy for simplicity and compute efficiency.

Leave-one-out baselines and the "group" in GRPO

Sampling several completions from the same prompt and centering each reward by the group's mean (and often normalizing by the group's standard deviation) predates the recent reasoning wave: the same idea appeared classically under names such as reinforcement learning with leave-one-out (RLOO), where one response's baseline is computed from the others in its set. GRPO ("group relative" policy optimization) makes that structure explicit: the group is the multiset of y's drawn for a fixed x, and advantages are measured relative to peers that faced identical conditioning. That answers the flaw of a single batch mean, which mingles easy prompts (where typical rewards are high) with hard ones (where typical rewards are low) and mis-calibrates what "better than usual" should mean.

Compute trade-off: Prompt-specific baselines need extra rollouts per x. Those generations could instead have been spent covering more diverse prompts. Whether to invest in a thick group for a few prompts or a thin group across many is an empirical allocation question, not a theorem—both sides show up in production systems.

Critic details that matter in implementation

A trained value head is initialized like a reward model—large language-model trunk plus a scalar linear layer on a hidden state—but its target is plain regression (squared error) toward the terminal reward observed after a full rollout, not pairwise preference logits. At each prefix the critic asks: if generation continued from here under the current policy, what final score would we expect? That is only partially observable—"Bob is a cow" might still lead to a brilliant unicorn story—but pathological prefixes (toxic repetition, derailment) are often enough for the critic to anticipate collapse. In full algorithms the target is sometimes a discounted notion of return so that uncertainty early in the sequence is not treated identically to uncertainty near the end.

The reward model scores finished (x, y) pairs only. The critic must predict outcomes for partial decodings under the current policy, so it is trained alongside the policy — a frozen critic would go stale as pθ shifts.

03

Reward Hacking and the KL Penalty

Reward hacking occurs when the model finds unintended ways to maximize the reward. The reward model is a proxy for human preferences — it is imperfect. Human preferences can be noisy and subjective. Common behaviors include producing more verbose responses (humans often favor detail), exploiting grammar or formatting biases (a single error may cause humans to rank a response lower regardless of content), or gaming the reward model in other ways. Preference data can be noisy — different humans may disagree, and the reward model treats all preferences equally.

Training Pipeline

The typical pipeline is: pre-training → supervised fine-tuning (SFT) → RL. We start from an SFT model pSFT (also called πref or πSFT in the literature). Our current policy is pθ. We want to maximize reward but not deviate too far from the SFT model.

Solutions to Reward Hacking

Solutions include training a better reward model (more or higher-quality preference data, larger base model) and intervening in the RL process — e.g., penalizing large deviations from the SFT model. Reward clipping is another technique: clip the reward to a bounded range so that a single extreme reward does not cause an oversized update.

KL Divergence Penalty

We add a penalty for deviating from the SFT model using the KL divergence:

DKL(pθ ‖ pSFT) = Ey~pθ(·|x) [ log pθ(y|x) − log pSFT(y|x) ]

If the two distributions are identical, the KL is zero. As the policy places more probability on responses where the SFT model assigns low probability, the KL grows. We subtract this penalty (scaled by a hyperparameter β) from our objective. A large β keeps the policy close to SFT but may limit reward improvement. A small β allows more deviation but increases reward hacking risk.

maxθ   Ex,y [   AKL(x,y)   ]    where    AKL = (R − baseline) − β · (log pθ(y|x) − log pSFT(y|x))

In writing, the KL term is often folded into the advantage alongside reward and baseline so that a single expression drives both "move toward high reward" and "do not wander into regions the SFT distribution would never touch." The coefficient β is a knob you sweep empirically: too large and the policy barely moves; too small and optimization may chase reward spikes that reflect evaluator quirks rather than durable quality.

What KL cannot promise: Keeping close to the SFT model in KL is a useful prior, not a certificate of safety or factuality. A policy can remain near the reference and still exploit systematic blind spots in the reward model, and conversely you may need real behavioral change from RL that necessarily shifts the distribution. Expect engineering trade-offs, not guarantees.

Intuitively, pre-training and SFT assume that the human-written targets are high-quality references worth imitating. In RL, sampled completions may be ungrammatical, unfaithful, or incoherent yet still receive a high score if the reward model mis-generalizes. The KL anchor biases updates toward regions where the supervised model already placed mass, so extreme "reward-only" moves that look good to a flawed critic are harder to reach.

Forward vs. Reverse KL

The reverse KL (used above) encourages the policy to put probability only where the SFT model already has probability — mode-seeking behavior. The forward KL would encourage the policy to cover all modes of the SFT distribution — mode-covering. For RL, we typically want mode-seeking: find high-reward regions that are still plausible under SFT. Some variants of GRPO drop the KL penalty entirely when the data and setup are configured appropriately.

Forward KL flavor

Penalties built from Ey~pSFT[log pSFT − log pθ] pressure the student to assign mass wherever the teacher does, even across multiple disjoint modes. That resembles "cover the training distribution" more than "only change where I already know how to speak."

Reverse KL (common in RLHF)

Sampling (or scoring) under pθ asks that whatever the policy actually produces stay plausible under pSFT. A limited model is not forced to resurrect every SFT mode—only to avoid wandering into high-reward corners that the reference would assign negligible density.

In practice the exact integral is unavailable; both reward and KL are estimated from a handful of samples, and swapping the direction of the KL or the sampling distribution changes curvature and variance. Published recipes disagree: some pipelines report better results with alternate formulations, and a few modern GRPO-style systems omit explicit KL altogether when other stabilizers and data curation carry the load. Treat the choice as empirical, not settled doctrine.

04

Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)

PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) was developed for control tasks (e.g., Atari games) and later adapted for language model RLHF. It adds a clipping mechanism to prevent the policy from changing too drastically between updates, stabilizing training.

The Policy Ratio

For a batch of prompts, we generate responses with the old policy (before any updates on this batch). We may then perform multiple optimizer steps on the same rollouts. The policy ratio at token position i is:

ri = pθ(yi | x, y1, ..., yi-1) / pθ_old(yi | x, y1, ..., yi-1)

This measures how much more or less likely the token is under the new policy versus the old one. Initially, θ = θold, so ri = 1. After updates, ri can grow or shrink.

Here θold is not the SFT checkpoint from the start of training—it is the policy snapshot taken at the beginning of the current minibatch (or outer iteration) before any PPO epochs reuse the same rollouts. All ratios in that phase compare the moving θ against that frozen batch anchor. The SFT model appears separately inside the KL penalty; the two mechanisms answer different questions. KL asks whether we are leaving the long-run supervised prior; the ratio asks whether we are distorting the on-policy data we already collected for this batch.

KL to pSFT

A global anchor tied to human-curated behavior. It penalizes putting mass on completions the reference would never make, even if those completions look attractive to the reward model.

Clipped ratio vs pθ_old

A local trust region for reusing the same sampled trajectories across multiple inner updates. It stops any single optimization sub-step from rewriting token probabilities far beyond what produced the data on disk.

Small learning rates are not a substitute: A tiny step size slows movement but does not remove the possibility of steadily crawling toward degenerate regions—places that yield huge reward under a flawed critic yet lie outside anything the SFT model would generate. Explicit KL and ratio clipping encode priors about where in output space updates are allowed to accumulate; learning rate alone only scales how fast you walk.

The Clipping Mechanism

PPO clips the ratio to a range [1 − ε, 1 + ε], typically with ε = 0.1 or 0.2. The objective at each token is:

min( ri · Ai, clip(ri, 1−ε, 1+ε) · Ai )

When the advantage Ai is positive (good token), we want to increase its probability. But if the ratio already exceeds 1 + ε, we take the minimum — the clipped term — and no gradient flows. We stop updating that token because it is already likely enough. When Ai is negative (bad token), we want to decrease its probability. If the ratio falls below 1 − ε, we again clip and stop. This creates a trust region: we only update when the policy has not moved too far.

Full objectives aggregate token contributions:   maxθ Ex, y~pθ_old(·|x) [ Σi=1|y| min( riAi, clip(ri, 1−ε, 1+ε) Ai ) ]   (possibly minus KL terms folded into A).

Worked numerics with ε = 0.2 (clip interval [0.8, 1.2])

Take Ai = +2 (the token sits inside a response judged better than the value baseline). If ri = 1.3, the unweighted objective term is 2.6, but clipping pulls the ratio down to 1.2, giving 2.4. The PPO objective uses the minimum (2.4), so the surrogate is flat if you try to push r higher—no gradient encourages further probability inflation on that token. If instead ri = 1.1, both branches read 2.2; the minimum is 2.2 and gradients flow normally: the token is still under the ceiling, so we may increase its likelihood.

For Ai < 0, the min/clip logic reverses which branch binds. Suppose Ai = −3 and ri = 0.6 (already below 0.8). The clipped product (−3)(0.8) = −2.4 is less than the unclipped (−3)(0.6) = −1.8, so the objective selects −2.4 and the gradient through the clipped branch vanishes—the policy has already suppressed that token enough relative to θold. The subtle case is negative advantage with ri > 1 + ε: the bad token has become more likely under the new policy than under the sampler. You still want to keep pushing probability down, because the model is doing the wrong thing with high confidence. On that side of the trust region the surrogate continues to penalize the token until the ratio returns toward unity.

How to read the piecewise surrogate: For positive advantages, clipping acts like a ceiling on how much you trust a single optimization step to up-weight a token. For negative advantages, clipping can freeze further down-weighting once the ratio hits the floor—except when the policy has become overconfident in a bad token (large ratio), where the objective keeps applying corrective pressure. Graphing surrogate value against r for fixed A produces the flat "tabs" outside [1−ε, 1+ε] that give PPO its stability.

Value Function and GAE

In full RLHF, a value function V(x, y<i) predicts the expected final reward given the prompt and the partial response so far. Unlike the reward model (which is frozen), the value function is trained alongside the policy — as the policy changes, the value function must adapt to predict rewards for the responses the policy actually generates. It is typically a language model with a scalar linear head, trained with L2 (squared) loss to match the final reward of samples. The advantage at each position can be computed using Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE), which combines multi-step returns. The value function requires significant extra compute and memory.

The original RLHF / InstructGPT stack cemented the three-model pattern—policy, frozen reward model, and trainable critic—because it empirically worked at scale, not because anything forces that factorization. GRPO and related algorithms can be viewed as deliberately shrinking the same PPO skeleton: keep the ratio clipping that stabilizes reuse of rollouts, replace the neural critic with closed-form group statistics, and sometimes trim or drop the KL term when other guardrails suffice. The research frontier is largely about which pieces of the classic recipe are load-bearing for a given data regime.

05

DPO and GRPO: Alternatives to PPO

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)

DPO avoids the reward model and PPO entirely. Instead of training a reward model and optimizing it with RL, DPO derives a loss directly from preference pairs (see Chapter 9 for the Bradley–Terry connection). Given a preferred response yw vs. a rejected response yl, DPO updates the policy to increase the probability of yw relative to yl. No reward model, no value function, no PPO — just a single supervised-style update. It is simpler and often more stable, but it does not use the full machinery of RL.

Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)

GRPO (DeepSeek) keeps the reward model but simplifies the baseline. Instead of a trained value function, it uses group statistics: for each prompt, generate multiple responses (a "group"), compute the mean and standard deviation of rewards, and use that as the baseline for each sample. GRPO combines this with policy ratio clipping similar to PPO. The result is a single model to update — no separate value network — which reduces memory and compute. GRPO has been used successfully for reasoning-oriented models.

Same skeleton, fewer networks: Think of GRPO as retaining PPO's trust-region intuition—reuse rollouts, bound per-token ratio movement—while replacing the critic's learned baseline with closed-form statistics over the sampled group. Some implementations also relax or remove the KL-to-SFT term when data filtering and clipping already keep trajectories on-manifold; that is an active design space rather than a settled recipe.

RL vs SFT

A critical difference: RL updates are scaled by the probability of producing the response. RL penalizes low-reward samples that the model actually produces and rewards high-reward samples it produces. It does fine-grained tweaking in the space of responses the model is already likely to generate. SFT has no such scaling — it pushes the model toward reference responses (from humans or teachers) that the model may have very low probability of producing. SFT is effectively off-policy; RL is on-policy. RL typically makes smaller parameter changes than SFT.

On-policy RL fine-tuning

Gradients arise from trajectories the model currently draws. High mass on a bad completion is directly penalized; high mass on a good completion is reinforced. Exploration is whatever the policy already prefers, modulated by temperature and sampling noise.

Off-policy-style SFT

Every step nudges logits toward expert tokens even when the model would assign them tiny prior probability—think of teaching a rare idiom or a precise tool-use format. There is no per-example reward multiplier tied to how likely the sample was under θ.

When a reference completion is already easy for the model (for example, factual trivia it knew from pre-training), SFT-like gradient steps and policy-gradient steps can look similar: both mainly reweight mass that already lived near the data. The divergence shows up on long-tail behaviors the model would almost never sample without exploration pressure—SFT drags the optimizer there; vanilla RL may never visit those strings often enough to learn them from reward alone unless initialization or curriculum supplies the path.
