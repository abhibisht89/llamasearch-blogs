What is a Language Model?

Most people today know a language model as something that "predicts the next word." That is correct — and it is a special case of a broader definition.

The General Definition

A language model assigns a probability to a piece of text. It tells you how probable some sequence of words is — whether that sequence is a sentence, a paragraph, or an entire document.

P("students opened their books") > P("students opened their door knobs")

The first sentence is more probable because it's a more natural, commonly expressed idea. A good language model captures this.

Why This Matters (Even Before LLMs)

Long before ChatGPT existed, language models were already extremely useful. Consider machine translation: if you're translating a sentence from French to English and have two candidate translations, you want to know which one is more probable in English. A language model gives you exactly that — a way to rank candidates based on how natural they sound.

The same principle powers autocomplete on your phone, which existed long before modern LLMs. In all these applications, more probable candidates tend to be more useful for whatever the task is.

The Modern View: Predicting the Next Word

The "predict the next word" definition is actually a conditional probability. Given a prefix — a partially written piece of text — we want to know the probability of whatever comes next:

Here, w₁, w₂, ..., wₙ₋₁ is the prefix — the words that have already been written. And wₙ is the next word we're trying to predict. You'll see many different notations for this: P(wₙ | w₁:ₙ₋₁), P(wₙ | w<n), and others. There's no single standard notation — just be aware that they all mean the same thing.

These two definitions — "probability of a text" and "probability of the next word given a prefix" — are equivalent. The chain rule of probability connects them.

Concretely, the joint probability is the chance that the entire ordered sequence occurs as written — not the words in any other order, and not a bag of words. Once estimated from data, it is whatever number your modeling assumptions assign to that exact string. The chain rule factors that full-sequence probability into a product of next-step predictions, which is how both classical and neural language models compute it.

A Note on Ordering

An important subtlety: the order of words in the prefix matters. When we write P(w₃ | w₁, w₂), we mean the probability of the third word given that the first word is w₁ and the second word is w₂ — in that exact order. If you rearrange the prefix, you get a different conditional probability. Most notation conventions omit this detail, but it's always implied.

02

Estimating Probabilities: Count and Divide

So we know what probabilities we want. But how do we actually get them? As with any machine learning model, we start with a training data set — a collection of text documents that can come from the internet, books, scientific papers, or anywhere else.

The simplest approach is literal: just count occurrences of words or phrases in the training data and divide to get proportions.

A Concrete Example

Suppose we have the prefix "my favorite LLM is" and we want to compute P("Gemini" | "my favorite LLM is"). Using count and divide:

P("Gemini" | "my favorite LLM is") = Count("my favorite LLM is Gemini") / Count("my favorite LLM is")

We look through our entire training data set, count how many times the exact phrase "my favorite LLM is Gemini" appears, and divide by how many times the prefix "my favorite LLM is" appears followed by anything.

If this worked perfectly, we'd have a straightforward recipe: for any possible completion, compute two counts and divide. But this approach has serious problems.

The Problems

  • ✗ Sparsity: For an accurate estimate, we need a huge number of occurrences of both the full phrase and the prefix. If the exact phrase "my favorite LLM is Gemini" appears only once (or never) in a trillion-word data set, our estimate is unreliable or undefined.
  • ✗ No semantic sharing: The phrase "my favorite large language model is Gemini" gets zero credit from observing "my favorite LLM is Gemini." The same happens for a wording like "my favorite language model is definitely Gemini" — it can yield no positive count for the exact numerator you need, even though the intent is almost identical to "my favorite LLM is Gemini." Count-and-divide treats every surface form as its own island. As phrasings get more elaborate, the sparsity problem compounds — more specific phrasings are even less likely to occur multiple times.
  • ✗ Vocabulary limits: The training data set limits your vocabulary. Words or constructions that don't appear in the data can't be modeled at all.
  • ✗ Subsequence information is wasted: The prefix "my favorite" is probably very common. But because we're looking for an exact match of the full prefix, all that frequency information about shorter subsequences is thrown away.
  • ✗ Case sensitivity: "My" and "my" are treated as different tokens. An exact match for "My favorite LLM is" won't count occurrences of "my favorite LLM is."
  • ✗ Storage: You would have to store a count for every possible sequence observed in the training data. With trillions of tokens, this is simply not a scalable solution. You'd also want entries for sequences you haven't observed, making any fixed-size data structure impractical.

The "Type of Answer" Problem

There's another subtle issue. Suppose our data only contains sentences like "my favorite LLM is Gemini" or "my favorite LLM is ChatGPT." The model can only produce completions it has actually observed. It could never produce "my favorite LLM is the best one available" — a semantically valid answer that just happens to be phrased differently. The model is fundamentally limited to the types of answers present in its training data.

Data set quality: In modern language modeling, we generally envision having so much data that it is treated almost like “infinite” data for modeling purposes — not because the web is literally infinite, but because the engineering goal is to keep adding text until coverage spans many domains, perspectives, and topics, which dilutes narrow biases. That aspiration never fully removes imbalance (a corpus can still over-represent one product or dialect), but it is the usual mindset when scaling language models. With a tiny corpus, imbalance bites harder: a few documents praising one system can dominate counts.

03

N-gram Models and the Markov Assumption

N-gram models address some of the problems above — not in an ideal way, but in a way that practically allows us to get some estimate for conditional probabilities. The high-level idea is simple:

Approximate the probability by dropping context from the prefix.

This is an application of the Markov assumption — the idea that we only need to look at the most recent few words to predict the next one.

Bigram Model (n = 2)

In a bigram model, we keep only the last word of the prefix. Our full conditional probability gets approximated as:

P("Gemini" | "my favorite LLM is") ≈ P("Gemini" | "is") = Count("is Gemini") / Count("is")

Now instead of counting occurrences of the long phrase "my favorite LLM is Gemini," we just need to count two-word sequences: how often does "is Gemini" appear, divided by how often "is" appears. This is much more tractable.

But of course, we've lost all context beyond the word "is." Many different words could follow "is" — the probability distribution is very spread out, because we have no idea what the full sentence is about.

Trigram Model (n = 3)

We can preserve more context. A trigram model keeps the last two words:

P("Gemini" | "my favorite LLM is") ≈ P("Gemini" | "LLM is") = Count("LLM is Gemini") / Count("LLM is")

Now we have more constraints on the next word — we know "LLM" was part of the prefix, which narrows things down considerably. But we still lose the fact that this could have been "the worst LLM is" vs. "my favorite LLM is."

What you lose

Every time you chop off part of the prefix, you discard constraints on what may come next. The conditional distribution becomes less specific: more completions look plausible because the model no longer “knows” the full story.

What you gain

Shorter contexts repeat in data; counts are larger and more reliable, and the storage grid grows more slowly in the length of the retained prefix than in the full sentence history. You are trading fidelity for tractability — the central bargain of the Markov assumption.

The General Pattern

In the name "n-gram model," n refers to the length of the prefix plus the next word. A bigram counts two-word phrases (one prefix word + one target word). A trigram counts three-word phrases (two prefix words + one target word). In general, an n-gram model fixes the context to n − 1 words.

Important clarification: Every n-gram model (bigram, trigram, 5-gram, etc.) is a Markov simplification of the same full chain-rule expansion. We're not simplifying a trigram into a bigram. Rather, all of them are different approximations of the full product of conditional probabilities — each fixing the context to n − 1 words. The higher n, the better the approximation, but also the more data you need.

Why Do the Nearest Words Matter Most?

The most recent words in the prefix are always the most constraining on what the next word can be. This is true not just for n-gram models, but for all language models. Even humans rely heavily on short-term memory when reading: psycholinguistically, people often hold only on the order of the previous seven words or so in verbatim form, while earlier material is represented more abstractly — yet that is still enough to predict the next word with reasonable accuracy in ordinary text.

That said, handling long-range dependencies is where modern models like ChatGPT truly shine. You can provide 200,000 words of context and ask it to summarize — even though summarization is just next-word prediction, the model can leverage information from words that appeared thousands of positions ago. This is a major capability gap between n-gram models and neural language models.

An important nuance: the next-word prediction task itself inherently depends most heavily on the most recent words. A word that occurred 200,000 positions ago has very little direct bearing on what comes next at any specific point. Getting models to pay attention to and use that distant information is still an ongoing challenge in LLM development.

The Unigram Model (n = 1)

The simplest model of all is the unigram model. Here, we use no prefix at all — we just multiply the individual word-level probabilities:

Each word's probability is simply its frequency in the training data, completely ignoring context. So P("Gemini" | "my favorite LLM is") is approximated as just P("Gemini") — how often does the word "Gemini" appear anywhere in the data set, regardless of what comes before it. This sounds terrible, and it is — but unigram models are still useful as baselines and for certain applications. They're trivially easy to implement: just store one count per word type.

Back-off: A natural question is: can we combine multiple n-gram models? Yes. A technique called back-off lets you start with a high-order model (say, 7-gram), and if it estimates a zero probability because the sequence was never observed, you "back off" to a 6-gram, then a 5-gram, and so on, until you find a model that gives a nonzero estimate.

Types, Tokens, and Vocabulary

Before we can build an n-gram model, we need some terminology. These terms are used constantly in NLP and it's important to get them right — many people use them interchangeably, but they mean different things.

Vocabulary

A vocabulary consists of all the unique words in your training data set. We denote its size as V. Each unique element of the vocabulary is called a word type.

Types vs. Tokens

Type

A unique entry in the vocabulary. The word "Sam" is one type, regardless of how many times it appears.

The vocabulary size (V) = the number of unique types.

Token

An instance of a type in the data set. If "Sam" appears twice, that's two tokens but one type.

The data set size is measured in tokens, not types.

Implementation Intuition (Before We Open the Corpus)

If you were engineering a bigram model and did not know the training text in advance, one natural data structure is to maintain a count for every two-word phrase that can be formed from your vocabulary — in principle, every ordered pair of types — and fill in the counts as you stream the corpus. That is why the storage question scales with V² for bigrams, V³ for trigrams, and Vn in general: you are prepared for any combination the vocabulary allows, whether or not it ever appeared. The toy corpus below is just a small instance of the same counting logic.

A Concrete Example

Consider this tiny corpus of three sentences:

<s> I am Sam </s>
<s> Sam I am </s>
<s> I do not like green eggs and ham </s>

Notice the special tokens: <s> (start of sequence) and </s> (end of sequence). These appear in every language modeling data set. The model needs to know when a sequence starts and, crucially, when it's done generating. These special tokens are part of the vocabulary.

Vocabulary for This Corpus

<s> </s> I am Sam do not like green eggs and ham

That's V = 12 word types. But the total number of tokens is around 20 — every individual word instance in the data set. Notice that "Sam" appears twice (two tokens, one type), "I" appears three times (three tokens, one type).

Data set size: When someone says their training data set has "2 trillion tokens," they're referring to the total number of token instances, not the number of unique types. Your vocabulary might be 100,000 types, but the data set can have trillions of tokens.

What About Punctuation?

Whether punctuation counts as a separate type depends on your tokenization scheme. You could treat commas and periods as separate types, or use individual characters instead of words. Subword methods (BPE, WordPiece) and character-level tokenization are common alternatives. The choice of tokenization directly determines your vocabulary.

One interesting consideration: punctuation is more grammatical than semantic. You wouldn't expect a period after every word, but you know periods could follow most words. With enough training data, the model's estimates improve — it learns when commas, periods, and other punctuation are likely based on the surrounding context.

Computing N-gram Probabilities

The following computes bigram probabilities from a tiny corpus, making the count-and-divide method concrete.

Worked Examples

P(I | <s>) — What's the probability of "I" following the start-of-sequence token?

The start-of-sequence token <s> appears 3 times (once per sentence). Of those, "I" follows it 2 times (sentences 1 and 3). So P(I | <s>) = 2/3 ≈ 0.667.

P(Sam | <s>) — "Sam" follows <s> in only the second sentence.

P(Sam | <s>) = 1/3 ≈ 0.333.

P(ham | <s>) — "ham" never follows <s> in our data.

P(ham | <s>) = 0/3 = 0. This is a serious problem — it means any test sentence that starts with "ham" gets assigned a probability of zero under our model, regardless of how reasonable the rest of the sentence is.

P(Sam | am) — The word "am" appears 2 times. "Sam" follows "am" 1 time.

P(Sam | am) = 1/2 = 0.5.

A Zero-Probability Test Sentence

To see the danger of zeros, consider this test sentence: <s> ham and eggs </s>. Under our bigram model, we need P(ham | <s>). But "ham" never follows <s> in our training data, so P(ham | <s>) = 0. The entire sentence gets probability zero — regardless of how reasonable "ham and eggs" is. If this were an entire Dr. Seuss book starting with "ham," the whole book's probability would be zero under our model. This is arguably the single biggest practical problem with n-gram models.

Start-of-sequence is never predicted: P(<s> | </s>) is not a meaningful quantity. The start-of-sequence token is always assumed to be part of the prefix, not something the model generates. The model only predicts actual words and the end-of-sequence token.

06

Storage, Sparsity, and the Limits of N-grams

How Many Counts Must We Store?

For a bigram model, we need a V × V table — all possible two-word pairs. For a trigram model, we need V² × V entries (all possible two-word prefixes times all possible target words). In general, an n-gram model requires Vn entries.

Storage Growth: Vn

Adjust the vocabulary size and n-gram order to see how storage requirements explode. Real-world vocabularies can be 100,000+ types.

Does Increasing n Always Help?

It might seem like a larger n should always produce a better model — more context means more information about what the next word should be. But this reasoning breaks down in practice:

  • ✗ Overfitting: As n increases, most entries in the count table are zero. The model memorizes specific training sequences rather than learning generalizable patterns.
  • ✗ Zero probabilities: If the probability of "ham" after <s> is zero, then any test document starting with "ham" gets assigned probability zero — regardless of how sensible it is. The model becomes incapable of generalizing beyond its exact training documents.
  • ✗ Exponential storage: Each increment of n multiplies storage by V, making large n impractical.

The optimal n depends on your data set size. With very little data, even a trigram model will overfit. With massive data (Google-scale), production systems have used up to roughly 10-gram models. But for most practical purposes, trigrams (n = 3) with smoothing techniques are a reasonable sweet spot.

Smoothing: A Brief Mention

There are advanced techniques to handle the zero-probability problem, collectively known as smoothing. The idea is to redistribute some probability mass from observed n-grams to unobserved ones, so that nothing gets an exact zero. These techniques are beyond our scope here, but they're essential in any practical n-gram deployment.

Historical note: When n-gram models were used in production — most notably at Google for machine translation — they probably reached around 10-gram level. That's an enormous amount of storage and data, but at Google scale it was feasible. For most researchers and applications, trigram models were the practical limit.

Evaluating Models: Perplexity

We've built a language model. But how do we know if it's any good? The key principle:

A good language model assigns high probability to test data it has never seen before.

We don't care about high probability on the training data — the model has already seen that. What matters is whether it has learned generalizable patterns of the language that transfer to new text.

Generous coverage, not memorization: There are enormously many strings you can assemble from a fixed vocabulary; any one of them might be produced by a human somewhere. A brittle model that assigns probability zero to reasonable unseen strings — only because the exact n-grams never appeared in training — is failing this broader goal. That is why zeros are not merely a numerical annoyance: they mean the model has declared whole regions of possible language “impossible.” Smoothing, backoff, and neural models all exist, in part, to mitigate that rigidity.

The Problem with Raw Probabilities

As sequences get longer, the product of conditional probabilities gets smaller and smaller. If your document has thousands of words, multiplying thousands of probabilities (each less than 1) together will cause numerical underflow — the number becomes so tiny that computers can't represent it.

Step 1: Log Probabilities

The solution: work with log probabilities instead. The nice property of logarithms is that they turn products into sums:

Instead of a probability like 0.000000001, we might get a log probability of −14. Much easier to work with.

Concretely, the probability of a test sequence is still the product of conditionals along the chain. For example, under a bigram model, a string such as <s> ham and eggs </s> would be scored as:

P(<s>, ham, and, eggs, </s>) = P(ham | <s>) · P(and | ham) · P(eggs | and) · P(</s> | eggs)

(We omit the leading factor P(<s>) because the start token is fixed as given.) Each factor is a fraction between 0 and 1; the product shrinks fast. Taking logs turns this product into a sum of log conditionals — the standard trick for long documents and for training objectives.

Step 2: Normalize by Length

Different test documents have different lengths. A 10-word sentence and a 10,000-word book will have very different log probabilities simply because of length. To compare fairly, we compute the average per-token log probability:

When the discussion is explicitly about held-out evaluation, it is common to denote the test string as x₁, x₂, …, xT (using x rather than w) to stress that these tokens are not training examples. The math is unchanged: we still want the joint probability P(x₁, …, xT) to be large, equivalently the average per-token log probability to be large after normalizing by T.

Because we've exponentiated the negative log likelihood, perplexity goes up when the model does badly. A lower perplexity = better model. You'll commonly see it abbreviated as PPL.

The Intuition: "How Many Choices?"

Perplexity has a beautiful intuition: it measures how many equally likely next words the model is choosing from, on average, at each position.

High Perplexity

The model thinks many words are equally likely. It's confused — like choosing from 5,000 options. This happens with very little context (e.g., bigram model where the prefix is just "is").

Low Perplexity

The model is confident about which word comes next. It's choosing from a small set of likely options — maybe just "Gemini," "ChatGPT," or "Claude."

Going back to our "favorite LLM" example: a bigram model conditioning only on "is" would have high perplexity — there could be thousands of words after "is." A trigram conditioning on "LLM is" would have lower perplexity. And a model with full context "my favorite LLM is" might have very low perplexity — now it's essentially choosing between a handful of LLM names.

In general: unigram models have the highest perplexity, bigram models lower, trigram lower still. But increasing n doesn't infinitely decrease perplexity — at some point, sparsity and overfitting cause it to climb back up.

Average log probability (or its negative) ranks models the same way as perplexity — the two are monotonically related. Perplexity applies an exponential to put the score on a scale that reads as an effective branching factor: how many roughly equiprobable next words the model is choosing among at each step. That interpretation is harder to see from a raw log score alone. Perplexity is a fixed transformation of the sequence probability; it adds no new information.

Train vs. Test Perplexity

There's a critical distinction between training perplexity and test perplexity. As you train longer:

  • ● Training perplexity keeps going down — the model fits the training data better and better.
  • ● Test perplexity initially goes down (the model is learning general patterns), but eventually starts going up — the model has started overfitting to the training data.
  • ┊ The point where test perplexity starts rising is where overfitting begins. You want to stop training near here.

(2) The formula −(1/T) Σ log P(wᵢ | ...) is the loss function that all modern LLMs minimize during training. (3) Always evaluate on held-out test data, not training data.

What comes next: We've built language models by counting and dividing, and we've seen all the limitations — sparsity, no semantic sharing, massive storage. The natural next step is to explore neural language models that take advantage of similarity and meaning between different prefixes. Instead of treating "movie" and "film" as completely unrelated, neural models represent words as dense vectors where similar words are close together — and that changes everything.
