01 — INFERENCE-TIME COMPUTE

Why Spend More Compute at Test Time?

Human beings give better answers when they think longer. Exam problems, Sudoku grids, and multi-step puzzles all reward deliberate computation over instant guesses. Large language models follow the same pattern: allocating more computational resources during inference — generating more tokens before committing to an answer — improves accuracy on complex reasoning tasks.

test-time compute = resources the model uses during inference before producing a final answer

This is the foundation of inference-time compute scaling. Training prepares the model (studying for an exam); inference is performance (writing the exam). As test-time compute increases, accuracy on hard benchmarks generally rises — though the relationship has nuances explored throughout this chapter.

Regular LLM

Question ──► LLM ──► Answer

Inference is nearly instantaneous — a few tokens, opaque reasoning.

Reasoning LLM

Question ──► LLM ──► Step₁ → Step₂ → … → Stepₙ ──► Answer

Visible intermediate steps; roughly 7–8× more tokens for the same problem.

The Roger Tennis Ball Example

Consider a GSM8K-style word problem:

Question: Roger has 5 tennis balls. He buys 2 more cans of tennis balls, 3 balls per can. How many tennis balls does he have now?

Correct answer: 11

A regular LLM may answer correctly with just three tokens — Answer is 11 — getting lucky through pattern matching. A reasoning-based model emits an explicit chain:

Step 1 (4 tokens): Roger started with 5 balls.

Step 2 (11 tokens): Two cans of 3 tennis balls each is 6 tennis balls.

Step 3 (5 tokens): 5 + 6 = 11.

Final: The answer is 11.

Three tokens versus roughly twenty-three — about 7–8× more compute. On easy problems both approaches may succeed; on harder multi-step problems, the extra tokens buy reliability.

Empirical scaling law: As test-time compute increases, model accuracy on complex reasoning benchmarks typically increases. This is why the field treats visible reasoning chains as a compute allocation strategy, not merely a formatting preference.

02 — FEW-SHOT CHAIN OF THOUGHT

Chain-of-Thought Prompting (Wei et al., 2022)

The breakthrough paper from Google Brain (released 2022, now cited over 12,000 times) showed that reasoning abilities in sufficiently large language models can be unlocked through prompting alone — no architecture changes, no fine-tuning, no weight updates.

December 2022 — GPT-3.5 Era

Models answered fluently but were not built to think. The research community began taking reasoning-based LLMs seriously.

Wei et al. — Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning

Two core ideas: (1) arithmetic and complex reasoning benefit from generating natural-language rationales leading to the final answer; (2) this can be achieved by simply prompting with input–output examples that include step-by-step reasoning.

Standard Prompting vs Few-Shot CoT

In standard few-shot prompting, each demonstration pairs a question with a direct answer: The answer is 11. In few-shot chain-of-thought prompting, the output instead lists every intermediate step required to reach the answer.

Standard Output Example
Q: Roger has 5 tennis balls. He buys 2 cans of 3 balls each. How many now? A: The answer is 11.
CoT Output Example
Q: Roger has 5 tennis balls. He buys 2 cans of 3 balls each. How many now? A: Step 1: Roger started with 5 balls. Step 2: Two cans of 3 tennis balls each is 6 balls. Step 3: 5 + 6 = 11. Step 4: The answer is 11.

Each thought links to the next — a chain of linked intermediate reasoning steps. Once the model sees this format in the prompt examples, it generalizes: new questions (like the cafeteria apples problem below) receive similar step-by-step treatment even though they were not in the original demonstrations.

Generalization example — cafeteria apples:

The cafeteria had 23 apples. They used 20 to make lunch and bought 6 more. How many apples do they have?

The cafeteria had 23 apples originally. They used 20 to make lunch, so 23 − 20 = 3. They bought 6 more apples, so 3 + 6 = 9. The answer is 9.

Key property: Model weights and architecture stay frozen. The LLM's "brain" does not change — only the prompt does. Through a series of input–output examples with visible reasoning, the model learns to answer other complex tasks in a similar format.

04 — WHY COT WORKS

Decomposition, Intermediate Steps, and Error Isolation

Wei et al. define chain-of-thought as a series of intermediate reasoning steps that significantly improve the ability of LLMs to perform complex reasoning. Three mechanisms explain why this format helps.

1. Decomposition

Complex problems break into smaller sub-problems. Roger's purchase decomposes into "count existing balls," "compute new balls," and "add." Each sub-problem is simpler than the whole — matching how humans solve word problems.

Natural-language rationales are the series of steps from question to answer — not a single leap.

2. Intermediate Steps

Each step is an explicit token the model must generate before proceeding. This increases test-time compute and forces the model to allocate capacity to reasoning rather than jumping to a plausible-sounding number.

PaLM's error analysis shows larger models fix semantic understanding and one-step missing errors as scale increases.

3. Error Isolation

When steps are visible, errors become localizable. Consider Tracy's wire problem: a 4-foot wire cut into 6-inch pieces. The correct answer is 8 pieces (48 inches ÷ 6). A smaller model incorrectly multiplies 4 × 6 = 24 — conflating feet with inches. With CoT, the error appears at a specific step ("failed to convert feet to inches"), making debugging possible. Without steps, you only see a wrong final number.

Correct chain: 4 ft × 12 in/ft = 48 in → 48 ÷ 6 = 8 pieces Small model error: 4 × 6 = 24 (skipped unit conversion)

From the paper: "We explore how generating a chain of thought — a series of intermediate reasoning steps — significantly improves the ability of LLMs to perform complex reasoning." Reasoning abilities emerge naturally in sufficiently large language models via this simple prompting method.

05 — GSM8K & SCALE

Arithmetic Reasoning on GSM8K

The primary benchmark in the Wei et al. study is GSM8K — a dataset of grade-school arithmetic word problems requiring multi-step reasoning. Examples include Natalia's clip problem:

Natalia sold clips to 48 of her friends in April, then sold half as many clips in May. How many clips did Natalia sell altogether in April and May?

48 + (48 ÷ 2) = 48 + 24 = 72

Experiments used Google's PaLM family — a large language model released by Google in multiple sizes up to 540 billion parameters. Accuracy is measured by counting how many GSM8K questions the model answers correctly.

Three Key Observations from the Paper

  1. CoT beats standard prompting — a large gap opens between standard and chain-of-thought prompting on GSM8K.
  2. CoT accuracy scales with model size — bigger models benefit more from CoT; small models show little to no improvement.
  3. Large PaLM ≈ supervised fine-tuning — at 540B parameters, CoT prompting achieves accuracy comparable to models that underwent expensive supervised fine-tuning on GSM8K — without any weight updates.

06 — EMERGENT ABILITIES

CoT Is an Emergent Ability of Scale

The most significant finding in Wei et al. is that chain-of-thought reasoning is an emergent ability of increasing model scale — mentioned repeatedly throughout the paper and confirmed in hands-on replication experiments.

As you increase model parameters and nudge the model toward chain-of-thought reasoning, larger models understand the nudge and produce aligned step-by-step answers. Smaller models simply do not capture the instruction — analogous to telling a person with limited capacity to "think step by step": the nudge fails regardless of phrasing.

Small Model (<8B params)

CoT prompting yields ~0–5% accuracy on GSM8K even with carefully crafted examples. The model may emit step-like text but does not perform reliable arithmetic. Hands-on tests with Flan-T5 (small/base/large), Llama, and Zire-7B confirm this.

Accuracy stays flat until roughly 20–100B+ parameters.

Large Model (540B PaLM)

CoT accuracy climbs steeply with scale. At 540B, performance rivals supervised fine-tuning. The model fixes semantic understanding errors (unit confusion, misread quantities) that smaller models make consistently.

Scaling from 62B → 540B progressively eliminates error categories.

Practical implication: Chain-of-thought is incredibly effective compared to standard prompting — but not for very small models. It works only where reasoning is an emergent capability: large models that understand the prompting nudge.

07 — REASONING TASK TYPES

Arithmetic, Common Sense, and Symbolic Reasoning

Wei et al. evaluate CoT across three reasoning categories. CoT consistently outperforms standard prompting on all three — though the margin varies.

Arithmetic Reasoning

GSM8K is the flagship benchmark. CoT works very effectively here — the largest gains and the clearest scaling curves. Word problems decompose naturally into sequential calculations.

Common Sense Reasoning

Example from CSQA-style benchmarks:

Q: Sammy wanted to go to where the people are. Where might he go?
Options: (A) racetrack (B) populated areas (C) desert (D) roadblock

CoT rationale in prompt The answer must be a place with a lot of people. Racetracks, deserts, apartments, and roadblocks don't have a lot of people, but populated areas do. So the answer is B.

CoT always outperforms standard prompting on commonsense tasks. The gap is modest on CSQA but huge on sports-related questions, where CoT even exceeds human-level performance on some benchmarks.

Symbolic Reasoning

Symbolic tasks test logical state tracking — for example:

Q: A coin is heads up. Maybel flips the coin. Shalonda does not flip the coin. Is the coin still heads up?

The coin was flipped by Maybel, so the coin was flipped 1 time (odd). The coin started heads up, so after an odd number of flips it will be tails up. So the answer is No.

LLMs struggle with symbolic reasoning even under standard prompting — they make errors on flip-parity problems. CoT prompting significantly outperforms standard prompting here, though absolute accuracy remains lower than on arithmetic.

Another commonsense probe: Would a steel ball sink in water? CoT helps the model articulate density-based reasoning rather than guessing.

Main result across task types: Chain-of-thought prompting works well on arithmetic, commonsense, and symbolic reasoning — but always with the scale caveat. Small models do not reliably benefit.

08 — ZERO-SHOT CHAIN OF THOUGHT

"Let's Think Step by Step" (Kojima et al., 2022)

The second inference-time technique is even simpler than few-shot CoT. Zero-shot chain-of-thought requires no input–output examples in the prompt — only a trigger phrase appended to the question.

Plain Zero-Shot

Zero examples in the prompt. The model receives only the question and must answer directly.

Q: [ arithmetic word problem ] A: [ often wrong — e.g. answers 2 instead of 4 ]

Zero-Shot CoT

Still zero examples — but append one sentence before generation:

Q: [ arithmetic word problem ] Let's think step by step. A: [ model generates reasoning chain ] The answer is 4.

The phrase works like telling a student: "Go study in your room — and make sure to think step by step before you write the answer." The model learns to reason on its own without demonstration examples.

Two-Step Prompting (Answer Extraction)

Kojima et al. prompt the LLM twice for cleaner evaluation:

  1. Step 1: Append Let's think step by step → model generates a reasoning chain.
  2. Step 2: Feed the chain back with Therefore, the answer is → model extracts a concise final answer.
Question + "Let's think step by step" ──► LLM ──► reasoning chain │ ▼ chain + "Therefore, the answer is" ──► LLM ──► final answer (e.g. 375)

On GSM8K with PaLM, zero-shot CoT significantly outperforms plain zero-shot — mirroring the scaling pattern from few-shot CoT. Larger models (540B) exhibit the strongest zero-shot CoT gains.

Replication finding on small models: On sub-7B models tested hands-on, zero-shot CoT (~20% accuracy) dramatically outperforms few-shot CoT (~0%). Zero-shot triggers work where elaborate few-shot demonstrations fail — because small models cannot absorb complex example formats.

09 — LIMITATIONS

Faithfulness, Correctness, and Prompt Sensitivity

Chain-of-thought is the first rung on the inference-time ladder, not a silver bullet. Three limitation classes matter in production.

Faithfulness

Visible reasoning steps may not faithfully reflect the model's internal computation. The model can produce plausible-sounding chains that rationalize a predetermined answer — steps that look correct but were generated post hoc.

Auditable traces build trust, but the trace is not guaranteed to be the actual reasoning path.

Correctness

Intermediate steps can be wrong even when formatted correctly. Hands-on experiments show models emitting full step sequences yet arriving at incorrect final answers (e.g., answering 300 when the correct answer is 18). CoT improves accuracy but does not eliminate arithmetic errors.

More steps ≠ correct steps. Each step is still a sampled token.

Prompt Sensitivity

CoT performance depends heavily on prompt design:

  • Example quality — poorly chosen few-shot demonstrations degrade performance.
  • Example count — adding 5 or 10 few-shot examples does not rescue small models; accuracy stays near 0%.
  • Trigger phrasing — zero-shot CoT is sensitive to the exact wording of step-inducing phrases.
  • Model scale — the same prompt that works on PaLM-540B fails on Flan-T5-small or Zire-7B.

Scale gate: Below ~8B parameters, CoT accuracy stays between 0–5%. Gains appear above ~20–100B and peak at frontier scale. Do not expect CoT miracles on models you can run on a laptop — the emergent ability simply is not there yet.

10 — SELF-CONSISTENCY

A Simple Improvement: Self-Consistency Decoding

Even with CoT, a single greedy decoding path can be wrong. Self-consistency (Wang et al., 2022) is the simplest upgrade: sample the model multiple times with stochastic decoding, extract each final answer, and take a majority vote.

CoT prompt ──► Sample path 1 → answer A ──► Sample path 2 → answer B ──► Sample path 3 → answer A ──► Sample path N → answer A │ ▼ Majority vote → Answer A
self_consistency = majority_vote({ answer(CoT_sample_i) for i in 1..N })

No separate verifier model is required — the vote count itself acts as a lightweight verifier. Wang et al. report substantial gains on arithmetic and commonsense benchmarks when combining CoT with self-consistency over greedy decoding, often matching models many times larger.

Single CoT Path

One chain, one answer. Fast and cheap. If the chain contains an early error, the final answer is wrong with no recovery mechanism.

Cost: 1× inference compute.

Self-Consistency (N samples)

N diverse chains via temperature > 0. Wrong paths often disagree; correct paths converge. Majority vote filters outliers.

Cost: N× inference compute — trades compute for reliability.

Critical hyperparameters: N (number of samples) and temperature (diversity). With temperature ≈ 0, all paths are identical and voting is meaningless. Self-consistency only helps when paths genuinely diverge.

Chapter 03 explores self-consistency, verifiers, and search-based methods in full depth — building directly on the CoT foundation established here.

Self-Consistency in Action

Three independent CoT samples for a train-speed problem. Two paths agree; one diverges.

SampleReasoning summaryFinal answer
Path 160 km/h × 3 h = 180 km180
Path 2Speed 60, time 3 → distance = 60×3 = 180180
Path 360 + 3 = 63 (skipped multiplication)63

Majority vote → 180. A single greedy path might have landed on 63. Self-consistency converts sampling diversity into a robustness mechanism — at the cost of 3× inference compute.

11 — HANDS-ON REPLICATION

What Happens on Small Models?

The lecture includes practical notebook experiments that replicate Wei et al. on models small enough to run locally. The results are sobering — and consistent with the paper's scaling analysis.

Observed Behavior on Sub-7B Models

  • Few-shot CoT with 5–10 examples — accuracy near 0%; outputs are random or ignore the demonstration format.
  • Zero-shot CoT — ~20% accuracy on Zire-7B; the trigger phrase alone outperforms elaborate few-shot prompts.
  • Models do emit step-like text when given CoT examples — they understand the format but lack the capacity for reliable arithmetic.
  • Scaling up within small range (1.1B → 7B) helps marginally but does not unlock emergent reasoning.

These findings align with the PaLM scaling curves: accuracy stays flat near 0–5% until roughly 8B parameters, then begins climbing above 20–30B, reaching competitive levels only at frontier scale (100B+). The practical lesson: CoT is a technique for large models. On small models, zero-shot triggers are simpler and often more effective than few-shot demonstrations.

Building from scratch: Implementing a minimal LLM (e.g., Llama 3.2 from scratch) and applying zero-shot CoT on top is a valuable exercise — it separates model capacity from prompting technique and builds intuition for why scale matters.

12 — KEY PAPERS & TAKEAWAYS

Three Papers That Started Inference-Time Reasoning

PaperAuthors / YearCore contribution
Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language ModelsWei et al., 2022 (Google Brain)Few-shot demonstrations with step-by-step rationales unlock reasoning in large models; GSM8K benchmark; emergent scaling
Large Language Models are Zero-Shot ReasonersKojima et al., 2022"Let's think step by step" triggers CoT without examples; two-step answer extraction

What Changed

  • Reasoning became a prompting problem, not just a training problem
  • Test-time compute became a tunable accuracy knob
  • Large models revealed emergent reasoning abilities absent in small ones
  • Visible chains enabled auditability and error isolation

What Didn't Change

  • Model weights remain frozen — inference-time only
  • Small models still fail at multi-step reasoning
  • CoT steps can be unfaithful or incorrect
  • Single-path CoT has no built-in error correction

Central tension: Chain-of-thought trades speed for accuracy and transparency. Three tokens versus twenty-three. Instant answer versus auditable reasoning. The entire reasoning-model revolution begins with this trade-off — and the next chapters show how verifiers, search, and training-time methods push accuracy further.
