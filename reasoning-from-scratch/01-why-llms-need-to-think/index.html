CHAPTER 01

Why LLMs Need to Think

Standard language models answer fast — but complex problems demand visible, multi-step reasoning. This chapter defines what reasoning means for LLMs, why pattern matching falls short, and the four paradigms that teach models to think.

01 — THE PROBLEM

What Are Reasoning Models?

Human beings carry an extraordinary capacity to think deeply about a subject before acting. Consider ordering a birthday cake for your mother: a cascade of thoughts runs through your mind in a fraction of a second — her favorite flavor, whether to try something new, her reaction last time, whether her tastes have changed, recent cravings she has mentioned, dietary restrictions, the bakery's reputation, delivery timing. These interconnected thoughts, happening almost instantly, constitute what we call thinking or reasoning.

That internal monologue is exactly what reasoning models externalize. Instead of a single opaque answer, the model emits a trace you can read, verify, and challenge — the same way you'd ask a colleague to "show your work" on a spreadsheet or proof.

Reasoning models bring this same quality to large language models (LLMs). A regular LLM pipeline is simple: you ask a question, it passes through the model, and you receive an answer. A reasoning LLM adds something critical in between — a visible sequence of thoughts that leads to the answer. The goal of this series is to take an LLM from the first diagram to the second: from instant answers to transparent, step-by-step reasoning.

Regular LLM

Question ──► LLM ──► Answer

Fast, fluent, opaque. You see the output but not the path.

Reasoning LLM

Question ──► LLM ──► Thought₁ → Thought₂ → … → Thoughtₙ ──► Answer

Slower, more compute, auditable. You see how the model reached its conclusion.

Why it matters in production: In healthcare, finance, or any high-stakes application, clients trust a system that shows step-by-step reasoning far more than one that delivers a black-box answer. Reasoning models make LLMs trustworthy, not just fluent.

The field moved quickly. OpenAI released o1 in September 2024 — the first widely deployed reasoning model that visibly "thought" before answering (e.g., spending seconds reasoning about movie recommendations). DeepSeek's R1 (January 2025) stunned the world again: comparable accuracy to o1, fully open-source architecture, and reasoning learned through pure reinforcement learning — including spontaneous "aha moments" where the model re-evaluates its own steps mid-problem.

02 — COGNITIVE FOUNDATIONS

System 1 vs System 2 Thinking

Daniel Kahneman's framework maps cleanly onto LLM behavior. Human reasoning comes in two modes:

System 1 — Fast

~95% of cognition

Driven by intuition, past experience, and instinct. Answers arrive immediately.

Example: "What is the capital of India?" → New Delhi, instantly.

System 2 — Slow

Deliberate

Logical, effortful, sometimes indecisive. Requires time to weigh many factors.

Example: "Which movie should I watch tonight?" → OTT platforms, reviews, Rotten Tomatoes, IMDB, mood, time available…

ChatGPT 3.5 (November 2022) was exceptionally capable at System 1 tasks — fast factual recall, fluent prose, immediate answers. But for complex tasks requiring reasoning — puzzles, planning, multi-step math — it showed no sign of genuine System 2 thinking. You received an answer with no visible chain of thought, leaving you unsure whether the model truly understood the problem.

System 2 in Action: Choosing a Movie

When o1 recommends a movie, it visibly spends seconds reasoning — checking OTT availability, genre preferences, recent releases, review scores on Rotten Tomatoes and IMDB, runtime constraints, and mood. That deliberation is System 2 behavior encoded in token generation: the model allocates inference compute to intermediate considerations before committing to an answer.

Contrast this with ChatGPT 3.5's instant movie suggestion: fluent, plausible, but opaque. You cannot audit whether it weighed the same factors you would.

Central question for AI: Can artificial intelligence reason? Many assume LLMs were built to think like humans. They were not. LLMs are built to answer like humans — and that distinction drives everything that follows.

03 — THE MECHANISM

Pattern Matching vs Genuine Reasoning

At their core, LLMs are next-token predictors. Given an input, the model predicts the next word with maximum probability, then repeats autoregressively until a full response is generated. ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, DeepSeek — all share this foundation.

P(tokent | token1, …, tokent−1) → argmax → output

This architecture excels at statistical pattern matching: reproducing fluent, human-like text that resembles answers seen during training. For System 1 queries, pattern matching is often sufficient — the model has seen similar question-answer pairs millions of times.

Multi-step reasoning breaks this assumption. Consider Sudoku: you cannot fill cells by immediate pattern recall. You must think from multiple angles, backtrack, and verify constraints. Crossword puzzles share the same structure — each clue constrains others, requiring iterative deduction rather than one-shot recall.

Mathematical proofs push further still. "Are there infinitely many prime numbers?" is not a System 1 question — it demands a structured argument (Euclid's proof, for instance) built step by step. Exam problems mirror this: students request more time because more thinking time yields better answers. LLMs face the identical constraint: without allocated compute for intermediate steps, they collapse complex problems into a single fluent-looking guess.

Three lifecycle stages: Pre-training teaches general language from massive text corpora. Fine-tuning adapts the model to specific tasks or formats. Inference is when the trained model actually generates answers. Module 1 (CoT, verifiers) operates entirely at inference; Modules 2–4 change weights during fine-tuning or RL training.

04 — FAILURE MODES

Hallucination and Reasoning Errors

ChatGPT 3.5 hallucinated frequently — producing confident answers that made no sense. This is a signature of System 1 behavior without System 2 verification: the model pattern-matches to plausible-sounding continuations rather than constructing a validated argument.

If a system truly understood a question and reasoned through it, random nonsensical answers would be far less common. Hallucination is evidence that the model was optimized to answer like humans, not to think like humans.

Reasoning errors take several forms beyond bare hallucination:

  • Arithmetic slips — standard prompting gets word problems wrong when it skips intermediate steps
  • Unverified chains — fluent step-by-step text that contains a logical error in step 2, uncorrected
  • Premature commitment — locking onto the first plausible answer without exploring alternatives (Sudoku, crosswords, proof search)
  • Missing "aha" recovery — human experts pause and backtrack; pre-o1 models rarely did

DeepSeek R1's breakthrough moment — the model saying "wait, wait, wait… let's re-evaluate this step by step" mid-solution — demonstrated that RL-trained models can learn self-correction, something pure pattern matching never develops on its own. The model had no CoT examples telling it to backtrack; the behavior emerged because backtracking led to correct answers and therefore higher reward.

Train Problem: Plain vs Reasoning Response

Question: A train moves at 60 mph for 3 hours. How far does it travel?

Regular LLM "The train travels 180 miles." — correct, but no reasoning visible.

Reasoning LLM "To find distance, use distance = speed × time. Given speed = 60 mph and time = 3 hours: distance = 60 × 3 = 180 miles. The train travels 180 miles." — same answer, full audit trail.

05 — DEFINITION

What "Reasoning" Means in This Series

Throughout this series, reasoning has a precise, operational definition:

The process of answering questions that require complex multi-step reasoning with intermediate steps — where each step is visible and the final answer follows from them.

When we say an LLM "has reasoning powers," we mean it can produce responses like the train example above: state the formula, substitute values, compute, conclude. Whether the model actually thinks in a philosophical sense remains unknown — but we can evaluate and build the behavioral signature of reasoning rigorously.

An LLM at its core takes input and predicts the next token. Reasoning models extend that loop with a longer, structured generation phase before the final answer — consuming more tokens, more compute, and more wall-clock time in exchange for accuracy on hard tasks.

07 — INFERENCE-TIME REASONING

Inference-Time Compute Scaling

Humans give better answers when they think longer. Step-by-step deliberation leads to correct conclusions — Sudoku, prime-number proofs, exam problems all share this property. The first method to induce LLM reasoning applies the same principle: allocate more computational resources during inference.

Test-Time Compute

test-time compute = computing resources used by the model during inference

Inference is everything that happens after training — when the model actually generates an answer. Training is preparation (studying for an exam); inference is performance (writing the exam). Test-time compute scales with tokens generated: 3 tokens for "Answer is 11" vs 23 tokens for a full chain-of-thought — roughly 7–8× more compute for the reasoning trace.

Key empirical finding: Model accuracy generally scales with test-time compute. More inference-time thinking → better answers on complex tasks. This is why the field uses the term inference-time compute scaling.

Chain-of-Thought Prompting (Google, 2022)

The Google Brain team introduced Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting: instead of asking for a direct answer, you provide few-shot examples where the model shows its work before concluding.

Few-shot CoT example (Roger tennis balls):

Roger started with 5 balls. Two cans of 3 tennis balls each is 6. 5 + 6 = 11. The answer is 11.

New question: Cafeteria has 23 apples, uses 20 for lunch, buys 6 more. How many now?

Cafeteria has 23 apples originally. Used 20 → 23 − 20 = 3. Bought 6 more → 3 + 6 = 9. The answer is 9.

Standard prompting on the cafeteria problem often fails (wrong arithmetic). CoT prompting teaches the model the format of reasoning from examples; the model generalizes to new questions. Subsequent methods extend this space:

  • Zero-shot CoT — "Let's think step by step" without examples
  • Tree-of-Thought — explore multiple reasoning branches
  • Verifiers / search — generate candidates, score them, pick the best

All Module 1 techniques share one property: nothing in pre-training or fine-tuning changes. Only inference behavior changes — via prompts or search — making this the lightest-weight entry point to reasoning.

Standard Prompting vs Chain-of-Thought

The cafeteria problem illustrates why CoT matters. With standard prompting, models often produce wrong arithmetic because they skip intermediate steps. CoT few-shot examples teach the model to emit a reasoning chain before the final number.

Standard Prompting
Model output (often wrong) Q: Cafeteria had 23 apples. Used 20 for lunch. Bought 6 more. How many? A: 27

No intermediate subtraction or addition visible — the model pattern-matches to a plausible number.

Chain-of-Thought Prompting
Model output (correct) 23 − 20 = 3 apples left. 3 + 6 = 9 apples now. The answer is 9.

Each arithmetic step is explicit — errors become detectable and correctable.

Verifiers and Search-Based Reasoning

Beyond prompting, a second inference-time category uses verifiers: auxiliary models or scoring functions that evaluate candidate reasoning chains. The pipeline generates multiple possible solutions, scores each one, and selects the best — analogous to a student writing several draft proofs and picking the one that checks out.

Generate K candidate chains ──► Verifier scores each ──► Select highest-scoring ──► Final answer

Verifiers sit alongside CoT extensions like Tree-of-Thought (exploring branching reasoning paths) and zero-shot CoT (appending "Let's think step by step" without examples). All three — prompting, tree search, verifiers — fall under inference-time compute scaling because they spend extra compute at generation time without updating model weights.

08 — TRAINING-TIME REASONING

RL, SFT+RL, and Distillation

Module 2: Pure Reinforcement Learning

DeepSeek showed that a model can learn to reason without explicit prompting — only through reward signals. Given a math problem, the model begins answering, then spontaneously realizes its approach is wrong: "Wait, wait, wait… that's an aha moment. Let's re-evaluate step by step." This self-correction emerged from RL, not from CoT examples in the prompt.

Unlike CoT (which modifies the prompt), pure RL uses reward functions to teach the model that reasoning is the path to maximum reward. GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization) — inspired by PPO — was central to DeepSeek R1's success. The series builds a reasoning model from an open-source base (e.g., Qwen) using this approach.

Module 3: Supervised Fine-Tuning + RL

DeepSeek R1's full pipeline — now public — is widely regarded as the blueprint for robust reasoning models. OpenAI's o1 was likely trained similarly, though its architecture remains proprietary. The pattern: warm-start with supervised fine-tuning on reasoning traces, then RL refinement. This hybrid combines demonstration learning with reward-driven exploration.

Module 4: Supervised Fine-Tuning + Distillation

Distillation transfers reasoning from a large teacher model to a small student:

  1. Take a large reasoning model (e.g., DeepSeek R1)
  2. Generate input-output pairs: questions + full reasoning traces with correct answers
  3. Fine-tune a smaller model (Qwen, Phi) on this supervised data
  4. The student inherits reasoning capability at a fraction of the parameter count

Think of it as Popeye eating spinach: the small model consumes reasoning-rich data from the teacher and emerges with reasoning strength. DeepSeek R1 distilled into smaller open models; the Sky-T1 project demonstrates the same pipeline from scratch.

RL Foundations (Classical → Modern)

Pure RL and SFT+RL both rely on reinforcement learning machinery. The series builds intuition from classical RL before applying modern policy optimization to language models:

Classical RL Concepts

Agents interact with an environment, receive rewards, and learn policies that maximize cumulative return. Core building blocks:

  • Agent & environment — the learner and the world it acts in
  • Rewards & returns — scalar feedback and discounted cumulative reward
  • Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) — formal framework for sequential decisions
  • Value & policy functions — estimating expected return and action selection
  • Monte Carlo & Temporal Difference (TD) learning — learning from episodes or bootstrapped estimates

Modern Policy Optimization

  • TRPO — Trust Region Policy Optimization; constrains policy updates
  • PPO — Proximal Policy Optimization; practical, stable policy gradients
  • GRPO — Group Relative Policy Optimization; DeepSeek R1's algorithm, inspired by PPO, comparing responses within a group to compute relative rewards
reward(answer) = 1 if correct else 0 → policy gradient → longer reasoning chains when they earn reward

For reasoning LLMs, the reward function is typically outcome-based: correct final answer on math or code tasks earns reward; incorrect answers receive none. Over training, the model discovers that emitting visible reasoning steps correlates with higher reward — even without explicit CoT examples in the prompt.

The SFT + RL Blueprint

DeepSeek R1's published pipeline — now the reference architecture for building Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) — combines two training phases:

  1. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) — warm-start the model on curated reasoning traces so it learns the format and basics of step-by-step output
  2. Reinforcement learning (RL) — refine the policy with reward signals so the model discovers better reasoning strategies, including self-correction and backtracking

OpenAI's o1 almost certainly follows a similar recipe, though its exact architecture and training data remain proprietary. The SFT+RL hybrid is more stable than pure RL alone: SFT provides a strong initialization; RL pushes beyond demonstration data into emergent behaviors like "aha moments."

Distillation Pipeline

Teacher (large reasoning model) │ ├── Generate: question + reasoning trace + answer (many examples) │ ▼ Student (smaller base model) │ └── SFT on teacher traces → deployable reasoning model

India's AI Mission, Fractal Analytics, and open projects like Sky-T1 are building LRMs through this exact distillation path — democratizing reasoning capability beyond frontier-scale models.

09 — THE ARC

From Inference-Time to Training-Time Reasoning

The four paradigms form a progression from lightweight prompting to full capability transfer:

2022 — Chain-of-Thought Prompting

Google Brain proves you can elicit multi-step reasoning at inference time with no weight updates. Accuracy scales with tokens generated.

2022–2024 — Inference extensions

Zero-shot CoT, Tree-of-Thought, verifier-based search. All spend compute at test time; model weights frozen.

Sep 2024 — OpenAI o1

First production reasoning model. Visible thinking traces. High-stakes trust through auditable chains. Training recipe undisclosed.

Jan 2025 — DeepSeek R1

Open-source reasoning at o1-level accuracy. Pure RL produces emergent self-correction. Full blueprint published.

2025+ — Distillation & LRMs

Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) proliferate — India's AI Mission, Fractal Analytics, and open projects like Sky-T1 compress teacher reasoning into deployable students.

Key Takeaway

LLMs were built to answer, not to think. Reasoning models close that gap by making intermediate computation visible and trainable — whether through inference-time prompts or training-time RL.

The shift from 3-token instant answers to 23-token reasoning traces is not cosmetic. It is the foundation of trust, accuracy, and the entire reasoning-model revolution.

Central Tension

Speed vs Accuracy

System 1 answers are fast and cheap. System 2 reasoning costs 7–8× more tokens but wins on multi-step tasks. Production systems must choose — or offer both modes, as modern APIs now do with "thinking" toggles.

Every technique in this series navigates that trade-off.
