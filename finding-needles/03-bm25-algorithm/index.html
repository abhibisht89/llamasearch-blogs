00 Why TF-IDF Is Not Enough

TF-IDF was a breakthrough because it fused two intuitive ideas: a document that repeats a query term is probably more on-topic (TF), and a term that is rare in the collection is more discriminative (IDF). For small, homogeneous corpora with similarly sized documents, that pairing often produces sensible rankings. As soon as you index the open web, enterprise knowledge bases, or product catalogs, the simplifying assumptions behind plain TF-IDF start to hurt.

The first weakness is that many TF weighting schemes treat frequency as nearly linear. If you double how often cat appears, you double (or nearly double) the contribution of cat to the score. Human relevance is not linear in repetition: the difference between seeing a term once and twice is large; the difference between ninety-eight and ninety-nine occurrences is negligible. An unbounded TF factor lets spammy, boilerplate-heavy pages accumulate enormous scores simply by repetition.

The second weakness is length normalization. Longer documents contain more tokens in total, so they accumulate higher raw counts even when the underlying topic relevance is unchanged. Without a principled correction, ranking becomes biased toward verbosity. Classic TF-IDF mitigations (sublinear TF transforms, cosine normalization of vectors) help, but they are not always aligned with probabilistic retrieval theory, and they interact awkwardly with fielded data such as titles versus bodies.

Probabilistic motivation. The probabilistic ranking principle suggests ordering documents by estimated odds of relevance given the query and the collection statistics. Scoring functions in the Best Match (BM) family were engineered to approximate those odds using quantities the inverted index already stores: term frequency, document length, document frequency, and collection size. BM25 is the sweet spot: expressive enough for production search, simple enough to implement efficiently.

The BM family grew out of the Okapi system at City University London in the early 1990s, where Stephen Robertson, Karen Spärck Jones, and colleagues developed BM1, BM11, BM15, and BM25 as practical approximations to probabilistic retrieval. BM25 became the default because it adds tunable k1 (saturation) and b (length sensitivity) in a closed-form formula that runs at index-query speed.

Lucene’s default similarity has long been BM25-style, and both Elasticsearch and Solr expose the same parameters in their similarity settings. That ubiquity makes BM25 the right mental model even when you later blend lexical scores with dense vectors or learning-to-rank models.

01 Term Saturation

In a linear TF model, if a document mentions insurance ten times, it contributes ten times the weight of a single mention. That sounds fair until you notice that after the first few mentions, you have already established topicality; additional mentions mostly reflect writing style, page template noise, or keyword stuffing. The marginal information gained from each extra occurrence drops sharply.

BM25 encodes diminishing returns with a saturating transform. Write the contribution of a term with raw count tf as a fraction that approaches a horizontal asymptote as tf grows. A common equivalent form isolates the saturation curve explicitly:

TFsaturated = (tf × (k1 + 1)) / (tf + k1)

As tf → ∞, the expression tends to k1 + 1, so the per-term contribution cannot run away without bound. The parameter k1 > 0 sets how fast the curve bends: small k1 saturates aggressively (extra mentions stop mattering quickly); large k1 stretches the curve toward linearity.

  • k1 = 0 (degenerate limit): the formula collapses to a binary “present or not” behavior—generally avoided in real engines, but useful as intuition.
  • k1 ≈ 1.2: the widespread default—moderate saturation, robust across many collections.
  • k1 ≈ 2.0: slower saturation; term frequency continues to matter across a wider numeric range.
  • k1 → ∞: the curve approaches raw tf; you lose explicit saturation (not recommended unless you have strong reasons).

k1 also interacts with tokenization: aggressive stemming inflates effective tf (often warranting a lower k1), while fine-grained tokenization keeps counts smaller (sometimes benefiting from a higher k1). The default is a starting point for grid search or click-driven tuning—not a physical constant.

02 Document Length Normalization

Consider two documents that each contain the token python exactly five times. The first is a hundred-word README; the second is a ten-thousand-word manual. Raw tf is identical, but the shorter file is far more focused: five mentions in a hundred words is a strong signal, whereas five mentions in ten thousand words might be incidental. Pure frequency without context systematically undervalues concise, information-dense texts and overvalues long texts that roam across many subtopics.

BM25 compares each document’s length dl to the average document length avgdl in the collection. The normalization factor multiplies k1 inside the denominator, effectively requiring more term evidence in longer-than-average documents to reach the same score contribution:

length factor = (1 − b) + b × (dl / avgdl)

The mixing parameter b ∈ [0, 1] controls how aggressively length is applied. With b = 0, the factor is always 1: lengths are ignored. With b = 1, the factor is exactly dl / avgdl: a document twice the average length needs twice the term count to match a shorter peer. The classic default b = 0.75 is a compromise that damps—but does not fully eliminate—length effects, which tends to work well when collections mix tweets, articles, and long-form help pages.

03 The Complete BM25 Formula

Putting saturation and length normalization together, the relevance score of document D for multi-term query Q is a sum over query terms qi. Let f(qi, D) be the raw term frequency of qi in D, let dl be the length of D, and let N be the total number of documents with df(qi) the document frequency of the term.

Score(D, Q) = Σi IDF(qi) · [ f(qi,D) · (k1 + 1) ] / [ f(qi,D) + k1 · ((1 − b) + b · dl / avgdl) ]

The IDF factor in the Robertson–Walker formulation is not the textbook log(N / df). It uses a smoothed ratio that behaves gracefully when terms are extremely common or rare:

IDF(t) = ln((N − df(t) + 0.5) / (df(t) + 0.5) + 1)

The +0.5 offsets are a form of smoothing inspired by Bayesian priors on counts. Unlike some classic definitions, this IDF can dip below zero when a term appears in more than half of the documents—signaling that the term is not discriminative in that collection. Engines sometimes add clamps or blend with other signals so extremely common terms do not invert rankings unexpectedly.

04 BM25 vs TF-IDF — Side by Side

      

        Both functions try to rank by lexical overlap, but they implement different assumptions. TF-IDF (in its vector-space cosine form)
        often combines sublinear TF with a classic IDF and then normalizes entire vectors—mixing term interactions globally.
        BM25 sums per-term contributions that each saturate independently and carries length correction inside every term’s denominator.

05 BM25F — Field-Aware BM25

      

        Real records are structured: a title, a body, a url, anchor text, metadata tags, and so on. A match
        in the title is usually a stronger confirmation of topicality than the same match buried in a footer. BM25F generalizes
        BM25 by scoring each field separately—each field may have its own length statistics, its own k1 and b,
        and a boost weight—then combining the field-level signals.
      

      

        Conceptually, you compute a weighted effective term frequency: sum over fields of boostf × TFf (with
        field-specific normalization), then plug that aggregate into the BM25 rational form, or you sum fully field-local
        BM25 scores. Aggregation semantics vary by paper and engine, but field boosts are first-class—not an afterthought layered on a flat bag of words.
      

      

        Elasticsearch exposes this idea through similarities per field, multi_match with typed field-centric combinations,
        and newer query types such as combined_fields that aim to treat multiple text fields as a single inverted index for scoring
        while still respecting term positions where possible. Tuning title boosts is often the highest leverage change you can make before touching
        embeddings.

06 Beyond BM25 — Bayesian BM25 and Modern Variants

      

        Research did not stop at the default formula. Bayesian BM25 places priors on term weights or hyperparameters so estimates adapt
        to collection size and sparsity more smoothly than fixed heuristics. Practically, you see this spirit in continued smoothing tweaks and
        collection-specific tuning rather than a single monolithic “Bayesian switch” in production engines.
      

      

        BM25+ and BM25L adjust the lower bound of the term-frequency component so very long documents are not
        over-penalized when lengths explode—useful in legal search and technical documentation where page length correlates weakly with relevance.
        Another extension adds a k3 parameter for query-term frequency (repeating a token in the query increases its weight), which
        mirrors how some interfaces let users paste long, repetitive queries.
      

      

        Neural dense retrieval and cross-encoders dominate leaderboards on many benchmarks, yet BM25 remains fiercely competitive as a
        zero-shot lexical baseline—fast, interpretable, and surprisingly hard to beat on exact-match-heavy tasks. The BEIR benchmark
        suite popularized reporting both neural models and lexical baselines across diverse domains; teams routinely cite BM25 numbers
        to prove that a new architecture generalizes beyond a single corpus. When neural models fail on rare entities or out-of-domain vocabulary,
        a strong lexical ranker is often the safety net.
