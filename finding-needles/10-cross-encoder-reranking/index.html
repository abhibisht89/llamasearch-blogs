Cross-Encoder Reranking

00 · The Retrieve-Then-Rerank Pattern

Large-scale search begins with a first-stage retriever that must be extremely fast over millions or billions of documents. Classical sparse methods like BM25 and modern dense methods using bi-encoders both fit this role: they produce a cheap, differentiable score or distance for every (or many) candidates, often implemented with inverted indexes or approximate nearest neighbor (ANN) structures.

That speed comes with a structural limitation: the retriever scores each document with only shallow interaction between query and text. A bi-encoder maps the query and each document to independent vectors; relevance is a similarity in embedding space, not a joint read of both sequences. Sparse retrieval matches lexical overlap but cannot deeply resolve paraphrase, negation, or fine-grained entailment without heavier machinery.

The second stage accepts a short list—often top-100 or top-1000—and applies a cross-encoder that ingests query and document together. This stage is slower because it runs a full transformer forward pass per pair, but it can exploit cross-attention between every query token and every document token. In production, that is the standard trade-off: cast a wide, fast net; then discriminate precisely among survivors.

Latency budgeting is how teams ship this in practice. First-stage retrieval stays cheap relative to reranking; cross-encoder cost grows with k, model size, sequence length, GPU batching, and whether you cascade multiple rerankers. Operational takeaway

Treat reranking as an optional but high-value layer behind the same API: if p95 latency allows another 50ms, you often buy measurable gains in nDCG, click-through rate, or downstream task success. If the product is autocomplete or sub-20ms SLAs, keep retrieval-only or use distilled bi-encoders instead.

01 · How Cross-Encoders Work

A cross-encoder for ranking is still a transformer encoder at its core—commonly initialized from BERT, RoBERTa, or DeBERTa. The crucial difference from dual-encoder retrieval is the input construction: the query and the candidate document are concatenated into one token sequence with special separators, then fed through the full stack of self-attention layers.

A typical pattern is [CLS] query_tokens [SEP] document_tokens [SEP]. The final hidden state at [CLS] (or a pooled combination of states) feeds a small classification head that outputs either a relevance logit or a distribution over ordinal grades. That scalar is your relevance score for the pair. Because every layer can attend across the boundary between query and document, the model can represent interactions like “this phrase in the query is contradicted by this sentence in the document”—something a single dot product between two fixed vectors struggles to capture.

[CLS] ⊕ Tokenize(q) ⊕ [SEP] ⊕ Tokenize(d) ⊕ [SEP] → EncoderL → h[CLS] → wTh + b → score(q, d)

Why it is more powerful than bi-encoders. Bi-encoders compute f(q) and g(d) separately; relevance is sim(f(q), g(d)). Information that only appears when both sides are read together—coreference, negation scope, fine overlap of multi-word expressions—has no dedicated channel except what happens to land in the two embeddings. The cross-encoder’s self-attention is fully “cross” between query and document positions, so it performs joint reasoning over the pair.

Cost. You cannot pre-compute a document-side representation that suffices for arbitrary future queries. Each new query requires new forward passes for every candidate document you want to score. Complexity is O(N) in the number of scored pairs for that query, versus O(1) amortized lookup per document after indexing with a bi-encoder (plus the one-time cost of encoding the query).

That is why cross-encoders are reserved for top-k reranking: you deliberately shrink N so that the expensive joint computation fires only on a manageable candidate set.

02 · Training Cross-Encoders

Training data for ranking models traditionally arrives as triples (query, document, relevance_label). The label may be binary—relevant or not—or graded on an ordinal scale such as 0–4 as in some TREC pooling judgments. Graded labels reflect degrees of usefulness: a document may be on-topic but shallow, or highly authoritative and complete.

Loss functions follow the label type. Binary relevance pairs naturally use binary cross-entropy against a sigmoid on the relevance logit. Graded labels are often treated as regression targets with mean squared error (MSE) on normalized scores, or as ordinal classification with appropriate link functions. Pairwise and listwise objectives—ranking SVM variants, ListNet-style surrogates—appear in research and some industrial trainers when full ranked lists are available.

Data sources include public passage collections such as MS MARCO, ad-hoc retrieval benchmarks from TREC, and proprietary human judgments from search logs (explicit ratings) or implicit signals (clicks, dwell time) carefully debiased. Fine-tuning almost always starts from a strong pretrained checkpoint: BERT-base, RoBERTa, or DeBERTa-v3 gives the cross-encoder linguistic coverage before it specializes on relevance.

Example checkpoint

The cross-encoder/ms-marco-MiniLM-L-6-v2 model is widely used as a default reranker: six transformer layers make it relatively small and fast while retaining strong accuracy on passage ranking tasks distilled from MS MARCO supervision. Larger DeBERTa-based variants trade latency for marginal gains—worth it when quality is paramount and hardware allows.

Binary setup

Labels in {0,1}. Optimize BCE; threshold scores for deployment or keep raw logits for ranking sort.

Graded setup

Labels in {0,…,4}. MSE or ordinal loss; better calibrated scores for blending with other signals.

03 · Bi-Encoder vs Cross-Encoder — The Full Comparison

As introduced in Chapter 6 (and extended in Chapter 7), bi-encoders encode query and document independently for fast ANN retrieval with shallow interaction; cross-encoders score pairs jointly with full cross-attention—more accurate per pair, but orders of magnitude slower at large N.

Sweet spot. Use a bi-encoder (or BM25 hybrid) to retrieve top-k, then a cross-encoder to rerank. You keep ANN-scale coverage and pay the heavy model only where it matters.

      04 · Reranking Pipeline Design

      Choosing k—how many candidates enter the reranker—is the central knob. Larger k improves the chance that the true best documents are still in the pool (higher recall at the reranker input) but increases GPU time and memory. Values like 20, 100, and 1000 appear in real systems depending on stage latency budgets and whether multiple rerankers are chained.

      Batch inference on GPU amortizes fixed launch costs: scoring 64 short pairs in one forward pass is far more throughput-efficient than 64 serial calls. Production stacks often pack variable-length sequences with attention masks, or use optimized runtimes (ONNX TensorRT, specialized kernels) to shrink wall-clock time.

      Cascading rerankers stack a cheap model on top-100 and a heavy model on top-20 emerging from the first rerank. Each stage trims the list for the next, preserving most of the accuracy of the final cross-encoder while bounding average cost. Caching repeated queries—common in B2B search or support portals—stores ordered candidate IDs or even scores to skip recomputation within a TTL.

      Checklist for engineers

      Log p50/p95 rerank latency separately from retrieval; track nDCG@k offline when labels exist; A/B test online metrics when they do not. If latency spikes at peak traffic, reduce k, narrow passages (chunking), or fall back to the first stage only under load shedding.

      05 · Distillation — Making Cross-Encoders Faster

      When cross-encoder latency is still too high—yet you want better-than-bi-encoder interaction—knowledge distillation trains a student model to imitate the teacher’s behavior. The teacher is typically a large cross-encoder; the student is often a bi-encoder (dual tower) that recovers a scoring function compatible with ANN indexes.

      Training pairs consist of queries and documents with teacher scores or teacher-implied rankings. A common objective is margin-MSE: penalize differences between student and teacher scores while amplifying separation between positives and hard negatives. Listwise variants match entire ranked orders produced by the teacher. The practical result is a student that approaches teacher quality while retaining O(1) lookup semantics at serving time.

      Modern strong retrievers such as ColBERTv2 and the E5 family leverage related ideas—multi-stage training, teacher signals, and sometimes late interaction—to close the gap between fast retrieval and cross-encoder precision. Distillation does not magically remove all limits, but it shifts the Pareto frontier in the right direction for many workloads.

      06 · Practical Tips

      When to rerank: whenever your latency budget tolerates roughly tens to a couple hundred milliseconds of extra work per query and you care about ranking quality more than absolute minimum response time. Reranking is standard in enterprise search, support knowledge bases, legal discovery assistants, and e-commerce browse-to-search flows.

      Model selection: start with ms-marco-MiniLM-class cross-encoders for a strong speed/quality balance on English passages; move to DeBERTa-based checkpoints when offline metrics justify the cost. Integration paths include the sentence_transformers.cross_encoder.CrossEncoder API in Python and hosted rerank endpoints from providers (for example Cohere Rerank) when you prefer not to operate GPU infrastructure.

      Monitoring: dashboard rerank stage latency percentiles, error rates, and timeout fallbacks; compare nDCG or task success with rerank on vs off. When not to rerank: keystroke-level autocomplete, high-frequency trading adjacent tools, and rigid sub-20ms SLAs are poor fits unless you use aggressive caching or distilled students only.

      Hybrid reminder. Combine sparse and dense first-stage signals, then let the cross-encoder arbitrate among the union. Rerankers are especially good at fixing cases where recall was right but initial ordering was wrong.
