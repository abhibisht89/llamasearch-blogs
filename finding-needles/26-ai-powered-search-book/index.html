Part 1 — Modern search relevance (book chapters 1–4)

Section 00 · Introducing AI-Powered Search Book Ch 1

AI-powered search is a systems mindset: domain-aware, contextual, learned, and assistive—not a single algorithm. Search is pull-oriented (stated queries); recommendations are push-oriented (behavior-driven proposals). In production the boundary blurs—type-ahead, related searches, and personalization sit on the same retrieval substrate.

Staged maturity runs keyword → statistical → AI-enhanced → AI-powered, each stage fixing failure modes the prior left open. Content becomes reflected intelligence (logged behavior) and then curated AI (human guardrails on training and deployment).

The hands-on path uses Python, PySpark, Docker, Jupyter, and Solr/Elasticsearch. The reflected intelligence feedback loop: behavior informs models, models change rankings, new behavior arrives—provided logging and governance keep pace.

Official code lives in the open repository. Start with the welcome notebook for environment setup and the chapter index that mirrors the printed progression.

Search engine posture

Query text is primary; retrieval is conditional on stated intent; evaluation often uses query-centric metrics (precision at k, nDCG) and human judgments keyed by query.

Recommender posture

User or item embeddings and co-occurrence drive candidates; queries may be implicit; evaluation may emphasize diversity, novelty, and calibration beyond a single query string.

RetroTech as a teaching device

RetroTech is the book’s nostalgic e-commerce vertical—rich attributes (compatibility, era, condition) on a catalog small enough for notebooks. Swap in your own inventory later; the checkpoints stay the same: tokenization, fielded matching, signal hygiene, train/serve skew, and query-independent evaluation splits.

Section 01 · Working with Natural Language Book Ch 2

Language is irregularly structured—tokenization, stemming, and Unicode normalization decide which equivalences the engine can discover. Distributional semantics, embeddings, synonyms, taxonomies, and knowledge graphs each address polysemy and query–document asymmetry. Models need content plus signals.

Chapter 2 is largely conceptual in print; the repository’s notebooks begin in earnest with vector similarity and relevance control in Chapter 3, while open information extraction in Chapter 5 demonstrates structure recovery from running text—useful to revisit once embeddings are intuitive.
Notebooks: welcome.ipynb · ch05/1.open-information-extraction.ipynb

From characters to decisions

Indexing policy—Unicode normalization, punctuation around model numbers, stemming choices—must be explicit before spell correction or phrase notebooks assume it. Embeddings help; curated synonyms still matter at launch boundaries and regional vocabulary.

Section 02 · Ranking and Content-Based Relevance Book Ch 3

Lexical ranking scores documents with sparse vectors and cosine similarity. The book’s baseline scorers are TF-IDF (Chapter 2) and BM25 (Chapter 3)—IDF weighting, term-frequency saturation (k1), and length normalization (b)—rather than re-deriving those formulas here.

Classic BM25 term component (conceptual): score ∝ (IDF-like) × (f × (k1 + 1)) / (f + k1 × (1 - b + b × |d|/avgdl))
where f is term frequency in document d, |d| is length, avgdl is average document length. Engine implementations add coordination, boosts, and field-specific statistics.

Beyond the textual score, function queries inject business logic: freshness curves, inventory thresholds, price bands, and popularity—all as query-time score components. The book contrasts multiplicative vs. additive boosting: multiplicative factors can dominate the base score if you are not careful; additive blends behave more predictably in tuning but may understate strong business constraints unless weights are calibrated.

A discipline the book enforces is matching vs. ranking: Boolean filters and structured constraints should often remove ineligible items entirely, while softer preferences belong in scoring layers. Mixing eligibility into fuzzy boosts produces brittle results that are hard to debug under load.

Domain-specific relevance

RetroTech exercises fielded matching and merchant-specific synonyms—not generic web BM25 defaults. Control relevance before asking ML to optimize it.

Section 03 · Crowdsourced Relevance Book Ch 4

Chapter 4 pivots RetroTech from text-only ranking to content + signals + models: clicks and conversions become boosts, then LTR features. Popularized, personalized, and generalized relevance—aggregate popularity, collaborative filtering, supervised ranking—are developed in Chapter 14.

When you replay these notebooks, pay attention to how often the authors separate eligibility from preference. Signals are powerful and dangerous: naïvely boosting whatever is clicked will entrench position bias and inventory skew.
Notebooks: ch04/1.setting-up-the-retrotech-dataset.ipynb · ch04/2.signals-boosting.ipynb

Signals as contracts

Define each signal’s schema, retention, and aggregation window before boosting with it. Separate eligibility (filters) from preference (scores)—naïve CTR boosts entrench position bias and inventory skew.

Part 2 — Learning domain-specific context (book chapters 5–7)

Section 04 · Knowledge Graph Learning Book Ch 5

Text flows through open extraction (spaCy, Hearst patterns) into a Semantic Knowledge Graph (SKG) with weighted foreground vs. background edges. Those neighborhoods drive query expansion, recommendations, and intent features—see Chapter 20.

Notebooks: ch05/1.open-information-extraction.ipynb · ch05/2.index-datasets.ipynb · ch05/3.semantic-knowledge-graph.ipynb

Operational checklist for graph-backed search

  • Provenance: store which sentence or field produced an edge so you can delete or down-weight bad extractions without rebuilding everything.
  • Cardinality caps: high-degree hub nodes (generic terms like “audio”) need trimmed neighborhoods or foreground filters before they are allowed to expand queries.
  • Refresh cadence: batch graph rebuilds on catalog updates; incremental patches for hot products if your commerce cycle demands it.
  • Explainability: when an expansion fires in the UI, surface “because your query matched category X” rather than exposing raw edge labels to shoppers.

Section 05 · Using Context to Learn Domain-Specific Language Book Ch 6

Once an SKG exists, it becomes an intent machine. Query intent classification can treat graph neighborhoods as features: a query that activates “portable audio” subgraph nodes differs from one that activates “home stereo” nodes even when the raw keywords overlap. Query-sense disambiguation is the operational sibling of polysemy resolution—choose the sense that best aligns with activated entities and historical successful reformulations.

The book shows how to mine related phrases from query logs using association statistics such as PMI² and composite ranking schemes that blend frequency, independence, and domain filters. Phrase detection and entity extraction turn flat token streams into units commerce cares about: model numbers, brands, formats (VHS, Betamax), and compatibility phrases. Misspelling correction combines evidence from document-side term distributions and signal-side query variants so that “walkmn” can route to “walkman” without a hand-tuned dictionary for every SKU typo.

Why signals matter for language learning

Documents tell you what merchants say; signals tell you what shoppers mean. The book repeatedly stitches these channels so that expansions remain grounded in inventory text but prioritized by how people actually search.

Notebooks: ch06/1.skg-classification-disambiguation.ipynb · ch06/2.related-keywords-from-signals.ipynb · ch06/3.spell-correction.ipynb · ch06/bonus.phrase-detection.ipynb · ch06/bonus.related-terms-from-documents.ipynb

Section 06 · Interpreting Query Intent Through Semantic Search Book Ch 7

Chapter 7 formalizes the query interpretation pipeline: parse the query into tokens and structural hints; enrich with entities, phrases, and graph contexts; transform into an executable query plan (expanded terms, filters, boosts, optional vector clauses); then search against the index. Each stage is a place where errors compound, so observability—logging intermediate representations—is part of the design, not an afterthought.

Tagging query terms aligns tokens with fields, entity types, or facets. Knowledge graph traversals expand queries along weighted edges, often with caps and filters to prevent explosion. Sparse lexical expansion models keep interpretability: you can explain why a synonym fired. Semantically-enhanced search begins to blend those expansions with vector similarity or learned sparse structures so that recall improves without surrendering every guardrail from earlier chapters.

Lexical expansion

High explainability; risk of query drift if graph edges are noisy; cheap at query time.

Dense augmentation

Better recall on paraphrases; requires ANN infrastructure and calibration against lexical precision.

Notebooks: ch07/1.index-datasets.ipynb · ch07/2.semantic-search.ipynb

Debugging the interpretation pipeline

When results look wrong after Chapter 7, bisect the pipeline: log the parsed tokens, the enriched entities, the expanded term set, and the final Solr query JSON. Wrong expansions usually mean foreground edges were too promiscuous; empty results often mean filters from intent classification were too aggressive. Semantic components should fail open to lexical fallbacks when confidence drops—users prefer noisy recall to a blank SERP.

Part 3 — Reflected intelligence (book chapters 8–12)

Section 07 · Signals-Boosting Models Book Ch 8

Signals boosting encodes behavioral evidence as normalized features—popularity, freshness, conversions—with caps against spam and time decay so flash trends do not stick forever. Index-time materialization trades reactivity for latency; query-time boosts do the reverse. When weight spaghetti outgrows manual tuning, graduate to LTR (Chapter 14).

Treat boosts as experiments: every boost is a hypothesis about user utility. If you cannot trace a boost to a logged field and roll it back, you do not have engineering control—you have folklore encoded in Solr JSON.
Notebooks: ch08/1.signals-boosting.ipynb

Where boosting ends and LTR begins

Inspectable boost weights help until dozens of partially correlated features make mental simulation impossible—then shared feature logging and a supervised ranker are cheaper to maintain, with hard eligibility still in filters.

Section 08 · Personalized Search Book Ch 9

Personalization is powerful and ethically loaded. The book warns about filter bubbles—rankings that overfit past behavior and hide valid discovery paths. Clustering-based guardrails attempt to keep users within diverse interest clusters rather than collapsing to a single embedding centroid that misrepresents multi-intent shoppers.

Embedding-based personalization profiles summarize a user as a vector (or small set of vectors) derived from historical interactions; at query time, similarity to item vectors or session context reranks candidates. Collaborative filtering with matrix factorization connects users and items in a shared latent space, echoing recommender literature while staying compatible with search indexes that still respect textual constraints.

The chapter returns to the spectrum between search and recommendations: personalization is not a separate product—it is a layer that decides which candidates deserve promotion after retrieval has already enforced hard constraints.

Notebooks: ch09/1.personalization.ipynb · ch09/2.embedding-based-personalization.ipynb

Section 09 · Learning to Rank Book Ch 10

Learning to rank (LTR) replaces hand-tuned boosts with supervised models over logged features—judgments, pairwise transforms, deployment via the Solr LTR plugin. Split train/test by query; watch train/serve skew and label leakage. See Chapter 22; click-derived labels in Chapter 23.

Pairwise idea: for query q, if doc A is judged better than doc B, learn w such that score(q, A; w) > score(q, B; w). Aggregate many such inequalities with a margin objective (SVM-style).
Notebooks: ch10/1.setup-the-movie-db.ipynb · ch10/2.judgments-and-logging.ipynb · ch10/3.pairwise-transform.ipynb · ch10/4.train-and-evaluate-the-model.ipynb

Training pitfalls called out in the LTR chapters

Hold out entire query clusters—not random rows—and explicitly test cold-start items and rare intents so offline metrics do not memorialize the training set.

Section 10 · Click Models for Automated LTR Book Ch 11

Human judgments do not scale forever. Click-through rate as a naive click model equates clicks with relevance, which confuses “seen and attractive at rank 1” with “actually satisfied the need.” The book catalogs biases: position bias (upper ranks get more looks), confidence bias (users click trusted domains even when a lower result is better), and presentation bias (thumbnails, badges, and sale flags move eyes regardless of intrinsic relevance).

The Simple Dynamic Bayesian Network (SDBN) model separates examination from relevance: a click is evidence of examination, but non-clicks after examination carry information too. Beta priors help stabilize estimates when data is sparse, which matters for long-tail queries where raw CTRs lie. The repository’s ltr/clickmodels/ folder provides scaffolding to explore additional assumptions—cascade models, position-based variants, and utilities to synthesize or transform session logs.

The narrative payoff is automated LTR: instead of paying annotators for every refresh of the catalog, you derive training labels from debiased click models—always with sanity checks, holdouts, and human spot audits for high-stakes queries.

Notebooks: ch11/1.click-through-rate-judgments.ipynb · ch11/2.sdbn-judgments-to-overcome-position-bias.ipynb · ch11/3.SDBN-Confidence-Bias.ipynb · ch11/4.train-upload-search-ltr.ipynb · ch11/a.synthesize-search-sessions.ipynb · ltr/clickmodels/

Section 11 · Overcoming Bias Through Active Learning Book Ch 12

Automated labels still inherit bias. The book shows an automated LTR engine compact enough to read in a sitting, then layers governance: A/B testing models, monitoring for when “good” models go bad under distribution shift, and targeted data collection to repair blind spots. Presentation bias is tackled with a Gaussian Process formulation for explore/exploit: maintain uncertainty about the value of showing unfamiliar layouts or candidates, and deliberately sample high-uncertainty cases when exploration is warranted.

The Expected Improvement criterion selects which ranking candidates or interventions to label or deploy next, trading off predicted gain against risk. The macroscopic loop is timeless: explore → exploit → gather → retrain. The theta parameter controls risk appetite—higher theta leans exploration and diversity of evidence; lower theta hunkers down on known winners.

Active learning is not “randomly try weird results.” It is a disciplined way to spend human attention where model uncertainty and business impact intersect.
Notebooks: ch12/0.setup.ipynb · ch12/1.ab-testing-to-active-learning.ipynb

Part 4 — The search frontier (book chapters 13–15)

Section 12 · Semantic Search with Dense Vectors Book Ch 13

Dense retrieval uses SBERT-style bi-encoders, ANN indexes (HNSW, NMSLIB), and optional quantization to improve recall on paraphrases. The book switches to Outdoors StackExchange for question-style stress tests. See Chapters 06, 07, and 08.

Notebooks: ch13/1.setting-up-the-outdoors-dataset.ipynb · ch13/2.introduction-to-transformers.ipynb · ch13/3.natural-language-autocomplete.ipynb · ch13/4.semantic-search.ipynb · ch13/5.quantization.ipynb

ANN serving: what the notebooks imply about production

ANN indexes trade build cost, memory, and recall@k for latency; hybrid pipelines often pre-filter lexical constraints or post-filter ANN hits and fuse with RRF when sub-retrievers disagree on score scale.

Section 13 · Question Answering with a Fine-Tuned LLM Book Ch 14

Question answering is presented through the classic retriever–reader pattern: retrieve candidate passages, then read them with a model that predicts answer spans or generates constrained answers. Data work is staged: a silver set bootstraps labels from a pretrained model’s predictions; a golden set applies human-in-the-loop correction so errors do not compound silently.

Fine-tuning RoBERTa for QA walks through tokenization alignment, the Hugging Face Trainer loop, and loss evaluation that respects span boundaries. The book then assembles retriever + reader + reranker into a coherent stack and exposes a single ask() function as the developer-facing seam—mirroring how products wrap complexity behind a narrow API.

Why span QA still matters in the generative era

Span supervision teaches grounding: the model must point to evidence. That habit carries forward when you add generative heads that must cite passages or abstain when retrieval is weak.

Bonus · Maven course notebooks beyond the printed book

The open repository also ships additional notebooks aimed at the Maven companion track. Treat them as optional accelerants once the book’s spine makes sense—they assume comfort with Solr/SKGs/signals from earlier chapters.

  • Late interaction (ColBERT): token-level similarity matrices for high-fidelity relevance with more compute than bi-encoders; useful when precision at the top matters more than index size.
  • MUVERA: multi-vector retrieval ideas that avoid collapsing entire documents into one centroid—helpful for long pages with multiple intents.
  • LambdaMART: gradient-boosted tree LTR; a staple industry baseline when sparse features dominate and deep models are unnecessary.
  • Cross encoders: full query–document attention for reranking a short candidate list; the accuracy layer atop fast bi-encoder retrieval.
  • Structured RAG: retrieval that respects schemas, tables, and graph constraints instead of dumping unstructured chunks into a prompt.
  • Semantic caching: reuse answers or retrieved sets for paraphrased queries that map to the same intent embedding—latency and cost win when safety checks pass.
  • Session-based evaluation: metrics that look across multi-turn journeys, not isolated queries—closer to product reality.
  • Agentic search loop: planners that issue searches, read, and revise; aligns with modern tool-using assistants while inheriting all logging and bias concerns from Parts 3–4.
Skim the course/ directory in the same GitHub repository for filenames and ordering; notebooks evolve with the ecosystem—verify paths after pulling main.

How these extras connect back to RetroTech

Maven notebooks stack on the book’s spine: cross-encoders and ColBERT spend extra compute on top-50 candidates; LambdaMART handles heterogeneous tabular features; structured RAG keeps SKG constraints in generation. Same Solr logging hooks—different head models and evaluation harnesses.
