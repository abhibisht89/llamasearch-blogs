00 Beyond Boolean — The Need for Ranking

In a purely boolean retrieval system, the index answers a simple membership question: does document d satisfy the query’s logical conditions? Every surviving document is treated as equally good. There is no notion of “more relevant” versus “barely relevant,” which is awkward for people who scan a short list of results and expect the best match at the top.

Real search interfaces almost always rank results. Ranking requires a score: a real number (or at least an ordering) that summarizes how well a document aligns with an information need. Once you have scores, you can sort descending, apply thresholds, blend with other signals, or explain “why this result” using contributing terms.

The vector space model turns unstructured text into numbers we can compare by fixing a vocabulary of terms (dimensions). Each document becomes a vector: component i describes how important term i is in that document. The query becomes another vector in the same space. Similarity between vectors approximates relevance.

Raw counts are a start, but they are skewed by document length and by how common a term is in the whole collection. TF-IDF combines a term frequency (TF) factor with an inverse document frequency (IDF) factor so that frequent-but-meaningless words like the do not drown out rare, specific words like superconductor.

01 Term Frequency (TF)

The simplest measure of how much a term t appears in a document d is the raw term frequency: count how many times t occurs in d. This is easy to compute and interpret, and it correlates with the intuition that a document that mentions diabetes ten times is probably more about diabetes than one that mentions it once.

Raw counts are not fair across documents of different lengths. A 5,000-word article naturally accumulates larger counts than a 200-word abstract, even when both are “about” the topic to the same degree. Longer documents are not inherently more relevant; they simply have more tokens. Any scoring function that uses raw counts without adjustment tends to bias toward long texts.

Log-frequency
1 + log(tf) for tf > 0, else 0. Each additional occurrence matters less than the previous one: the first hit is a big jump; the twentieth hit adds only a little. This dampens runaway scores in long documents.
Augmented frequency
Divide by the most frequent term in the document: tf / max_tf. This normalizes within the document so all weights lie in [0, 1] relative to that document’s own term distribution.

Another useful variant is boolean frequency: 1 if the term appears at least once, 0 otherwise. Boolean TF throws away intensity entirely and is common when you care only about presence in structured fields or when combining with other binary features.

02 Document Frequency and IDF

Document frequency, written df_t, is the number of documents in the collection that contain term t at least once (definitions sometimes differ on counting; consistency matters more than the exact convention). If df_t is large, the term is common across the corpus. If df_t is small, the term is rare and often more discriminative: it narrows the set of plausible documents sharply.

The word the appears in nearly every English document, so seeing the in a document tells you almost nothing about the subject. The token quantum appears in far fewer documents, so its presence is a stronger hint about topic. IDF formalizes “how surprising is it to see this term?”

Classic IDF (smooth variants exist):
IDF(t) = log(N / df_t)
where N is the total number of documents and df_t ≥ 1 for terms in the vocabulary.

The logarithm is not decorative. Without compression, a term that appears in only one document could receive an enormous multiplier relative to a term that appears in two documents; the gap grows explosively as rarity increases. The log reduces dynamic range so rare terms matter more, but not so much that a single odd token can dominate every score. Different bases change scale only; ranking with cosine similarity is often invariant to a constant factor applied to all components. Production systems also add smoothing to avoid division by zero and to tame outliers, e.g. log((N + 1) / (df_t + 1)) + 1. The demos here use the classic form with df ≥ 1.

03 TF-IDF — The Full Formula

TF-IDF multiplies how strongly a term shows up in a specific document (TF) by how informative that term is across the collection (IDF). The product rewards terms that are both locally prominent and globally rare.

TF-IDF(t, d) = TF(t, d) × IDF(t)

There is no single official TF or IDF; research papers and search engines pick combinations that behave well on their data. What matters is the pattern: local frequency times a collection-level rarity weight. The rest is engineering.

Worked example (toy corpus, by hand)

Collection: three documents. Vocabulary terms for illustration: cat, dog, park.

  • d1: “cat cat dog”
  • d2: “dog park”
  • d3: “cat park park”

Let N = 3. Document frequencies: cat appears in d1 and d3 → df=2. dog in d1, d2 → df=2. park in d2, d3 → df=2.

Use raw TF and IDF = log₂(N/df) (base 2 here for tidy numbers): IDF = log₂(3/2) ≈ 0.585 for each term.

Query q: “cat park”. Treat the query as a mini-document: TF(cat)=1, TF(park)=1, others 0.

DocVector (cat, dog, park) TF-IDF
d1(2×0.585, 1×0.585, 0) ≈ (1.17, 0.585, 0)
d2(0, 1×0.585, 1×0.585) ≈ (0, 0.585, 0.585)
d3(1×0.585, 0, 2×0.585) ≈ (0.585, 0, 1.17)
q(0.585, 0, 0.585)

Both d1 and d3 share two query terms with non-zero mass; d2 matches only park. Cosine similarity ranks d3 and d1 above d2 for this query. The exact order between d1 and d3 depends on vector direction, not just raw dot product, because lengths differ.

TF captures topical focus within the document; IDF down-weights terms that fail to separate documents. Together they approximate “this document emphasizes the same unusual words the query emphasizes.”

Common variants include sublinear TF (1 + log(tf)), smoothed IDF, and L2 normalization of document vectors before similarity. BM25 applies the same ideas with probabilistic term weighting and stronger length normalization.

04 The Vector Space Model

Fix an ordered vocabulary {t₁, …, tₙ}. A document d maps to ℝⁿ by choosing a weight for each term. With TF-IDF, weight i is zero if term tᵢ is absent (depending on TF definition), otherwise it reflects prominence times rarity. The query is embedded using the same vocabulary and weighting scheme so that “closeness” in space means “overlap on important terms.”

This representation is a bag of words: word order is ignored. Two permutations of the same multiset of tokens are identical vectors. That limitation is severe for phrases and negation, but it keeps computation tractable and makes inverted indexes practical.

Full visualization of ℝⁿ is impractical for large n, but low-dimensional projections still clarify the geometry. Each document is a point (or arrow from the origin); the query is another arrow. Neighboring directions correspond to shared vocabulary emphasis. In 3D, pick three terms as axes and place every document and the query at coordinates (w₁, w₂, w₃), where each wᵢ is the TF-IDF weight for that term. Distance from the origin reflects overall weighted mass; direction reflects which terms dominate. The demo below projects onto two terms for readability; the same geometry extends to arbitrary dimension.

05 Cosine Similarity

Euclidean distance between raw count vectors often punishes long documents: they sit far away in space simply because their vectors have larger magnitude, even when the topic mix is similar. Cosine similarity measures the angle between vectors, intentionally discarding magnitude to a large extent.

cos(θ) = (A · B) / (‖A‖ × ‖B‖)

If vectors are non-negative (typical for TF-IDF), cosine similarity ranges from 0 (orthogonal, no shared weighted terms) to 1 (same direction). Negative values appear if you allow negative weights (some embeddings), but classical TF-IDF stays in [0, 1] for the usual setups.

Worked cosine example (query vs three documents)

Use the same vectors as in the toy table (already TF-IDF weighted):

  • q = (0.585, 0, 0.585)
  • d1 = (1.17, 0.585, 0)
  • d2 = (0, 0.585, 0.585)
  • d3 = (0.585, 0, 1.17)

Dot products: q·d1 = 0.685, q·d2 = 0.343, q·d3 = 1.37. Norms: ‖q‖ ≈ 0.827, ‖d1‖ ≈ 1.31, ‖d2‖ ≈ 0.827, ‖d3‖ ≈ 1.31. Cosines: sim(q,d1) ≈ 0.63, sim(q,d2) ≈ 0.50, sim(q,d3) ≈ 0.79. Ranking: d3, then d1, then d2.

DAAT vs TAAT. Document-at-a-time (DAAT) walks postings for each query term in parallel pointers, accumulating a score per document as you go—friendly to block-max pruning. Term-at-a-time (TAAT) processes one term’s postings fully before moving to the next, maintaining per-document accumulators—simpler to implement but often harder on cache for wide queries. Both compute the same dot products when scoring is decomposable per term.

06 Putting It All Together — A Scored Search

End-to-end retrieval with TF-IDF and cosine ranking follows a repeatable pipeline. First, normalize text: tokenize, optionally stem or leave tokens raw, filter stopwords if desired (some systems keep them for phrases). Build the vocabulary from the indexed corpus (or use a fixed lexicon). For each document, compute term frequencies, attach collection statistics for IDF, and form the document vector. At query time, form the query vector with the same formula.

Scoring is then vector similarity—most often cosine. Sort documents by descending similarity and return the top k. The same machinery supports thresholding, snippet highlighting (terms with high query contribution), and diagnostic displays.

TF-IDF is simple to implement, fast to score with inverted indexes, easy to explain, and requires no labeled training data. It remains a strong baseline in many domains. Its limits are familiar: bag-of-words models discard order and syntax; vocabulary mismatch hurts recall when a query says cars but documents say automobile; identical tokens can carry different meanings (Java the island versus the language); and naive IDF can still over-weight rare tokens.

Later rankers and dense embeddings address synonymy, context, and length effects differently, but the term-space vectors defined here remain the reference point for how lexical similarity is computed.
