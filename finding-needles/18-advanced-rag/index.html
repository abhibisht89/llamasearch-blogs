00 · Beyond naive RAG

Naive RAG always fetches a fixed number of chunks, stuffs them into a prompt, and generates. Two problems follow: retrieval is not always necessary (many questions need only parametric knowledge, and redundant retrieval adds latency and cost), and retrieval is not always trustworthy (noisy passages pollute the context and encourage citation-backed hallucination).

Advanced RAG adds decision points at runtime: skip retrieval, validate evidence, fall back to another source, or regenerate when support is weak. These patterns sit one step before fully agentic RAG—interpretable controllers with better precision and cost than always-on retrieval.

Naive RAG

Fixed k chunks, single pass. Fast to ship, brittle under mismatch between query and index.

Advanced RAG

Adaptive retrieval, critique, refinement, optional rerouting. More moving parts, clearer failure modes.

Agentic RAG

Multi-step plans, tools, state. Powerful; needs guardrails, tracing, and evaluation harnesses.

01 · Self-RAG

Self-RAG trains or prompts the model to emit critique tokens—[Retrieve], [IsRelevant], [IsSupportive], [IsUseful]—that structure its own use of retrieval. At inference, the model decides whether to fetch, scores passages against the draft answer, and can regenerate when support is missing.

Retrieval becomes on-demand rather than always-on. A typical trace: emit [Retrieve], ingest top passages, label each with [IsRelevant], produce an answer segment, mark whether evidence is [IsSupportive], and retry retrieval or revise the answer when support fails—tightening the coupling between what was retrieved and what was said.

Training and inference

Training uses supervised fine-tuning on trajectories that include critique tokens as targets, sometimes augmented with reward modeling. At inference, parse the stream and branch orchestration: on [Retrieve], call the retriever; on negative [IsSupportive], retry retrieval or roll back part of the completion.

02 · Corrective RAG (CRAG)

    

      Corrective RAG inserts a quality gate after retrieval. A lightweight evaluator (classifier, cross-encoder, or prompted mini-model)
      assigns each batch to CORRECT (use as-is), AMBIGUOUS (refine), or INCORRECT (escalate).
    

    

      On ambiguous verdicts, knowledge refinement keeps only sentences that pass a relevance or entailment filter.
      On incorrect verdicts, web search fallback pulls fresh evidence and reruns the gate. The retriever stays simple;
      a small, testable module owns the decision to trust, compress, or replace evidence.

    03 · Adaptive RAG
    
    
    
      Adaptive RAG classifies questions by complexity or need for external knowledge, then routes to different pipelines:
      direct LLM for simple queries, single-step RAG for focused lookups, multi-step RAG for multi-hop questions.
    
    
    
    
      The router can be a few-shot classifier, fine-tuned encoder, or small LLM judge. A large share of traffic—often
      sixty to seventy percent in production logs—does not benefit from retrieval at all, so adaptive routing cuts cost as well as latency.

    04 · FLARE — forward-looking active retrieval
    
    
    
      FLARE (forward-looking active retrieval) interleaves generation with retrieval based on uncertainty, not a single upfront query.
      When a running confidence estimate drops below a threshold, the system pauses, uses the uncertain continuation as the retrieval query,
      fetches grounding passages, and replaces the low-confidence segment with evidence-backed text.
    
    
    
    
      Compared with Self-RAG’s discrete critique tokens, FLARE uses continuous signals from token probabilities or a small verifier.
      Engineering challenges: latency from many short generations, threshold tuning, and keeping replacements coherent.

    05 · The retriever–reader pattern (extractive QA)
    
    
    
      The classic open-domain QA stack is retriever + reader: a retriever recalls candidate paragraphs; a reader model predicts
      start and end token indices of an answer span. The output is an extractive substring—ideal for verbatim policy text, legal clauses, or log lines.
    
    
    
    
      Modern systems often retrieve broadly, then either extract a span for precision or hand the passage to an LLM for synthesis.
      Extraction is easier to audit; abstraction handles fusion and rephrasing.

    06 · Fine-tuning LLMs for domain-specific QA
    
    
    
      Retrieval quality has diminishing returns if the generator mishandles domain vocabulary, citation norms, or reasoning templates.
      Fine-tuning aligns the model to your task distribution—tone, safety constraints, structured outputs, and the mistakes your eval suite catches.
    
    
    
    
      Error attribution drives the split: if the right passage never surfaces, fix indexing, chunking, and hybrid retrieval first.
      If the right passage is retrieved but the conclusion is wrong, prioritize generator training.
    
    
    
    
      
    When to fine-tune vs fix retrieval
    
      
      
        
    • Tune the generator when phrasing, format, or domain reasoning fails despite good recall.
      • 
        
    • Fix retrieval when recall@k is low, chunks are misaligned, or metadata filters drop relevant docs.
      • 
        
    • Do both iteratively with a frozen eval set so improvements do not chase leaderboard noise.

    07 · Context compression and optimization
    
    
    
      Long retrieved contexts inflate latency and cost. Context compression preserves task-relevant information while shrinking tokens:
      sentence extraction (related to CRAG refinement but optimized for brevity), LLMLingua-style token pruning, and
      selective context reranking within each chunk.
    
    
    
    
      Aggressive pruning can delete negation or qualifiers—pair compressors with regression tests on safety-critical prompts.
      Semantic caching on embedding similarity avoids both retrieval and long prompts for repeated questions.
