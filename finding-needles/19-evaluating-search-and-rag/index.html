00 · Why evaluation is hard

Search quality is subjective. The same ranked list can be excellent for a power user and mediocre for a novice. Context—locale, session history, task stage—shifts what “good” means and shows up as label noise and annotator disagreement.

Offline metrics diverge from real-world satisfaction. High nDCG@10 on a static benchmark does not guarantee good snippets, fresh results, or usable UI. Modest offline scores can still win online if they reduce pogo-sticking or surface safer answers. Metrics summarize ranked lists; they do not encode latency, trust, diversity, or compliance.

Use both tracks: offline evaluation for reproducible regression tests; online A/B tests for adoption, session success, and side effects.

Offline

Judged lists, nDCG, RAGAS-style scores, LLM rubrics. Fast iteration, tight control, risk of overfitting benchmarks.

Online

CTR, dwell, reformulation, session success. Ground truth for behavior, noisy, needs discipline (sample size, peeking).

Hybrid

Gate deploys with offline parity checks; promote winners after online verification. Calibrate offline to online periodically.

01 · Retrieval metrics

Fix a cutoff k. Precision@k is the fraction of the top-k slots that are relevant. Recall@k is the fraction of all relevant documents for the query that appear in the top-k. When the total relevant count is unknown, recall is reported against a pooled judgment set (see §03).

F1@k is the harmonic mean of precision and recall at the same k. Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR) uses 1 / rank of the first relevant hit (0 if none)—ruthless about burying the first good answer. Mean Average Precision (MAP) averages precision at every depth where a relevant document appears, rewarding multiple relevant hits without junk at the top.

Convention in this page. For mixed grades, treat a document as relevant for P/R/F1/MRR/MAP when its grade is ≥ 1 (binary 1 counts; graded 2 or 3 also counts). nDCG uses full grades. Adjust thresholds in your own tooling to match annotation guidelines.

02 · nDCG — graded ranking quality

Normalized DCG (nDCG@k) is DCG@k / IDCG@k, in [0, 1] when gains are non-negative. It rewards highly relevant documents at top ranks with a logarithmic position discount. Graded labels and the gain formula were introduced in Chapter 10 (reranking evaluation) and applied to hybrid benchmarks in Chapter 12.

Pair nDCG with pool-based recall or session metrics when long-tail coverage matters—it does not measure that by itself.

    03 · Judgment lists

    Offline evaluation rests on judgment lists: rows of (query, document_id, relevance) from human annotators or a trusted oracle. Without judgments, “ground truth” is whatever the old system emitted.

    Grading scales

    Teams use binary (relevant vs. not), 4-point (0–3), or 5-point (0–4) scales. Finer scales help nDCG but need clearer guidelines and more adjudication. Document boundary cases with examples.

    Inter-annotator agreement

    Report agreement beyond chance: Cohen’s kappa for two raters; Krippendorff’s alpha for multiple raters and missing data. Low agreement means fix instructions before trusting a leaderboard spread of 0.002 nDCG.

    How many judgments?

    Head queries with one obvious answer need fewer labels than tail queries with multiple valid facets. Start with dozens to hundreds of queries for directional comparisons; grow to thousands when metrics must stabilize across slices.

    Pooling and shared-task methodology

    Pooling unions top results from several systems per query, deduplicates, then judges that pool. Recall is computed relative to the judged pool, not the web at large—the classic TREC workflow: reproducible, but blind to relevant docs outside the pool.

    Quick reference · pooling vs. exhaustive judging

    ApproachProsCons
    Depth-k pool per queryFeasible at scale; comparable across submissionsUnderestimates recall; pool depth matters
    Exhaustive corpus labelsUnbiased recall in principleOften impossible for open web
    Dynamic pool expansionAdds unseen hits when new systems find themMore rounds of judging; logistics

    04 · Golden sets vs. silver sets (and synthetic pairs)

    Golden sets are carefully human-judged evaluation corpora—expensive, authoritative, held out from training and protected from leakage. Silver sets come from clicks or implicit feedback—cheap at scale but carry position bias and presentation bias. Click models like SDBN attempt to debias observed clicks into estimated relevance.

    Synthetic data (LLM-generated Q&A from documents) helps cold start when no logs exist; filter, deduplicate, and verify before trusting rare domains.

    How to build each

    • Golden: sample queries from logs or personas; write annotation guidelines with worked examples; use multiple annotators and adjudication; store rationale for borderline docs; refresh periodically for drift.
    • Silver: aggregate clicks and hovers per query–document; apply click models or pairwise de-biasing; down-weight ambiguous sessions; cross-check a subset against golden labels to estimate noise.
    • Synthetic: prompt an LLM with document chunks to produce questions and answers; enforce constraints (answer must be substring or must cite offsets); run consistency checks; discard toxic or off-policy generations.

    Golden

    Use for evaluation and high-stakes regression gates. Keep pristine; do not tune hyperparameters directly on the primary golden test set.

    Silver

    Use for training rerankers and embeddings at scale. Expect noise; monitor calibration to golden slices.

    Synthetic

    Use for cold start and coverage expansion. Always validate with human spot checks before trusting rare-domain behavior.

    05 · RAG-specific evaluation

    RAG adds generation failure modes: unsupported claims, hedging that ignores evidence, fluent but off-topic answers. Frameworks package scoring for these dimensions—RAGAS (faithfulness, answer relevancy, context precision, context recall), DeepEval (test harnesses), TruLens (trace-attached feedback functions).

    Key RAG metrics (conceptual)

    • Faithfulness: Is the answer entailed by or directly supported by the retrieved context? Penalize added facts.
    • Answer relevancy: Does the answer address the user question without unnecessary tangents?
    • Context relevancy / precision: Is retrieved context on-topic and free of distracting passages?
    • Context recall: Does the context contain the information needed to answer (coverage of necessary facts)?
    • Groundedness: Can each atomic claim be traced to a source span (sometimes checked with NLI or citation overlap)?

    06 · LLM as judge
    
    
    
      An LLM judge scores answers, compares pairs, or checks entailment against context.
      Pairwise (“Which answer is better?”) reduces verbosity sensitivity; pointwise rubrics assign 1–5 with criteria per level.
    
    
    
    
      Pros: cheap at the margin, consistent on repetitive rubrics, easy in CI.
      Cons: position bias, verbosity bias, self-preference when judge and generator share a family.
      Mitigate with order swapping, binary checklists, human calibration sets, and cite-match or NLI checks.
    
    
    
    
      On well-defined tasks with tight rubrics, LLM–human agreement often lands in the roughly 80–90% band—useful for triage, not alone for regulated claims.

    07 · A/B testing for search relevance
    
    
    
      Online evaluation compares a control to a treatment on live traffic.
      Primary metrics: CTR, reformulation rate, dwell time, and session success proxies.
      Search is sequential—session-level summaries can disagree with query-level CTR.
    
    
    
    
      Pre-register sample sizes when possible; compute p-values and confidence intervals.
      Watch for novelty effects, Simpson’s paradox, and latency confounds (treatment gets slower pages).
      A better ranker can lower per-query CTR if it resolves intent faster—pair CTR with success and reformulation signals.
