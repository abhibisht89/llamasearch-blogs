Word Embeddings — From One-Hot to Word2Vec

Sparse bag-of-words and BM25 treat every word type as an isolated symbol. That breaks under vocabulary mismatch. One-hot vectors encode the limitation geometrically; distributional semantics, Word2Vec, GloVe, and FastText learn dense vectors that support similarity, analogy, and softer matching between related terms.

§0 The vocabulary mismatch problem

Lexical matching assumes that relevance is signaled by sharing the same surface forms. In practice, authors and searchers rarely choose identical strings for the same underlying intent—a page about automobile safety may never contain the query term car, yet a human reader would still judge the match as on-topic.

Classical sparse retrieval scores such as TF-IDF and BM25 compare term statistics at the type level. If the query term does not appear in the document, there is often no direct signal from that token at all. Heuristics such as stemming or query expansion help at the margins, but they do not provide a geometry of meaning: sparse representations allocate one dimension per word type, so two distinct types remain unrelated unless explicitly linked by rules or external knowledge.

With one-hot encodings, each word is represented by a vector of length |V| with a single entry equal to one and all others zero. The dot product between any two distinct one-hot vectors is zero, and their cosine similarity is undefined or zero depending on convention. Orthogonality sounds mathematically tidy, but semantically it is catastrophic: every unrelated pair of words is as “dissimilar” as every related pair.

Dense vectors in a comparatively low-dimensional real space—typically tens to hundreds of dimensions—place distance and angle in correlation with semantic and syntactic relatedness. Learning such vectors from text is the program of modern static word embeddings. They do not replace inverted indexes by themselves, but they underpin semantic search, dense retrieval, reranking, and many downstream models that must generalize beyond exact token overlap.

§1 One-hot encoding

Given a finite vocabulary V = {w₁, …, w|V|}, the one-hot representation of word wᵢ is the standard basis vector eᵢ ∈ ℝ|V|. The encoding is exact and injective: no two types collide. It is also extremely sparse: exactly one nonzero entry per vector, regardless of how large the vocabulary grows.

Three structural properties follow immediately. First, sparsity: storing a full vector for every token in a long document scales linearly with |V| if materialized naïvely. Second, high dimensionality: the ambient dimension equals the number of types, which in open-vocabulary settings can reach millions. Third, orthogonality of distinct words makes every pair of different types maximally separated under dot-product similarity.

These vectors carry almost no transferable signal. A model that sees one-hot inputs must allocate separate parameters for each type, so statistics for one word type do not help predictions for a synonym. Frequency statistics help at the document level for BM25, but the representation of the types themselves does not smooth across related terms.

The curse of dimensionality also bites when you treat each word as an isolated corner of a hypercube. In very high dimensions, most probability mass in simple generative stories lies far from any training example unless you impose structure. Dense embeddings impose that structure by sharing dimensions across many words, so co-occurring or substitutable words tug on overlapping coordinates during training.

§2 Distributional semantics

The distributional hypothesis, often summarized by Firth’s dictum that “you shall know a word by the company it keeps,” proposes that meaning is reflected in contextual patterns. Words that appear in interchangeable or overlapping environments should be represented more similarly than words that do not. This is not a claim that context equals full logical meaning, but a practical statistical principle that turns raw corpora into geometry.

A classical construction is the co-occurrence matrix. Fix a window (for example, ±5 tokens). For each center word i and context word j, count how often j appears in the window around i. Row i then looks like a very long, sparse histogram of neighbors. High counts link words that tend to show up together: doctor and hospital, keyboard and typing, and so on.

Raw counts overweight frequent function words. Pointwise mutual information (PMI) rescales counts into association scores. Writing empirical probabilities from the corpus, a common definition is

PMI(i, j) = log₂ P(i, j) / (P(i) · P(j))

Large positive PMI suggests that i and j co-occur more than chance; negative values suggest avoidance. PMI is noisy for rare pairs, so practice often uses shifted positive PMI (PPMI) or adds smoothing. Either way, raw integers become information-theoretic edge weights that emphasize informative neighbors.

The co-occurrence matrix is still wide. Singular value decomposition (SVD) and related factorizations approximate it with a low-rank product, yielding dense rows that serve as word vectors. This pipeline—count, reweight, factorize—is the conceptual ancestor of Word2Vec and GloVe: first summarize global pairwise statistics, then embed them into a small number of latent factors that behave like semantic dimensions.

§3 Word2Vec — continuous bag-of-words (CBOW)

Word2Vec replaces explicit matrix factorization with a shallow neural prediction problem. In the continuous bag-of-words variant, the model reads the multiset of context words within a window and tries to predict the center word. The architecture is deliberately minimal: an embedding lookup for each input token, an aggregation step (typically averaging the context vectors), a linear hidden transformation, and an output layer over the vocabulary.

Parameter tying is what makes the vectors useful. The same embedding matrix maps token ids to vectors whether a word appears as context or as a prediction target (modulo some implementations that split input and output embeddings). During training, errors backpropagate into those shared vectors, nudging co-occurring words toward arrangements that make good predictions. The hidden layer weights therefore encode distributional structure even though the training objective is pure prediction.

If the output uses a full softmax over |V| classes, each step is expensive. Negative sampling approximates the gradient by treating the task as binary classification: distinguish the true center word from a handful of randomly drawn noise words. Noise is typically drawn from a smoothed unigram distribution so frequent tokens are sampled more often as distractors, which empirically stabilizes training.

The window size sets how far context stretches along the sentence. Small windows emphasize syntactic substitution—words that fit the same local slot. Larger windows pull in more topical associates. There is no universal best value; it is a bias knob that trades local structure against broader co-occurrence.

CBOW objective (informal)
Maximize P(center | context₁, …, contextk) by averaging context embeddings and comparing against the true center token.
Why averaging?
Order inside the window is often discarded for simplicity. The bag-of-contexts assumption keeps the model fast and still captures strong distributional signal for many tasks.

§4 Word2Vec — skip-gram

The skip-gram model flips the prediction direction: given a center word, it asks the network to predict each context word. The objective is to maximize P(context | center) across pairs drawn from windows. This inversion is not mathematically equivalent to CBOW, and in practice skip-gram often performs better for rare words because each rare center token generates multiple training pairs when it appears, whereas CBOW collapses its contexts into a single averaged prediction problem.

Architecturally, you still embed the center word, map through a hidden layer, and score candidate context words. The comparison to CBOW is therefore about what is conditioned on what, not about deep hierarchy. Both models are shallow; depth enters later in contextual models such as ELMo and transformers, which produce token vectors that depend on the entire sentence.

Frequent tokens like the create noise: they co-occur with almost everything and dominate gradient counts. Subsampling stochastically drops high-frequency words during pair generation, preserving training time and reducing the pressure to waste dimensions explaining trivial predictability. The exact rule varies by implementation, but the intent is always to stop common function words from swamping the signal.

CBOW
Many context vectors → predict one center. Noise averaging can help for frequent words; rare centers see fewer updates per appearance.
Skip-gram
One center → predict many contexts. Rare words enjoy more training signal per occurrence; computation per sentence can be higher.

§5 GloVe — global vectors

Word2Vec scans corpora locally through sliding windows, which is efficient and streaming-friendly. GloVe (Global Vectors) instead foregrounds the global co-occurrence matrix X, where Xij stores how often word j appears in the context of word i. Rather than factorizing X with SVD directly, GloVe defines a weighted least-squares objective that approximates log co-occurrence counts with bilinear dot products of learned vectors plus scalar biases.

Let wᵢ and w̃ⱼ be word and context vectors (the final representation often uses their sum or just the word vector, depending on usage). The objective encourages

J = Σi,j f(Xij) ( wᵢ · w̃ⱼ + bᵢ + b̃ⱼ − log Xij )²

The weighting function f down-weights rare entries so noise does not dominate, and it caps very large counts so frequent pairs do not overwhelm the loss. A typical choice is piecewise: zero below a cutoff, power-law growth in the middle, and a flat maximum for large x. The details matter for optimization, but the conceptual role is always to balance dynamic range in the co-occurrence statistics.

GloVe versus Word2Vec: both learn vector spaces from co-occurrence structure, but GloVe makes the global matrix explicit in the loss, while skip-gram with negative sampling can be interpreted as implicit factorization of shifted PMI matrices. In deployment, quality differences are often smaller than differences in training data, dimensionality, and tokenization. Both share the same backbone—co-occurrence log counts approximated by dot products of low-dimensional vectors—expressed either through global least squares or local softmax classification. If two words share similar rows in the co-occurrence matrix, their dot products with third words must look alike; the least-squares objective compresses those long rows into parallel directions in embedding space.

§6 FastText — subword embeddings

Standard Word2Vec assigns an independent vector to every type in the training vocabulary. At deployment time, any out-of-vocabulary (OOV) string— a rare surname, a product code, a typo—has no embedding at all unless you map it to <UNK> or invent a fallback. That limitation is painful for web text, morphologically rich languages with long word forms, and noisy queries.

FastText addresses the gap by representing a word as the sum (or average) of vectors for its character n-grams, including boundary symbols to distinguish prefixes and suffixes, plus optionally the whole-word token itself. The model learns embeddings for those subword units during the same skip-gram-style training as before, but scoring breaks a word into many pieces.

For example, with suitable bounds and minimum n, the word playing might contribute <pl, pla, lay, ayi, yin, ing, ng>, and longer grams such as play and aying. Rare inflections still overlap heavily in subwords with frequent lemmas, so their composed vectors inherit partial meaning. Typos that preserve many n-grams remain close to the intended word in cosine distance.

The trade-off is memory and computation: many more parameters are active per token, and very short words can be dominated by boundary artifacts if hyperparameters are poorly chosen. Still, for open-vocabulary retrieval and classification, subword models remain a pragmatic default alongside byte-pair and sentence-piece tokenizations used in transformers.

§7 Embedding arithmetic and properties

Well-trained word vectors arrange linear structure that is not obvious from the training objective alone. The famous example king − man + woman ≈ queen is not an identity in any formal sense, but a robust empirical pattern: vector differences aligned with gender in one pair transfer when added to another word whose gendered component points similarly. The relationship holds approximately in cosine neighborhoods, not as exact equality.

Cosine similarity is the standard similarity measure for embeddings because vector length often encodes frequency or training artifacts more than semantics. Cosine focuses on direction: cos(θ) = (u·v) / (‖u‖‖v‖). Nearest-neighbor search in embedding space—whether exact or approximate—typically uses cosine or Euclidean distance after normalization.

Clustering and visualization (t-SNE, UMAP) reveal topical islands: countries near capitals, verbs near their objects, hypernyms near hyponyms in coarse structure. These views are exploratory only: distances in projected 2D plots distort global geometry, so they should never be read as quantitative ground truth.

Static embeddings assign one vector per type. The word bank shares the same representation for river edges and financial institutions until contextual models condition on surrounding tokens. Dense retrieval today often uses sentence or document encoders for exactly this reason.

Modern systems often hybridize—retrieve candidates with an inverted index, then rescore with dense vectors—or embed queries and passages jointly with a trained bi-encoder.
