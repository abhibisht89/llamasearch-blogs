00 · Three Disjoint Spaces

Production search systems almost always maintain several parallel views of the same corpus. Each view optimizes a different notion of relevance, and each view lives in its own geometry: sparse term statistics, dense neural geometry, and implicit user behavior. Together they describe “what matches,” “what means the same thing,” and “what people actually use together”—but until recently there was no principled way to let one view inform another except rank fusion at the very end.

Three representation families

  • Lexical space — BM25, inverted indexes, bag-of-words style sparse vectors. Terms are dimensions; most entries are zero. Local overlap drives scoring.
  • Semantic space — dense embeddings from encoders (bi-encoders, sentence transformers). Meaning is continuous; paraphrases and synonyms cluster even without shared tokens.
  • Behavioral space — co-clicks, co-purchases, session sequences. Similarity is “what users treated as related,” which can diverge sharply from textual or conceptual similarity.

Each space captures a facet the others miss. Lexical retrieval is interpretable and exact on rare tokens; semantic retrieval generalizes across wording; behavioral signals encode latent intent and complementarity (for example, accessories bought with a device). The difficulty is composition: how do you let a dense query vector “talk to” a sparse retriever, or let click patterns refine a text-only index, without smashing everything into one late list with RRF (Reciprocal Rank Fusion) and accepting the information loss that entails?

01 · The Semantic Knowledge Graph (SKG)

A Semantic Knowledge Graph (SKG) is a graph whose nodes are linguistic or conceptual units drawn from your corpus and query stream—terms, concepts, entities—and whose edges encode statistical relatedness in context, not hand-curated ontologies. Unlike a traditional knowledge graph built by editors, an SKG is induced from co-occurrence and conditional statistics, so it reflects domain vocabulary and user language as they actually appear in your system.

Edges carry relatedness scores derived from comparing how often two nodes appear together in a foreground context versus how often you would expect them together under background corpus statistics. Large positive scores indicate association stronger than baseline; scores near zero suggest independence; structured penalties can down-weight generic pairs dominated by stopwords or global frequency effects.

edge_weight(A, B) ≈ log( P(B | A in foreground) / P(B | background) )

In spirit this is pointwise mutual information (PMI) “on steroids”: you are not only asking whether A and B co-occur, but whether they co-occur more than their marginal rates predict in the specific slices of data you care about (documents, query reformulations, clicked results). The same machinery applies whether the foreground is “paragraphs containing entity A,” “sessions where product A was viewed,” or “embedding neighborhoods around centroid A.”

Where the graph comes from. You can build SKG edges from document text (sliding windows, passages), from anonymized query logs (query-term and query-document co-clicks), or from hybrid corpora. The graph is your statistical portrait of language and behavior in the product—not a static external taxonomy.

02 · Foreground / Background Analysis

The workhorse behind SKG edges is foreground / background analysis. Fix a context C—for example, all passages where term A appears, or all sessions where a user engaged with item A. The foreground distribution tells you how often term B appears near A inside C. The background distribution is the unconditional (or globally conditioned) frequency of B in the corpus or log stream.

ratio(B | A) = P(B | A, foreground) / P(B | background)

When the ratio is high, B is discriminating for A in that context: it shows up alongside A far more often than base rate suggests. When the ratio is near 1, B is generic with respect to A—think function words or universally common attributes. When the ratio is very low, B is anti-associated with the foreground (rare but informative for filtering in some designs).

Example: java in a tech foreground

spring, kotlin, heap rise in foreground probability. coffee may stay near background rate if the corpus is mostly programming docs.

Same token, café foreground

espresso, latte, roast dominate. Programming neighbors fall toward background—context slice picks the “meaning.”

Foreground/background ratios surface domain-specific relationships automatically—conditional structure from data, thresholded and smoothed like any statistical model. The SKG makes that structure navigable as a graph.

03 · Wormhole Vectors — The Core Idea

A wormhole vector is a cross-space procedure: start from a vector in one representation (for example, a dense query embedding), use the SKG to extract the most discriminating surface forms—terms, entities, item IDs—for that neighborhood, then enter another space with those symbols as coordinates or query expansions.

Procedural sketch

  1. Start from a vector in space S₁—for example, a dense query embedding.
  2. Retrieve k nearest neighbors in S₁ (approximate nearest neighbors in a vector index).
  3. Map those neighbors to their textual or catalog labels (passage spans, product titles, entity strings).
  4. Run foreground/background scoring (or read weights from a precomputed SKG) to rank terms that discriminate this neighborhood from the corpus.
  5. Use the top terms as a wormhole exit in lexical space—BM25 query expansion, boolean filters, or sparse vector re-encoding.

The reverse direction is symmetric in principle: begin from lexical matches or behavioral item vectors, expand through the graph into terms or entities that co-occur in the right foreground, then inject those as inputs to a dense encoder or as keys into a semantic index. Behavioral activations can traverse the same bridge when sessions are mapped to nodes (products, queries) that participate in the SKG.

Bridge before merge. A semantic neighborhood becomes a structured lexical hypothesis—not just another ranked list to fuse.

04 · Behavioral Embeddings

Word2Vec and related shallow embedding algorithms do not care that their symbols are “words.” They care that you provide a sequence of discrete IDs and a notion of co-occurrence within a window. If you replace tokens with product SKUs, video IDs, or playlist items, the same training objective learns a space where items that tend to appear in similar contexts—here, user sessions—receive similar vectors.

Concretely, treat each user session (or click stream) as a sentence: [product_1, product_2, product_3, …]. Training skip-gram or CBOW on these sequences yields behavioral embeddings: vectors aligned with substitutability and co-interest in behavior, not with textual description. A phone case and a screen protector may be far apart in a text encoder’s space if their descriptions share few words, yet behaviorally adjacent because buyers pair them.

Content similarity

Driven by shared text, specs, categories. Strong for SEO-style matching, weak on accessories and “goes with” patterns.

Behavioral similarity

Driven by co-clicks, co-purchases, shared sessions. Captures latent intent bundles retail taxonomies omit.

Connecting behavioral space to the SKG follows the same pattern as semantic↔lexical: map embedding neighbors to catalog nodes, read off high-PMI edges to terms and entities, and use those edges as bridge exits into sparse retrieval or content features for recommendation. With thin behavior, semantic neighborhoods still anchor cold start; with rich logs, behavioral vectors tighten foreground definitions.

05 · Pooling and Manipulating Semantic Meaning

Many retrieval pipelines need a single vector from a set of vectors: multiple query reformulations, multiple passage chunks, multiple clicked items in a session, or hybrid signals from more than one encoder. Pooling is how you collapse that set to one point (or a small basis) before nearest-neighbor search or re-ranking.

Mean pooling—take the elementwise average of vectors—is simple, differentiable, and often surprisingly strong when vectors live in the same space and are normalized consistently. Weighted pooling assigns coefficients by importance: inverse document frequency (IDF), attention from a cross-model, or foreground/background scores from the SKG so that discriminating terms (or items) pull the centroid more than generic ones.

v_pool = Σᵢ wᵢ vᵢ / Σᵢ wᵢ    (mean pooling: wᵢ = 1)

The same bridge pattern applies to pooling across heterogeneous sources: average dense semantic vectors with behavioral vectors after aligning them through shared graph nodes, or pool in embedding space while weighting neighbors by SKG foreground scores.

06 · Practical Applications

Query expansion via wormhole. Encode the query as a dense vector; retrieve neighbors; project through the SKG to lexical terms with high foreground/background ratios; feed those terms into BM25 or a learned sparse model. You get expansions that are contextual to the embedding neighborhood, not static synonym lists.

Personalized search. Maintain a short-term behavioral embedding from recent clicks; traverse the SKG to content terms or entity nodes; bias lexical or dense retrieval toward inventory that matches both stated query and latent session intent.

Cross-modal recommendations. Map textual descriptions through the SKG to item IDs that behave like bridge nodes; project behavioral neighbors back into textual explanations for transparency (“users who clicked X also engaged with Y”).

Cold start. When behavioral data is thin, semantic neighborhoods still produce SKG foregrounds from content co-occurrence; wormhole exits give sparse retrieval something concrete until click volume grows.

Retrieval-augmented generation stacks often emphasize textual match over what similar users found useful. Doug Turnbull has summarized a common gap as: “RAG’s big blind spot is ignoring behavioral signals.” Wormhole-style bridges are one architectural response—let behavior, text, and embeddings meet in the graph, not only in the final prompt assembly.

Operational checklist

  • Define foreground slices (passage windows, sessions, query reformulation chains) consistently with privacy policy.
  • Smooth and cap edge weights; filter stopwords and globally dominant nodes.
  • Version the SKG with your index and embedding model so wormhole exits stay calibrated.
  • Evaluate with offline ranking metrics and online outcomes—behavioral bridges optimize for different surfaces than pure nDCG on editorial labels.
