DeepSeek-R1: Incentivizing Reasoning via RL

How DeepSeek-R1 trains large language models to produce extended, verifiable reasoning using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), rule-based rewards, and a multi-stage recipe—often without imitating human chain-of-thought traces at scale—and how distillation transfers that behavior to smaller models.

Language models as policies

An autoregressive language model maps a context (prompt plus tokens generated so far) to a distribution over the next token. Sampling repeatedly yields a full response. In reinforcement-learning terms, the context is the state, choosing the next token is an action, and the model’s next-token distribution is the policy πθ.

Standard pre-training maximizes likelihood on large text corpora; supervised fine-tuning nudges the model toward demonstrations. Reinforcement learning instead optimizes expected return under a reward signal: the model is encouraged to repeat actions that led to high reward and discouraged from actions tied to low reward—without necessarily providing a single gold transcript for every problem.

at ∼ πθ(· | st)   trajectory τ = (s0, a0, s1, a1, …)

For long generations, the reward is often sparse: you only observe a score after the full answer (for example, “unit tests pass” or “numeric result matches”). Credit for intermediate tokens is propagated through advantage estimates in the policy-gradient objective, so early choices that tend to precede success receive positive pressure.

Using the grid-world framework from Chapter 15, the same RL vocabulary applies to text: state = prompt plus generated prefix, action = next token, policy = the model’s next-token distribution. Autoregressive decoding repeats that step until you stop.

Typical training stacks three ideas: pre-training on broad corpora (web-scale text, encyclopedic material, forums, code, and the like) so the model learns structure across languages; supervised fine-tuning to raise the likelihood of higher-quality completions; and alignment so the model follows instructions and social norms (helpfulness, format, safety). Alignment often uses reinforcement-style objectives—historically algorithms in the PPO family, later approaches such as DPO, and for DeepSeek-R1, GRPO.

Variance and search size. The space of token sequences is astronomical: with a vocabulary on the order of tens of thousands of tokens (think roughly 30,000 choices per position), enumerating all completions for a prompt is intractable. Policy gradients therefore use Monte Carlo sampling: a few trajectories per update, not all paths. That injects high variance. Baselines and advantage shaping—which you will see in GRPO as group-relative scores—are ways to stabilize learning without visiting every possible generation. Sparse end-of-answer reward still shapes each token because the advantage attaches to the full trajectory: optimization pushes up log-probability along high-reward completions and down along poor ones.

01

DeepSeek-R1 in one pass

The DeepSeek-R1 line studies reasoning: decomposing hard tasks into steps, revising, and producing an answer that can be checked. The work highlights two closely related systems:

  • DeepSeek-R1-Zero applies large-scale RL starting from a strong base model (DeepSeek-V3 family) without a supervised reasoning cold-start stage. The model is pushed only by reward signals.
  • DeepSeek-R1 adds a small amount of high-quality cold-start data (curated chain-of-thought–style examples) before RL, improving readability and stability, then continues with RL and further alignment stages.

Both settings rely on GRPO and predominantly rule-based rewards (verifiable correctness and formatting) rather than a separate neural reward model trained on human preferences—though the general RL-from-feedback picture (policy, samples, advantages, KL control) is the same.

The DeepSeek-R1 line builds on a strong base in the DeepSeek-V3 family. Walkthroughs of the paper often cite the flagship base as on the order of 600 billion parameters (always cross-check the official model card for the exact figure). DeepSeek-R1-Zero is the pure-RL path: start from that base and optimize with GRPO and verifiable rewards without a supervised reasoning cold-start. DeepSeek-R1 interleaves a small amount of high-quality supervised data and further stages so the model reads cleanly and follows instructions, not only scores well on checks.

The paper opens the pure-RL setting with this line of thought: “In this section we explore the potential of language models to develop reasoning capabilities without any supervised data, focusing on their self-evolution through a pure reinforcement learning process.” “No supervised data” here means no curated chain-of-thought dataset driving the first reasoning stage—the model still rests on pretrained weights. The incentive is entirely the reward structure: the model must discover internal deliberation that earns points.

Benchmark tables. For exact pass@k, AIME, code, and math suite numbers, read the paper’s evaluation section and any updated release notes—this chapter focuses on mechanism, not a duplicate table that would go stale.

Pure RL on reasoning differs from supervised fine-tuning: instead of copying a gold trace, the optimizer shifts probability toward completions that score high under a verifier and away from those that do not—even when no human wrote the ideal scratch work.

Compared with other reasoning stacks

Industrial “reasoning” models (for example OpenAI’s o-series) also combine large base capability, RL or search, and careful data—but exact recipes differ. DeepSeek-R1 emphasizes group-based relative advantages, cheap verifiable rewards, and a path where extended thinking emerges from incentives rather than from millions of human-written reasoning traces.

Why RL here?

Supervised fine-tuning says “match this trace.” RL says “shift probability toward behaviors that earn reward and away from behaviors that do not.” That distinction matters when the best internal deliberation is unknown but outcomes (passing checks) are known.

02

GRPO: Group Relative Policy Optimization

Classic PPO-style methods often train a critic or value network to estimate baselines and advantages. GRPO avoids that extra model by reusing multiple completions from the same prompt as a comparison group.

For each question, sample G outputs {o₁,…,oG} from the current policy (or a frozen snapshot for off-policy reuse). A rule-based or verifier assigns a scalar reward ri to each completion. Define group mean and standard deviation:

μG = (1/G) Σi ri   σG = sqrt( (1/G) Σi (ri − μG)² + ε )

The group-relative advantage for completion i is the z-score of its reward within the batch for that prompt—so optimization depends on relative quality, not the raw scale of rewards:

Âi = (ri − μG) / σG

The training objective resembles clipped policy optimization: increase log-probability of tokens in completions with positive Âi, decrease those with negative Âi, with a ratio clip between old and new policy (often written with πθ / πold) to limit overly aggressive updates, plus a KL penalty toward a reference policy πref (the initial base model) to reduce collapse and reward hacking.

J ≈ E[ clip( πθ/πold, 1−ε, 1+ε ) · Â ] − β · DKL( πθ ‖ πref )

Intuition. If one sampled answer passes all tests and three fail, the winner gets a positive advantage without needing a learned value function to say the state was “promising” halfway through—the group supplies the baseline.

From expected reward to the GRPO surrogate

Reinforcement learning selects policy parameters θ so that trajectories sampled from the policy yield high expected return. Policy-gradient methods sample trajectories, observe rewards, and nudge θ so actions that led to better outcomes become more probable—the gradient of the objective points in the direction that increases expected reward.

For a fixed question, one completion oi is a sequence of tokens. The policy assigns a conditional distribution at each position; with sampling (greedy, top-p, or otherwise), you obtain a trajectory. The probability of the full string under the policy is the product of per-step conditional probabilities; in log space that is a sum of log-probabilities over tokens. Training signals are applied to that entire sum (weighted by advantages), which is how terminal reward shapes early tokens.

Draw a question q from the task bank. Sample G completions {o₁,…,oG} from the policy (the same prompt, different stochastic draws). Score each completion with a scalar reward ri from your verifier (rule-based in R1: tests pass, answer matches, format satisfied, etc.).

Compute the mean reward of the group and a stabilized standard deviation (with a small constant ε to avoid division by zero when all rewards are identical):

μG = (1/G) Σi=1G ri
σG = √( (1/G) Σi=1G (ri − μG)² + ε )

Define the advantage of completion i as the z-score within the group:

Âi = (ri − μG) / σG

After this normalization, the list {Âi} has mean approximately 0 and scale comparable to a unit standard deviation across the group. What drives learning is not the raw magnitude of r but how much better or worse completion i was than its siblings on the same prompt. Two prompts with different reward scales therefore contribute comparably shaped updates—only relative quality within each group matters for the advantage sign and size.

Let πθ be the policy you are updating and πold the policy that produced the stored completions (off-policy reuse). For each token t in completion i, form the ratio

ρi,t(θ) = πθ(ai,t | si,t) / πold(ai,t | si,t)

If ρ > 1, the new policy has increased mass on the taken action; if ρ < 1, it has decreased mass. Multiplying by Âi tells you whether that change aligns with “this completion was good” (positive advantage) or “this completion was bad” (negative advantage).

To prevent huge policy jumps when the model becomes overconfident, PPO-style methods clip ρ. Writing Li,t = log πθ(ai,t|si,t) − log πold(ai,t|si,t), so ρ = exp(L), the surrogate uses min(ρ Âi, clip(ρ, 1−ε, 1+ε) Âi) aggregated over tokens and completions. Even when Âi is strongly positive, ρ cannot run away without bound in one update—training stays stable.

Add −β · DKL(πθ ‖ πref) with πref the initial base model (e.g. pretrained DeepSeek-V3 before this RL stage). Without it, the policy can reward hack: imagine a politeness reward—an unconstrained model might spam “thank you” forever because that correlates with approval, abandoning factual answers. KL keeps the tuned policy close enough to a coherent LM that it still “behaves like a language model” while shifting mass toward verifiably good reasoning.

Classical PPO with human-feedback-style training often learns a separate value network to estimate expected return from each state and to compute advantages (e.g. via GAE). GRPO removes that head: the group of completions supplies a baseline and spread, so advantages come straight from {ri} after normalization. The group mean μG acts as a per-prompt baseline—you learn from being better or worse than the other samples drawn for the same question. With rule-based rewards, there is no need to train another transformer to imitate human scores—engineering effort moves into verifiers and curricula.

Concrete group example (same prompt)

Prompt: “Where is Shanghai?” Suppose three samples: (1) “Shanghai is in China” — high reward; (2) “The sky is blue” — essentially zero reward (irrelevant); (3) “Shanghai is beautiful” — nearly zero (mentions Shanghai but does not answer the question). The group mean sits between high and low; the good answer’s advantage is positive; nonsense answers pull the mean down and receive negative advantages. The optimizer increases probability mass on trajectories like (1).

Advantage at the token level

Ask whether, after “Where is Shanghai?”, the first token should be “Shanghai” versus “Pizza” versus “Coffee.” Completing with “Shanghai …” tends to continue toward answers that earn reward (“… is in China”); a random first token often derails the completion. The shared advantage Âi for that completion assigns credit to every token along the path—so the first token “Shanghai” receives pressure consistent with the final judgment, even though no per-step process reward exists.

Off-policy reuse. Pure on-policy training would require regenerating fresh completions after every small weight change—expensive for large models. In practice you generate a large batch of responses once, store them with their log-probs under πold, and run several optimization steps on minibatches drawn from that pool before resampling. The ratio ρ corrects for the mismatch between the behavior policy and the updated policy.

03

Rule-based rewards for reasoning

Neural reward models (Bradley–Terry style on human preferences) are flexible but can be gamed and costly to maintain. DeepSeek-R1 leans on rewards you can compute with programs and parsers:

  • Math and logic: extract a boxed or final numeric answer; compare to ground truth (exact match or structured check).
  • Code: compile, run against hidden tests, enforce time limits—binary or graded success.
  • Format: reward wrapping scratch work in delimiters (for example <think>…</think>) so reasoning stays structured and parseable for logging or hybrid pipelines.

Rewards are predominantly outcome-based: the model earns signal only after producing a full candidate solution. Intermediate “process” rewards (scoring each reasoning sentence) were explored in the literature, but are brittle—local steps can look wrong yet be necessary for a correct proof.

Historically, preference-based RLHF trains a neural reward model: same backbone as the policy with a scalar head that outputs one score per completion. Preferences from annotators are turned into a training signal—often via a Bradley–Terry–style ranking loss—so chosen answers get higher scores than rejected ones. PPO then uses that scalar at the end of each sample; the advantage machinery spreads that signal back across tokens. DeepSeek-R1 largely skips learning a reward LM for math and code and instead uses rules you can trust: compile, run hidden tests within a time limit, compare final numeric or boxed answers to ground truth.

Outcome-based reward

The model must emit a full solution (e.g. final program or answer). Only then does the environment return a score. The advantage for the whole trajectory tells earlier tokens whether they were on a path that ended well—there is no separate judge for each sentence.

Process reward model (PRM)

An alternative assigns scores to each step or subgoal. That requires decomposing tasks into substeps—which differs per problem—and judging whether an intermediate line is “good,” which is often ambiguous. In a long proof, a line may look odd yet be essential; withholding reward until the end avoids mislabeling such steps.

Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) over partial solutions explores a tree where intermediate nodes correspond to partial progress; nodes that tend to lead to success get explored more. A typo at the root of a program means no successful compile—entire subtrees can be pruned. MCTS can be powerful but adds heavy engineering; in the R1 setting, large-scale RL with verifiable outcome rewards and GRPO can reach stronger results than some MCTS-heavy pipelines, while letting the model discover its own decomposition and scratch-work format rather than having humans prescribe step templates.

The design philosophy aligns with this idea: rather than explicitly teaching the model how to divide a problem into subproblems, which format to use, or how to solve each piece, the model learns the right format and chain of thought from incentives—subject to the reward specification (accuracy, structure tags, etc.). You still shape behavior by choosing what the verifier checks.

Formatting rewards matter: the system can require scratch work inside delimiters such as <think> … </think> so logs stay parseable and hybrid pipelines can strip or analyze reasoning blocks separately from the final answer.

04

Multi-stage training pipeline

Starting from a pretrained base, the full DeepSeek-R1 recipe interleaves supervised and RL phases: a small cold-start SFT pass makes early RL stable; large-scale GRPO improves reasoning; additional SFT on accepted trajectories (rejection sampling from the improved policy) restores instruction-following and tone; a final RL pass aligns broad usage; distillation then copies behavior into smaller dense models.

The high-level pattern emphasized alongside the paper is alternation: instead of “one RL stage and stop,” the strongest variant adds a little high-quality supervised fine-tuning, then more RL, then another fine-tuning round, then RL again. Each SFT phase can stabilize language, format, and non-reasoning skills; each RL phase pushes verifiable reasoning and alignment under reward. Distillation is the capstone for efficient deployment: smaller models inherit both style and competence from the teacher’s generations (and optionally its per-token distributions).

Four conceptual beats (repeatable recipe). (1) Strong base LM. (2) Optional cold-start SFT on a small set of curated reasoning demonstrations—omitted for R1-Zero, used for R1. (3) Large-scale reasoning RL with GRPO and rule-based rewards. (4) Rejection sampling + SFT on accepted trajectories, then a final RL pass for helpfulness and safety, and finally distillation to student sizes. The exact data mix and iteration counts are engineering choices; the structure is the reusable blueprint.

05

Emergent chain-of-thought and caveats

Under outcome-only rewards, DeepSeek-R1-Zero exhibits a striking pattern: average response length grows during training as the model discovers that longer, explicit deliberation correlates with higher success on verifiable tasks. The model was not given millions of human chain-of-thought documents; the behavior is emergent from the incentive structure.

RL-only training can also surface side effects: for example mixed languages inside scratch work if the reward never penalizes non-English deliberation for an English prompt. Mitigations include cold-start data, broader RL objectives, and post-training that enforces style.

A useful analogy: you pose a math question in English to someone who is bilingual. In their head they may blend English and another language while reasoning, then still produce a correct solution. Unless you explicitly require “think only in English,” there is no pressure against that habit. If the reward checks only correctness and format—not the language of hidden deliberation—the model may freely mix languages whenever it helps it reach a passing answer. That is not caprice; it follows from the incentive structure.

The authors contrast outcome rewards with process reward models (per-step scores) and heavy search such as Monte Carlo Tree Search over partial solutions. Those approaches add engineering complexity and can underperform a well-tuned RL recipe that lets the model discover its own intermediate structure.

Compute reality. Each RL step still runs gradient descent on a massive transformer; generations are sampled at scale per prompt. Expect large GPU clusters for the full recipe—the method is not a small fine-tune on a laptop.

06

Distillation to smaller models

Distillation transfers capabilities from a large teacher to a smaller student. A common recipe: sample prompts, have the teacher generate completions (often including long reasoning), then train the student with standard next-token prediction on those strings—optionally with logit-level distillation so the student matches the teacher’s full softmax at each position, not only the single argmax token. That richer target tightens the student’s distribution and speeds convergence on hard reasoning behaviors that are rare under naive self-training.

DeepSeek-R1 reports that strong small models distilled from R1 can outperform models trained only on large generic corpora for reasoning benchmarks—capacity still matters, but the target distribution encodes how to allocate tokens to intermediate work.

Picture learning graduate mathematics alone versus with a professor. Teaching yourself, you must discover study strategies and heuristics from scratch. A professor supplies not only facts but hints about how to learn faster. Distillation is analogous: the large model is the “professor” that already knows how to allocate a long reasoning trace; the small model learns from demonstrations that embed those strategies instead of rediscovering them from raw web text alone.

Standard LM pre-training is self-supervised next-token prediction: mask nothing, slide a window, predict the next token from context. Example sentence fragment: “for distilled model we report representative results …” Given the prefix “for,” the target might be “distilled”; given “for distilled,” the target might be “model.” Transformers can score the whole sequence in parallel, but the training objective is still per-position cross-entropy toward the tokens that appear in the corpus—often a nearly one-hot target (probability 100% on the true next token, 0 on others at that position) when training on discrete text.

A small model trained only on such raw data faces a harder search problem than a large one: fewer parameters mean less flexibility, so it is a slower learner on rare reasoning patterns. Distillation fixes this by feeding teacher outputs and, in the stronger variant, the teacher’s full probability vector at each step. Concretely, for “Where is Shanghai?” after the prompt, the teacher might assign about 0.6 to the first token “Shanghai,” 0.1 to “Pizza,” 0.05 to improbable continuations, and so on—probabilities that sum to 1 over the vocabulary. After “Shanghai,” the next distribution might put roughly 70% on “is” and very little mass on tokens that break grammar. The student is trained to match that entire vector, not only to imitate the argmax. The extra supervision signals what the student should do, could do, and should avoid at every position—stronger than a single hard label.

Distillation in this spirit applies across all generated positions: for each forward pass, align student logits to teacher logits (or minimize KL between the two distributions) over the sequence, not only on the final answer token.
