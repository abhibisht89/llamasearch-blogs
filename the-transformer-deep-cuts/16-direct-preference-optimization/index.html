DPO: Preference Alignment Without RL

Direct Preference Optimization aligns a language model from pairwise human choices by optimizing a closed-form loss—no separate reward model training and no policy-gradient RL loop. The method, introduced by Rafailov et al. (2023), rewrites the KL-regularized reward objective so the Bradley–Terry preference structure can be trained directly on log-probabilities from πθ and a frozen πref, with the partition function Z(x) canceling inside each pair.

Section 00

Language models as distributions

A language model assigns a probability distribution over the next token (or piece) given everything so far. Conditioning on a prompt x, it specifies conditional probabilities for each candidate continuation. Autoregressive generation repeats: append a sampled or argmax token, feed the extended prefix back in, and stop at an end-of-sequence symbol or a length limit.

For intuition, think of “token” and “word” interchangeably when reasoning about structure; real systems use subword tokenization, but the probabilistic story is the same.

Concretely, take a prompt such as “Shanghai is a city in” (or the common question form “Where is Shanghai?”). The model’s only job at each step is to assign probabilities to candidate next pieces—whether the next token might be China, Beijing, cat, pizza, or anything else in the vocabulary—conditioned on everything to the left.

Text generation is iterative: feed the prompt, read off a distribution over the next token, then either take the argmax (highest score) or sample. Append that token to the context and query the model again. Repeat until you hit an end-of-sequence symbol or a token budget. After a handful of steps—say, four generated tokens—the same machinery can produce a full answer such as “Shanghai is in China,” which completes the original question in a way humans recognize as correct.

Section 01

The alignment problem

Pre-training on large text corpora teaches broad fluency and knowledge; it does not, by itself, guarantee politeness, safety, or assistant-style behavior. A model trained on open web text can reflect undesirable patterns in the data. Alignment means steering the model toward behaviors and norms we want—helpful, respectful answers—while preserving competence.

  • We care about how the model acts across many prompts, not just single-token accuracy.
  • Human values are easier to express as comparisons (“A is better than B”) than as absolute scalar scores everyone agrees on.

Pre-training ingests enormous text—think on the order of thousands of books, billions of web pages, and corpora such as Wikipedia end to end. That scale yields broad linguistic competence and factual association, but it does not, by itself, teach a single agreed notion of “politeness,” safety, or assistant-style behavior. A model trained mainly on open web text can mirror undesirable patterns present in the data; alignment is the deliberate project of steering behavior toward norms we want (helpful, non-offensive, non-racist, and so on) without throwing away what pre-training already learned.

Section 02

Policies, rewards, and preferences

In sequential decision making, a policy maps states to distributions over actions. A language model is analogous: the “state” is the prompt (and generated prefix), and the “action” is the next token. A reward model scores full responses so that “good” assistant behavior gets high reward and unwanted behavior gets low or negative reward. The training goal is to increase the expected reward of completions while not collapsing into degenerate hacks (e.g., repeating “thank you” forever if that alone scores well).

Absolute ratings for a single answer are noisy and subjective: one annotator may prefer a terse reply, another a verbose one. Pairwise preferences—choose yw over yl given prompt x—are often more reliable. A dataset is a collection of triples (x, yw, yl) where the subscripts mean “winning” and “losing” (chosen vs. rejected).

The difficulty with absolute scores shows up even on unambiguous facts. For “Where is Shanghai?” and an answer “Shanghai is a city in China,” one annotator may rate it highly for being short and on point; another may argue that the single word “China” suffices because the question did not ask for a full sentence; a third may want a longer, assistant-style reply that opens with a greeting and restates the question. Humans often fail to agree on a single numeric reward for one completion, yet they remain good at comparing two completions side by side. That is why practice moved toward preference data: generate several candidates—often by sampling from the LM at higher temperature to diversify—and ask annotators which answer they prefer under instructions (correctness, tone, helpfulness).

Reinforcement learning as the organizing metaphor

In classical reinforcement learning, an agent takes actions in an environment, receives rewards, and transitions between states. The policy π(a | s) gives the probability of each action in each state. The objective is to choose (or learn) a policy that maximizes expected return—the reward accumulated along trajectories—so that behavior systematically moves toward high-reward regions of the world.

Picture a minimal grid world for intuition. An agent is a cat at coordinates (x, y); the grid is the state. From each cell the cat can move up, down, left, or right. A concrete toy reward specification might read: moving to an empty cell yields reward 0; moving toward a broom yields −1; landing in the bathtub yields −10 (the cat is terrified of water); reaching a piece of meat yields +100. A policy optimized for expected return will tend to navigate toward the meat while avoiding the tub and the broom—because that is how expected cumulative reward is maximized under the specified dynamics.

Language modeling fits the same schema at a high level. The state is the prompt (plus any tokens generated so far). The action is the next token. The LM already defines a policy: a distribution over the vocabulary at each step. What alignment adds is a notion of which token sequences should be rewarded—responses that answer the user, stay polite, and avoid harmful content should receive higher reward under a reward or preference model than sequences that do not.

Training with explicit rewards is analogous to giving a pet treats for desired behavior: the animal associates the action with a positive outcome and becomes more likely to repeat it. In digital form, “treats” are numeric rewards that reinforcement-style objectives use to reinforce token choices that lead to preferred completions—except that our dataset is often preferences rather than absolute scores, so we first need a principled way to turn comparisons into a reward signal (the Bradley–Terry layer).

Absolute scores per answer

Requires everyone to agree on one number per completion; noisy and inconsistent across annotators and styles (terse vs. verbose, formal vs. casual).

Pairwise preferences

Annotators pick the better of two (or rank a small set). The dataset becomes triples (x, yw, yl)—prompt, winner, loser—which are easier to collect reliably and feed directly into preference models.

Section 03

Bradley–Terry preference model

The Bradley–Terry model turns latent scores (rewards) into the probability that one completion is preferred over another. If r(x, y) is a scalar reward for prompt x and completion y, the probability that yw is preferred to yl is proportional to exp(r(x, yw)) in the numerator and normalized against both options:

PBT(yw ≻ yl | x) = exp(r(x, yw)) / ( exp(r(x, yw)) + exp(r(x, yl)) )

Training a reward network fits this likelihood: maximize the model’s probability of the observed ordering (equivalently minimize negative log-likelihood). The exponential ensures positive unnormalized masses and a proper softmax over the two completions.

This is a modeling choice: we declare that human preferences behave as if each completion carries a latent score, and the chance of preferring one over the other is a softmax over those two scores. Exponentials appear because we need nonnegative weights that can be normalized into a probability; the two-way softmax guarantees a proper distribution over the pair {winner, loser}. In the literature on direct preference optimization, the chosen and rejected completions are often called the winner and loser; here yw and yl play those roles.

Fitting the scores. Given a dataset of observed preferences, a standard approach is maximum likelihood: adjust the reward function (e.g., a neural network producing rφ(x, y)) so that the predicted probability of the observed ordering—winner preferred to loser—is as high as possible. Equivalently, minimize the negative log-likelihood of those orderings. Gradient descent on that loss encourages the model to assign higher reward to the winning answer and lower reward to the losing answer, because the BT probability is largest when the numerator’s exponential dominates the denominator.

ℒBT = −log PBT(yw ≻ yl | x)   (summed or averaged over the preference dataset)
Under the Bradley–Terry likelihood, minimizing this loss is the same problem as “make the model’s implied ordering match the annotators’ ordering as often as possible,” which is why the same probabilistic skeleton later reappears inside DPO—only with rewards rewritten in terms of log-ratios of policies.

Section 04

From two-way softmax to sigmoid

With two items, the Bradley–Terry probability has a closed form in terms of the logistic sigmoid σ(z) = 1 / (1 + e−z). Let a = r(x, yw) and b = r(x, yl). Then:

ea / (ea + eb) = 1 / (1 + e−(a−b)) = σ(a − b)

Proof sketch: divide numerator and denominator by ea, then rearrange 1 + eb−a as 1 + e−(a−b). Optimization uses log probabilities because log is monotonic and turns products into sums; frameworks minimize loss, so we use negative log-likelihood to turn maximization into minimization.

Denote the reward of the winning completion by a = r(x, yw) and of the losing completion by b = r(x, yl). The Bradley–Terry probability is P = ea / (ea + eb). Dividing numerator and denominator by ea gives P = 1 / (1 + eb−a); since eb−a = e−(a−b), this is σ(a − b). The probability of preferring the winner is exactly the sigmoid of the score gap a − b—pure exponential rearrangement, no approximation.

Because the logarithm is a strictly increasing function, optimizing log-probability is equivalent to optimizing probability itself, but log turns products of factors into sums—stable and convenient for autodiff. Frameworks such as PyTorch are built around minimizing a loss, so we pack the BT objective as negative log-likelihood: maximizing the chance of the observed winner is the same as minimizing −log P.

Concrete numbers. Take a = 2 and b = 0. Then e2/(e2 + e0) = e2/(e2 + 1) ≈ 0.8808, and σ(2 − 0) = σ(2) = 1/(1 + e−2) ≈ 0.8808—the two expressions agree to machine precision.

Section 05

RL-style objective and the KL penalty

A canonical alignment objective maximizes expected reward under the policy (the LM) while keeping the policy close to a reference model πref—typically the supervised-fine-tuned or pre-trained checkpoint before preference optimization. The Kullback–Leibler divergence KL(πθ ‖ πref) acts like a penalty on drifting too far from behaviors the reference already encodes, reducing reward hacking and preserving fluency.

Symbolically, one writes a constrained or regularized problem: increase reward from a (possibly implicit) reward model, but do not move πθ too far from πref. This is the same high-level goal as classical RL from human feedback, but the implementation can differ.

Takeaway DPO targets the same constrained optimum in spirit: match preferences while staying near the reference distribution, without explicitly fitting a separate reward network or running PPO-style updates.

The Kullback–Leibler divergence KL(πθ ‖ πref) is not a metric in the strict mathematical sense (it is asymmetric), but it is often described as a distance-like measure between two distributions over the same outcomes. In alignment, πref is typically a frozen checkpoint of the language model as it existed before preference optimization—trained on books, web text, Wikipedia, and related corpora—while πθ is the version we update. Penalizing KL keeps πθ from collapsing into degenerate hacks that chase reward while abandoning fluent, diverse text.

If the objective were only “maximize reward,” a model could become greedy: imagine a reward model that heavily favors politeness markers. The policy might then loop “thank you, thank you, thank you” or strings of “please” because those tokens score well, even when the result is useless as an answer. The KL term fights that pathology by anchoring the optimized policy to the reference’s broader behavior.

Analytic maximization as intuition for “closed-form” solutions

Unconstrained maximization of a smooth function is familiar from calculus. Consider the downward-opening parabola

f(x) = −x2 + 6x + 4.

To maximize f, take the derivative and set it to zero: f′(x) = −2x + 6 = 0, so x = 3. That critical point is the global maximum (the parabola opens downward). The constrained RLHF/DPO story is more involved, but the same flavor applies: under regularity conditions there can exist an analytical characterization of the best policy—expressible in closed form—even when evaluating that form naïvely is still too expensive for language-sized action spaces.

Section 06

Why plain RL on sampled text is awkward

Maximizing reward through on-policy rollouts requires sampling discrete tokens from the LM. Sampling is not differentiable: small parameter changes do not give a smooth gradient through the draw. That is why algorithms such as PPO use surrogate objectives, advantage estimates, and careful variance control. DPO sidesteps an explicit RL inner loop by reparameterizing the problem so a single differentiable loss on fixed preference pairs suffices.

The RLHF pipeline also trains a standalone reward model, then optimizes the LM against it—two heavy stages. DPO merges the preference signal into direct updates on πθ.

Why does vanilla backprop not “just work” on the RLHF objective written as an expectation over model samples? Generating a completion is an iterative loop: append one token, run the transformer again, repeat. At each step you may use greedy argmax decoding, beam search, top-k sampling, nucleus (top-p) sampling, or other discrete choice rules. The draw of a discrete token is not a differentiable operation with respect to the logits in the way a continuous reparameterization would be, so the objective that averages over sampled trajectories does not yield a simple path gradient through the sampling operator itself. That gap is exactly why classical RLHF turns to policy-gradient-style algorithms such as PPO (proximal policy optimization): surrogate objectives, advantage estimates, and variance reduction machinery are built to optimize stochastic policies even when the environment (here, the reward model acting on generated text) is non-differentiable in the naive sense.

RLHF-style loop

Train or use a reward model on preferences, then run RL (e.g., PPO) with sampled completions, KL penalties, and careful engineering. Sampling blocks straightforward end-to-end differentiation through the discrete generation path.

DPO-style optimization

Reparameterize the constrained reward problem so that, on fixed preference pairs, the objective reduces to a differentiable sigmoid-of-log-probabilities loss. No separate reward-network training phase and no inner RL loop—only gradients through log-probabilities under πθ and πref.

Section 07

Deriving the DPO loss from the constrained objective

Starting from the KL-regularized reward-maximization problem, one can write an analytical form of the optimal policy in terms of the reward r(x, y), the reference πref(y|x), a temperature-like coefficient β, and a partition-like term Z(x) that normalizes over all possible completions y. Computing Z(x) exactly requires summing over an enormous space of strings, so the closed form is not directly usable.

The key trick: substitute the expression for r(x, y) implied by that optimal policy into the Bradley–Terry log-likelihood for pairs (yw, yl). The Z(x) contributions are the same for both completions in a pair, so they cancel in the difference of rewards. What remains is computable from log-probabilities under πθ and πref alone.

LDPO(πθ; πref) = − E(x,yw,yl) [ log σ( β ( log πθ(yw|x) − log πθ(yl|x) − log πref(yw|x) + log πref(yl|x) ) ) ]

Intuition: the model should assign higher probability than the reference to the winning completion, and lower than the reference to the losing one—scaled by β. The sigmoid inside the log is the same structure as the Bradley–Terry probability, but with rewards expressed through log-ratio shifts relative to πref.

Attribution. Direct Preference Optimization was introduced by Rafailov, Sharma, Mitchell, Ermon, Manning, and Finn (NeurIPS 2023), in the paper titled Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model. The derivation below follows the paper’s logic: start from the KL-regularized RL objective, use its closed-form optimal policy, solve for the implicit reward, then substitute into the Bradley–Terry model so partition terms cancel.

Step 1 — KL-regularized reward maximization (conceptual setup)

The high-level problem is to increase expected reward under the language-model policy while penalizing deviation from a reference policy πref. Temperature-like coefficient β > 0 controls how strongly preferences pull against the KL anchor. Abstractly, one studies objectives of the form “maximize reward minus β times KL(πθ ‖ πref)” (equivalently, a constrained or Lagrangian formulation with the same tradeoff).

Step 2 — Analytical form of the optimal policy and the partition function Z(x)

Under standard regularity assumptions, the optimizing stochastic policy can be written in Boltzmann form relative to πref:

π*(y | x) = (1 / Z(x)) · πref(y | x) · exp(r(x, y) / β),

where the partition function (per prompt) normalizes over all completions y:

Z(x) = Σy πref(y | x) exp(r(x, y) / β).

This expression is the sense in which the constrained problem admits an exact solution on paper: π* is characterized explicitly in terms of r, πref, and β. The catch is computability. The sum defining Z(x) ranges over every string the model could emit. If completions may run to on the order of 2,000 tokens and the vocabulary has on the order of 30,000 tokens, a naïve count of length-L strings scales like 30,000L—astronomically beyond exhaustive evaluation. So Z(x) is mathematically the right normalization, but practically intractable to compute by brute force.

Step 3 — Inverting for the reward r(x, y)

Rearrange the optimal-policy identity to isolate the reward. From

π*(y | x) = πref(y | x) exp(r(x, y) / β) / Z(x),

multiply both sides by Z(x) and divide by πref(y | x) (assuming positive reference probability on the support you care about):

Z(x) · π*(y | x) / πref(y | x) = exp(r(x, y) / β).

Take natural logarithms on both sides. Use log(AB) = log A + log B and log(exp(·)) = · on the right:

log Z(x) + log π*(y | x) − log πref(y | x) = r(x, y) / β.

Multiply through by β:

r(x, y) = β log(π*(y | x) / πref(y | x)) + β log Z(x).

Training replaces the ideal π* with the current policy πθ while keeping the same algebraic skeleton—so the reward estimate tied to πθ inherits the same Z(x) term.

Step 4 — Plug into Bradley–Terry; watch Z(x) cancel

For a preference pair (yw, yl), the Bradley–Terry logit is the difference r(x, yw) − r(x, yl). Substitute the inverted form for each:

r(x, yw) − r(x, yl)
= β[log πθ(yw|x) − log πref(yw|x) + log Z(x)]
  − β[log πθ(yl|x) − log πref(yl|x) + log Z(x)].

The two occurrences of β log Z(x) are added once and subtracted once:

+ β log Z(x) − β log Z(x) = 0.

Only the log-probability differences remain:

r(x, yw) − r(x, yl) = β( log πθ(yw|x) − log πθ(yl|x) − log πref(yw|x) + log πref(yl|x) ).

The preference probability is therefore σ of that scalar (Section 04), and the DPO training loss is its negative log:

ℒDPO = −log σ( β( log πθ(yw|x) − log πθ(yl|x) − log πref(yw|x) + log πref(yl|x) ) ),

matching the compact expectation form already displayed above. This is the central trick of DPO: the intractable Z(x) never has to be estimated for pairwise training, because it depends only on x and cancels when you compare two completions under the same prompt.

Same objective family as classical RLHF—maximize reward subject to staying close to πref—but the preference signal is folded into a single differentiable loss on (πθ, πref) without an explicit reward-network phase and without PPO-style sampling through discrete generation during the preference update.

Section 08

Computing log π(y|x) in practice

For a fixed completion y, concatenate prompt and answer so the transformer sees the full sequence. For each answer token position, take the model’s logits, apply log-softmax over the vocabulary, and read off the log-probability assigned to the actual token at that position (the label). Because independent token choices multiply in probability space, in log space you sum these token log-probabilities over the answer span to get log π(y|x). Mask out the prompt positions so only completion tokens contribute.

Implementations (e.g., Hugging Face DPOTrainer) pair two models: the trainable policy and a frozen copy for πref. Both forward passes supply the log-probabilities that enter the DPO loss.

In code, you typically pass (i) the language model being optimized, (ii) a frozen reference copy used only for log-probabilities and the implicit KL story, and (iii) training hyperparameters—most importantly β, which sets how aggressively the update pushes πθ away from πref to satisfy preferences. Libraries document recommended β ranges for common setups; tuning depends on model size, data noise, and stability.

Token-level construction of log π(y | x)

Concatenate prompt and completion into one sequence for the transformer. At each position in the completion, the network produces logits; apply log-softmax across the vocabulary to obtain a log-probability distribution for the next token. Index that vector at the token id that actually appears in the dataset (the label). Because independent choices multiply in probability space, log-probabilities add along the answer span:

log π(y | x) ≈ Σt in answer log π(tokent | x, token<t).

Concretely, for prompt “Where is Shanghai?” and winning answer “Shanghai is in China,” the first scored step might be the probability of generating Shanghai immediately after the question; the next step conditions on …? Shanghai and scores the token is; then in, then China, and so on. Implementations mask out prompt positions so only completion tokens contribute to the sum—exactly the “labels = answer tokens” pattern used inside preference trainers.

Autoregressive models are not independent across tokens in reality: each step conditions on the full prefix. The independence language applies to the factorization π(y|x) = ∏t π(yt|x, y<t), not to ignoring context inside each factor.

Section 09

Reference model, β, and training workflow

πref is usually an earlier checkpoint (often the SFT model) held fixed. It anchors the implicit reward and prevents collapse. β controls the strength of the preference signal relative to staying near the reference: small β makes updates gentler; large β pushes harder toward the chosen responses (tuning depends on model size, data noise, and stability).

  • Dataset: tuples (x, yw, yl) from human or AI-assisted labeling.
  • Optimization: standard gradient descent on the DPO loss; no reward-model training phase or PPO loop.
  • Engineering: mixed precision, gradient clipping, and learning-rate schedules mirror other LM fine-tuning.
Contrast Classical RLHF: train reward model on preferences → RL optimize policy against reward with KL to reference. DPO: skip explicit reward model and RL, optimize one preference-aware loss on πθ with πref in the formula.

RLHF (PPO-style)

Fit a reward model to human rankings or preferences. Alternate generation, reward scoring, advantage estimation, and policy updates with explicit KL control. Sampling discrete tokens during training keeps the raw objective from being a vanilla differentiable loss through the full generation graph.

DPO

Keep a frozen πref (often the SFT checkpoint). On each minibatch of triples (x, yw, yl), compute log-probabilities for both completions under πθ and πref, evaluate the sigmoid loss, backpropagate into πθ only. The pipeline stays closer to supervised fine-tuning machinery than to full RL stacks.

Direct Preference Optimization reached wide attention in 2023 as a simpler alignment path that preserved the intent of KL-regularized preference learning while removing the outer RL loop from the training story engineers must implement day to day.

Playground

Tie the pieces together: Bradley–Terry scores imply a preference probability; the same quantity appears as a sigmoid of the score gap; DPO rewrites rewards as log-ratio corrections relative to πref, eliminating the partition function for pairs.

Read the chain once more end to end: language models factor as sequential conditional distributions; alignment prefers comparisons over absolute scores; Bradley–Terry maps latent rewards to a two-way softmax; that softmax is σ(a − b); KL-regularized RL would need sampling through discrete generation; the DPO construction instead uses the analytical optimal policy to express r(x, y) with a Z(x) term that vanishes in reward differences; the implementable loss is therefore a log-sigmoid of β times differences of completion log-probs under πθ and πref—computed by log-softmax gathers and a sum over answer tokens, masked so the prompt does not contribute.
