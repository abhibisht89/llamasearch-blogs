Roadmap

This chapter connects architectural choices to their goals: stable training, relative position awareness in attention, fast autoregressive inference, memory-bandwidth-aware attention, and expressive feed-forward layers.

  • Architecture — Why LLaMA drops the encoder and uses a stack of identical decoder-style blocks.
  • Pre-norm vs post-norm — Where normalization sits relative to sublayers and residual paths.
  • RMSNorm — Rescaling without mean-centering; fewer statistics than LayerNorm.
  • RoPE — Rotary position applied to queries and keys; geometric intuition via rotations.
  • KV cache — Reusing past key/value projections across decode steps.
  • MQA & GQA — Sharing key/value heads to cut memory traffic; GQA as a middle ground.
  • SwiGLU — Gated feed-forward with SiLU; three projection matrices vs two in the classic ReLU FFN.

Prerequisites. Familiarity with matrix multiplication, dot products, and scaled softmax attention from Chapter 01 (encoder vs decoder contrast in Chapter 03). Complex numbers help for RoPE but are not strictly required if you accept the rotation-matrix view.

01

Decoder-only stack vs encoder–decoder

The original Transformer pairs an encoder (bidirectional context over the input) with a decoder (causal self-attention plus cross-attention into the encoder). LLaMA is a large language model trained primarily on next-token prediction: a single stream of tokens is embedded, processed with causal self-attention only, and the model learns to predict the next token from all previous ones.

Encoder–decoder

Separate stacks; decoder attends to encoder representations. Natural for tasks with distinct source and target sequences.

Decoder-only (LLaMA)

One repeating block (×N layers): normalization → attention → residual → normalization → feed-forward → residual → … The output of the last layer is fed to a final RMS Norm, then a linear layer, then softmax over the vocabulary.

Block-level differences from classic decoder blocks. Positional information is not added to embeddings as sinusoidal vectors; rotary positional embeddings (RoPE) act inside attention on queries and keys after their linear projections. The feed-forward uses SwiGLU instead of ReLU between two layers. Larger variants use grouped-query attention (GQA) to reduce key/value head count.

Decoder-only. LLaMA drops the encoder stack entirely: one repeating decoder block, causal self-attention only, no cross-attention—trained purely on next-token prediction.

02

Pre-norm vs post-norm

In many early Transformer diagrams, layer normalization appeared after each sublayer, combined with the residual (post-norm). In LLaMA, all normalizations have been moved before every block — before the attention sublayer and before the feed-forward (pre-norm). The sublayer always sees a normalized residual stream.

Pre-norm often yields smoother optimization in deep stacks: gradients flow through the residual path more directly, while the normalized activations keep scale controlled going into attention and MLP weights. In pre-norm, xout = x + branch(x), so the gradient for x is 1 + (gradient of the branch)—a direct highway through dozens of layers. Post-norm normalizes after each sublayer, so gradients pass through more transforms at every stop; pre-norm keeps the raw residual stream intact while each branch adds a normalized correction.

03

Embeddings and token IDs

Text is split into tokens (commonly subword units via BPE or SentencePiece — not naive whitespace splitting). Each token maps to an integer index in a fixed vocabulary — the list of all tokens the model recognizes. The embedding table maps each index to a learnable vector of dimension d_model (the same lookup pattern as in Chapter 01 and Chapter 03). In the original Transformer this was 512; in LLaMA it grows to 4096 or larger depending on the model size.

Take a sentence like “Your cat is a lovely cat.” The tokenizer splits it into subword tokens; each token becomes an input ID that indexes the embedding table. If d_model = 4096, the word “cat” becomes a vector of 4096 floating-point numbers.

Semantic geometry. Because embeddings are learnable parameters, they shift during training so that semantically related tokens end up with similar vectors. For example, “cat” and “dog” — both animals — will develop nearby embeddings. Similarly, “house” and “building” — both structures — will cluster together in embedding space. The geometry of embedding space reflects co-occurrence patterns and semantic relationships in the training corpus.

04

Why normalize? Linear layers and internal covariate shift

Linear layers: the building block

A linear layer (e.g. nn.Linear(3, 5, bias=True) in PyTorch) creates two learnable objects: a weight matrix W of shape (output_features × input_features) — so 5×3 here — and a bias vector b of length 5 (one bias per output neuron). You can think of each row of W as one neuron, with one weight per input feature.

Given an input X of shape (10 items × 3 features), the output Y is (10 × 5). Each output element Z₁ for item 1 is the dot product of that item’s feature row with neuron 1’s weight column, plus the bias: Z₁ = (a₁·w₁ + a₂·w₂ + a₃·w₃) + b₁. The key insight: the output of a neuron for an item depends only on that item’s features and the neuron’s weights — not on other items in the batch. The bias b is broadcast (added to every row independently via PyTorch broadcasting). After the linear layer, a non-linearity (like ReLU) is typically applied — the input to this non-linearity is called the activation of the neuron.

The problem: internal covariate shift

Since the input to a layer is the output of the previous layer, a problem arises during training. When a lower layer’s weights are updated by gradient descent, its output distribution can change drastically. The next layer then receives inputs it has never seen before — forcing it to readjust its own weights heavily. This makes training oscillate and slow down, as each layer chases a constantly moving target. This phenomenon — the distribution of internal layer inputs changing during training — is called internal covariate shift. Normalization standardizes each handoff so the next sublayer always sees activations in a predictable range, no matter how far the previous layer moved.

05

LayerNorm: mean, variance, and learnable gain

Layer normalization (derived in Chapter 01) computes, per row (per token position in a sequence batch), the mean μ and variance σ² across features, standardizes, then applies learnable γ and β. LLaMA replaces this with RMSNorm in the next section.

Variance uses the mean: σ² = (1/d) ∑i (xi − μ)². Here ε avoids division by zero. Batch normalization differs by normalizing across the batch dimension per feature (column-wise statistics), whereas LayerNorm is row-wise — better suited to variable-length sequences in Transformers.

Standardization intuition. If a scalar random variable has mean 5 and variance 36, then (X − 5) / 6 has mean 0 and variance 1 — a standard Gaussian. This is exactly what LayerNorm does: it transforms feature distributions into zero mean and unit variance, then lets the learnable γ and β adjust as needed.

06

RMSNorm: rescaling without centering

Root Mean Square Layer Normalization (RMSNorm) was introduced in the paper “Root Mean Square Layer Normalization.” LayerNorm provides two properties: recentering invariance (insensitivity to shift noise on inputs and weights — enabled by subtracting the mean) and rescaling invariance (keeping representations stable under random scaling — enabled by dividing by the standard deviation).

The key hypothesis. The paper states: “In this paper we hypothesize that the rescaling invariance is the reason for success of layer Norm, rather than the recentering invariance.” In other words: it’s the division by scale (having variance ~1) that matters for stable training — not the subtraction of the mean (having mean ~0). Can we drop mean-centering entirely and still get the same benefit?

Since variance requires the mean (σ² = (1/d) Σ(xᵢ − μ)²), the authors needed a different statistic that doesn’t depend on μ at all. They chose the root-mean-square:

The RMS statistic is calculated per row (per token position), just like LayerNorm — but it needs only one statistic instead of two. You just square each element, average, and take the square root. Then divide and multiply by the learnable γ (no β needed since we’re not recentering). At billion-parameter scale, applied at every layer and token position, skipping the mean saves measurable compute. In practice, RMSNorm works just as well as LayerNorm in models like LLaMA — confirming the hypothesis that rescaling invariance is what drives most of the training benefit.

07

Position: absolute vs relative

Absolute positional encodings are like latitude and longitude on a map: every slot in the sequence gets a fixed address. Each position receives a unique vector that is computed once (not learned from data), then reused for every sentence at that index.

In the vanilla Transformer, those are typically sinusoidal vectors of size (for example) 512, added directly to token embeddings. When attention later forms Q·KT, the queries and keys already have absolute position baked in alongside semantic content.

Relative positional encodings: reasoning about pairs

Relative schemes reason about two tokens at a time: instead of encoding “I am position 47,” they encode how far apart two tokens are. Absolute encodings hard-code a slot index, but language usually cares about relative distance—the phrase “the cat sat” means the same at position 0 or 500. Relative methods add distance-dependent terms to the attention score matrix (see Chapter 01 for the base softmax attention formula).

RoPE placement. RoPE is applied after multiplication by WQ and WK (not before, unlike adding sinusoids to embeddings in the vanilla Transformer). It is not applied to V—only Q and K form the scoring mechanism; V carries the content being mixed.

08

RoPE (Rotary Position Embedding)

RoFormer, from a Chinese research group, asked a sharp question: can we arrange the attention inner product so it depends only on the token embeddings and their relative distance (e.g. m − n), not on absolute indices alone?

The construction often starts in complex space with a function g(xm, xn, m−n) and Euler's formula linking rotation to eiθ. In two real dimensions, each pair of coordinates acts like a 2×2 rotation:

For higher model dimensions, RoPE uses a block-sparse structure: mostly zeros with 2×2 rotation blocks along the diagonal (each pair of feature dimensions gets its own angle). In efficient implementations, that becomes element-wise multiply with precomputed cos / sin vectors, plus swapped pairs scaled by sin — no need to materialize the full huge matrix.

The base frequencies θi follow a fixed geometric series, e.g. θi = base−2i/d — nothing to learn in the geometry itself; the model learns how to use the resulting positional signal. Because rotations depend on m − n, the dot product of rotated Q and K encodes relative distance; when m = n, the relative angle is zero and the score is purely content-based.

Complex form. You can read RoPE as applying a position-dependent complex phase per 2D subspace; the real implementation is the same rotations written out with cos and sin.

Long-term decay intuition. Different frequency channels respond at different spans; the construction induces an envelope where very distant pairs are softly down-weighted — helpful inductive bias for modeling locality while still allowing attention to long context.

09

Self-attention recap

LLaMA uses causal self-attention with RoPE on queries and keys—the full scaled dot-product derivation, multi-head wiring, and matrix shapes are in Chapter 01. Bidirectional encoder attention (BERT) is covered in Chapter 03. At decode time a causal mask zeroes entries above the diagonal so token t may attend only to positions 1…t.

Multi-head attention in one forward pass: copy hidden states to Q, K, V; multiply by learned matrices; split into H heads; run attention independently per head; concatenate; multiply by WO to mix heads back together.

10

Training vs inference

Decoder models are typically trained on next-token prediction. A training target might be a line from Dante: "Nel mezzo del cammin di nostra vita / mi ritrovai per una selva oscura…" — the model sees a prefix and is trained to predict each next token in parallel across positions (with masking so it cannot cheat on future tokens).

Training setup: often a start-of-sequence token is prepended and an end-of-sequence token appended to the target. Each input position maps to an output position predicting the following token. The entire sequence is processed in one forward pass (“teacher forcing”): no Python for loop over time steps inside the model for that example.

Inference is different: we generate step by step. Step 1: feed [SOS] and read the distribution for the first word — say "love". Step 2: feed [SOS, love] and read the next token — say "that", and so on.

Even though the network can compute logits for all positions in one forward pass, at decode time we usually care only about the last token's prediction. Computing full attention over the growing prefix every time creates redundant work — exactly what a KV cache is meant to remove.

11

KV cache

Without caching, each new generated token retriggers attention with a longer prefix. The effective score matrix grows: 1×1, then 2×2, 3×3, 4×4, … Two issues stand out: (1) many dot products are recomputed again and again; (2) with a causal mask we only need the last row of scores at decode time to form the next output.

Solution: store the projected K and V for all past tokens. The new step only computes Q for the latest token and dots it against cached keys (and uses cached values when weighting). That trades redundant compute for memory: illustrative classroom numbers put cache near ~2.6 MB per token in some large-model configs (precision, width, layers, and heads all matter), so ~5.2 GB for 2048 tokens shows how fast VRAM use grows.

Step-by-step with KV cache

  • After processing token t, append that layer's Kt and Vt to the cache tensors.
  • At step t+1, compute only Qt+1; attention scores are a single row against all cached keys up to t+1.
  • The weighted sum uses the same cached V vectors for past positions plus the new Vt+1.

Inference-only. KV caching is an inference-time optimization. It applies to all autoregressive Transformer decoders in principle, not only LLaMA.

12

Memory bandwidth

Modern GPUs are so fast at arithmetic that many Transformer workloads become memory-bound. For perspective, an NVIDIA A100 might offer on the order of ~19.5 TFLOPS FP32 compute versus ~1.9 TB/s memory bandwidth — roughly a 10× imbalance favoring "math is cheap, moving bytes is expensive."

The practical goal is to minimize memory access, not only the operation count. Without KV cache, attention can be relatively compute-heavy (reuse ratios small). With KV cache, analysis often writes effective traffic ratios in terms of sequence length n, model width d, and bytes-per-element B — schematically like O(n/d + 1/B) in simplified roofline stories: when n grows toward d or B is small, the bottleneck tips toward bandwidth.

Irony. KV cache solved the obvious redundant-compute problem for autoregressive decoding, but it can push systems into a regime where memory bandwidth and capacity dominate — motivating MQA, GQA, quantization, and batching strategies.

13

MQA (Multi-Query Attention)

MQA is due in large part to Noam Shazeer (also a co-author on "Attention Is All You Need"). The idea: keep many query heads for expressive matching, but share one key head and one value head across all query heads.

Where a rough traffic ratio might have scaled like n/d in a one-head-per-layer story, sharing K/V across H heads improves it toward something like n/(d·H) — a factor of H fewer distinct K/V bytes to read from memory per step in that sketch.

Reported trade-offs in the MQA paper include small quality deltas on translation benchmarks (e.g. BLEU 26.7 multi-head vs 26.5 MQA) alongside large latency wins (illustrative numbers: inference dropping from ~≈48 μs to ~≈6 μs per step — about 8×). Local attention variants restrict keys to a window (e.g. the previous 31 positions) to cap cost for very long sequences.

14

GQA (Grouped-Query Attention)

GQA partitions query heads into G groups; each group shares its own K/V head pair. G = H recovers full multi-head attention (MHA); G = 1 recovers MQA. GQA interpolates quality and bandwidth between the two. With 64 query heads and one shared K/V pair (MQA), cache traffic drops roughly 64× versus full MHA; collapsing all the way to one pair can hurt quality, so LLaMA 2 uses intermediate grouping on larger checkpoints.

LLaMA 2 applies GQA on the larger 34B and 70B variants while smaller sizes may still use standard MHA — a pragmatic scaling choice.

SwiGLU feed-forward

Also from Noam Shazeer (same research line as attention variants and MQA): compare many activation choices for the Transformer FFN and find SwiGLU especially strong.

The classic FFN is two matrices with a simple nonlinearity in between, e.g. W2 · ReLU(W1 · x). SwiGLU uses the Swish with β=1, also called SiLU (Sigmoid Linear Unit), as a gate:

There are three weight matrices; fair comparisons shrink the intermediate width so parameter and FLOP counts stay comparable to two-matrix baselines. In Shazeer's studies, SwiGLU achieves the lowest perplexity among the alternatives tested.

Honest science. Shazeer et al. write: "We offer no explanation for the hookup of our favorite non-linearity and the gated linear unit, hoping that the benefit of the combination is yet another example of the unreasonable effectiveness of divine benevolence."

Ablation studies swap activations and widths while holding compute roughly fixed; practical evaluation then checks translation quality and training stability. SiLU keeps a small, smooth tail for negative values unlike ReLU’s hard zero—avoiding classic dying ReLU units that stop receiving gradients. The gate lets the network modulate how much of the transformed vector passes through the parallel projection.
