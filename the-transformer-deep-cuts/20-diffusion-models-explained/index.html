Diffusion Models: How DDPM Works

Denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPM) learn a reverse process that turns pure noise into structured data. The forward path is a fixed Markov chain that adds Gaussian noise; training teaches a network to predict that noise.

00 — Generative landscape

Where diffusion sits

Generative modeling aims to learn a distribution over high-dimensional data—images, audio, text latents—so new samples can be drawn. Several families have been explored; each makes different trade-offs between sample quality, mode coverage, and training stability.

GANs

Adversarial training: a generator faces a discriminator. Sharp samples, but optimization can be brittle and mode dropping is a known issue.

VAEs

Encoder–decoder with a latent prior (often Gaussian). Stable likelihood-based training; samples can be blurrier than GANs.

Normalizing flows

Invertible transforms with tractable Jacobian determinants give exact likelihoods. Expressive but architecture constraints apply.

Diffusion

Gradually destroy structure with noise, then learn to undo it. Strong empirical results; training reduces to regressing noise under Gaussian assumptions.

DDPM (2020) popularized the discrete-time Gaussian diffusion formulation used throughout this chapter: a fixed forward noising process and a learned reverse denoising process. Earlier work by Sohl-Dickstein et al. framed learning as reversing a gradual noising process using nonequilibrium statistical mechanics; Ho et al. gave the practical discrete Gaussian recipe—clear notation, stable training objective, and strong image samples—that most codebases follow today.

Diffusion extends the generative goal from variational autoencoders (Chapter 19): instead of one latent vector, it uses a Markov chain of noisy states. This chapter covers the DDPM forward schedule, noise-prediction objective, and sampling loop.

01 — From latents to a noise trajectory

Beyond a single latent vector

The latent-variable intuition from Chapter 19 applies here: we posit hidden structure behind observed data x₀. Diffusion replaces one draw z with a Markov chain of increasingly noisy states x₁, …, x_T, ending near isotropic Gaussian noise. Clean data is t = 0; the terminal state at T (often ~1000 steps) is pure noise—that sequence is the forward process.

The reverse process walks backward one step at a time: from x_T, infer something useful about x_{T−1}, and repeat. Each transition depends only on its neighbor. Learning fills in the reverse kernels pθ; the fixed corruption schedule is q.

02 — Forward and reverse intuition

Forward and reverse intuition

Each forward step adds a small Gaussian perturbation. After many steps—often on the order of a thousand in the classic DDPM setup—the signal resembles isotropic Gaussian noise. That iterative corruption is the forward process.

The reverse process asks the harder question: given noise at timestep T, can you infer the state at T − 1? Each step only talks to its neighbor—no skipping—so the chain is Markov.

Forward (easy)

Adding noise is something you can always do. Hand the Mona Lisa to a three-year-old with crayons and you will get all the corruption you could want. The forward kernel is chosen by us; it involves no learnable weights.

Reverse (hard)

Removing noise to recover a coherent real-world object—without ever having seen that exact trajectory—is the hard direction. Because it is hard, we train a neural network to approximate the reverse transitions.

Mathematically, write the clean datum as x₀. Imagine a chain of latent-style variables: x₀ depends on z₁, which depends on z₂, which depends on z₃, and so on until the terminal variable is pure noise. The forward direction is noiseification; the reverse direction is denoising (recovering structure from chaos).

Notation: t = 0 is the clean data x₀; larger t means more noise, and T denotes the noisiest state (≈ pure noise). The forward chain is fixed—we know how to move from less noise to more—whereas we do not initially know how to move from more noise to less. That missing knowledge is exactly what θ must supply.

03 — Gaussian forward process and schedules

Markov chain, βt, and one-step jumps

The discrete-time Gaussian formulation used throughout this chapter is the one introduced in the DDPM paper by Ho et al. (2020). There the forward distribution is written q. Crucially, it is not qθ: forward transition rules carry no learned parameters. The reverse distribution is written pθ; the subscript reminds you that a network must learn how to step from noisy to cleaner states.

q — forward

Fixed Markov transitions from a less noisy state (smaller t) to a more noisy state (larger t). Means and variances are dictated by a predetermined schedule, not by gradient descent.

pθ — reverse

Learned Gaussian transitions from more noise toward less noise. Training discovers the means and covariances (or equivalent parameterizations) that best invert the corruption.

Each step applies a Gaussian perturbation; the whole forward process is a Markov chain of Gaussian variables whose moments you can write down. One-step notation matches the paper: conditioning on the immediately previous state, the mean shrinks the signal and adds fresh noise according to a variance hyperparameter βt fixed for that step.

q(xt | xt−1) = 𝒩( √(1 − βt) · xt−1 ,  βt · I )

In sampling form, that is xt = √(1 − βt) · xt−1 + √βt · ε with ε ~ 𝒩(0, I). The sequence β₁, …, βT is the noise schedule: you decide it before training. Each βt is small enough that the chain is gradual, but their cumulative effect drives the final state toward standard Gaussian noise.

Step-by-step: one forward transition with explicit numbers

Take a toy scalar image pixel xt−1 = 1.00 and a schedule choice βt = 0.02 for this step. Then αt = 1 − βt = 0.98, and √(1 − βt) = √0.98 ≈ 0.9899, while √βt = √0.02 ≈ 0.1414. Draw a concrete noise sample ε = 0.50 (in practice it is random; we fix it here to show the arithmetic).

xt = √(1 − βt) · xt−1 + √βt · ε
    ≈ 0.9899 · (1.00) + 0.1414 · (0.50)
    ≈ 0.9899 + 0.0707 = 1.0606

So a single forward step nudges the value and injects stochasticity. Repeating this for hundreds or thousands of indices with different βt values is what erases semantic content.

Skipping intermediate steps: α, ᾱ, and closed-form noise

Because the forward kernel is linear-Gaussian, you never need to materialize every link in the chain during training. Define αt = 1 − βt and the cumulative product ᾱt = ∏s=1t αs. Then the marginal at time t given clean x₀ is

q(xt | x₀) = 𝒩( √ᾱt · x₀ ,  (1 − ᾱt) · I )

Equivalently, xt = √ᾱt · x₀ + √(1 − ᾱt) · ε with ε ~ 𝒩(0, I). Here is a second micro-walkthrough with numbers. Suppose after accumulating several betas you have ᾱt = 0.25. Then √ᾱt = 0.5 and √(1 − ᾱt) = √0.75 ≈ 0.8660. If x₀ = 1.00 and again ε = 0.50,

xt = 0.5 · (1.00) + 0.8660 · (0.50) ≈ 0.5000 + 0.4330 = 0.9330

The signal weight √ᾱt shrinks toward zero as t grows; the noise weight √(1 − ᾱt) approaches one. At the terminal T, the clean component has effectively vanished—consistent with “pure noise” at the end of the forward chain.

Training exploits this closure property: pick a random t, draw fresh ε, build xt in one shot from x₀, and supervise a network to predict the same ε. No loop over all intermediate states is required in the innermost loss.

This reparameterization is what makes training efficient: sample t, sample ε, build xt, and supervise the network to predict ε.

04 — Training objective

ELBO and noise prediction

As in VAE training (Chapter 19), direct maximization of log pθ(x₀) is intractable for rich neural pθ. The latent chain x₁, …, xT plays the role of VAE latents z, but with Gaussian Markov dynamics. Training maximizes a variational lower bound (ELBO); DDPM reduces the practical objective to noise prediction.

The full algebraic path from the ELBO to the simplified noise-prediction loss is in the DDPM paper. Gaussian identities let you re-express reverse means in terms of ε; optimizing that surrogate is stable in practice.

A key simplification in DDPM is that the learned reverse mean can be rewritten in terms of predicting the noise ε that was used to construct xt from x₀. Because the forward map x₀ ↦ xt is closed form, you always know which Gaussian draw ε you injected at the sampled time index. The network εθ sees the corrupted image and the index t (or an embedding of it) so it knows the noise level; its target is the exact same noise tensor you added.

The training loop, aligned with the paper’s description, is:

  • Step 1: Draw a minibatch of real images x₀ from the training distribution.
  • Step 2: For each image independently, sample a corruption index t uniformly from {1, …, T} (the discrete noise levels).
  • Step 3: Sample Gaussian noise ε ~ 𝒩(0, I) and construct xt = √ᾱt x₀ + √(1 − ᾱt) ε using the precomputed schedule.
  • Step 4: Forward through the U-Net (or any architecture) to obtain εθ(xt, t).
  • Step 5: Compute mean squared error between the predicted noise εθ and the ground-truth ε you used to build xt. Backpropagate.
ℒsimple = 𝔼t,x₀,ε [ ‖ ε - εθ(xt, t) ‖² ]

Gaussian conditioning identities let you translate between predicting noise, predicting the reverse mean, and predicting the clean signal—they are reparameterizations under fixed forward schedules. The ε-parameterization matches the closed-form corruption xt, which is why implementations emphasize it.

The network’s job is not to reconstruct x₀ in one shot but to estimate the noise component at the chosen noise level—across all t—which indirectly shapes the reverse transitions and thereby the generative distribution pθ(x₀).

05 — Sampling (generation)

Start from noise, walk backward

Sampling—in the generative sense—means drawing a fresh example from the learned model, not retrieving a database row. Initialize xT ~ 𝒩(0, I), then iteratively apply the learned reverse transition for timesteps T, T−1, …, 1 until you reach t = 0. Each update asks the network how to shave off a thin slice of noise consistent with the schedule. After the full trajectory, the tensor you hold is an x₀ the model synthesizes on the fly.

That output generally does not coincide with any single training image: you are decoding a particular random noise draw through the same computational pathway a VAE decodes a latent vector—except the “latent” here is the entire stack of noisy states traversed backward. The latent space is doing double duty: it must remain easy to sample from (start anywhere in broad Gaussian noise) and it must encode enough structure that the denoising trajectory lands on plausible data. The resulting x₀ is a new draw—not a memorized training point.

Reverse process, step by step

The sampling routine in the DDPM paper is the mirror image of training: instead of jumping from x₀ to xt with a known ε, you begin where the forward chain ends and peel noise away one layer at a time.

  • Step 1: Sample the initial state xT ~ 𝒩(0, I)—pure isotropic noise matching the forward limit.
  • Step 2 (inner loop): For t = T, T−1, …, 1, evaluate εθ(xt, t). Combine that prediction with the fixed schedule constants αt, βt, and ᾱt to form the Gaussian mean of the reverse kernel, add the prescribed variance (optionally scaled stochasticity at small t), and draw xt−1. This is the denoising move the network was trained to support.
  • Step 3: After the final update, read off x₀ as the generated sample.

One convenient ancestral-sampling parameterization (matching the usual DDPM code) expresses the mean of pθ(xt−1 | xt) directly in terms of the predicted noise. Let z ~ 𝒩(0, I) for stochastic sampling (or set z = 0 for a deterministic denoising trajectory). With schedule terms αt = 1 − βt and cumulative ᾱt, a standard update is:

xt−1 = 1⁄√αt ( xt − βt⁄√(1 − ᾱt) · εθ(xt, t) ) + σt · z

Here σt is fixed by the forward variance schedule (the paper discusses tying it to βt or learning it). Numeric check of the algebraic skeleton: suppose (only for order-of-magnitude intuition) αt = 0.98, so 1/√αt ≈ 1.010; βt = 0.02; and at some late time ᾱt = 0.09, so √(1 − ᾱt) = √0.91 ≈ 0.954 and βt/√(1 − ᾱt) ≈ 0.0210. If the network predicts εθ = 0.4 and the current state is xt = 0.3, the bracketed correction is 0.3 − 0.0210 × 0.4 ≈ 0.2916, and scaling by 1/√αt yields roughly 0.2945 before adding the σt z term. The exact posterior mean uses the full DDPM coefficients; the point is to see how εθ enters linearly and how schedule constants set the step size.

You do not need to memorize every coefficient to read code: repositories implement the paper’s sampling algorithm verbatim—often an outer setup (draw xT) and an inner for-loop over timesteps whose body matches the equation block in Ho et al. Variable names frequently mirror the symbols so you can move between PDF and source without a glossary.

06 — U-Net backbone and score matching

Architecture and a continuous-time link

The DDPM paper adopts a U-Net because that architecture already had a track record on dense prediction tasks. Originally introduced around 2015 for biomedical image segmentation, the classic U-Net compresses an input through an encoder tower until a tight bottleneck, then expands back to full resolution with skip connections that preserve fine spatial detail. Visually and functionally it resembles an autoencoder with wired shortcuts—exactly the inductive bias you want when predicting a field (here, a noise residual) aligned pixel-for-pixel with the corrupted input.

Two modifications matter relative to an off-the-shelf segmentation U-Net:

  • Timestep conditioning: the denoiser must know how noisy its input is. Following the Transformer literature, sinusoidal positional encodings (or closely related Fourier features) represent the integer timestep t. Those vectors are concatenated (or added after projection) at each downsampling and upsampling stage so εθ truly acts as a function of both xt and t, not of pixels alone.
  • Self-attention: at lower spatial resolutions—where memory is affordable—self-attention layers mix information across distant image regions. That helps capture global layout while convolutions handle local texture.

Educational repositories sometimes strip attention blocks, channel widths, or residual depth to keep files short. That is excellent for reading the training loop, but the resulting U-Net may no longer match the capacity profile used in the benchmark DDPM experiments. Treat minimalist code as pedagogical scaffolding, not as the final word on architecture fidelity.

Nothing in the diffusion math mandates a U-Net. Ho et al. chose it because it works well for predicting spatially aligned noise fields. Any model class that accepts xt and t and emits a tensor the same shape as ε is fair game—from tiny convnets for toy data to heavier backbones for high-resolution media. What matters is the conditional mapping (xt, t) ↦ ε, not a trademarked skip-network diagram.

Connection to score matching

For a Gaussian perturbation kernel, the score ∇x log pt(x) of the noisy density is proportional to the noise used to create xt from x₀. Predicting ε is therefore related to score matching and Langevin-like updates: the network learns vector fields that point toward higher-density regions at each noise level. Diffusion sampling can be read as discretely integrating these score estimates from pure noise down to the data manifold.
