00

Why quantize: memory, speed, energy

Large deep networks can hold billions of parameters. A concrete anchor: the smallest Llama 2 variant has on the order of 7 billion parameters; if every parameter were stored as 32 bits, weight storage alone is about 28 GB on disk—and at inference you must load those parameters into RAM (CPU) or GPU memory. That scale is why “big” checkpoints do not fit comfortably on a typical laptop, phone, or small embedded board.

Quantization maps floating-point values to low-bit integers (often uint8 or int8) using a reversible approximate affine map. It is not “round every float to the nearest whole number”; the scale s and zero_point Z stretch the tensor’s dynamic range across the integer lattice.

Integer multiply-accumulate is typically cheaper than full fp32 pipelines on most hardware, and int8 GEMM paths exist in CPU and GPU libraries—matrix work on integers is an attractive target once weights and activations live on a fixed grid.

Smaller activations and weights cut memory traffic and energy—important on battery-powered devices where you often run batch size one. On very small embedded hardware you may have no floating-point unit at all; integer-only paths are not merely faster there—they can be mandatory.

Prerequisites: Basic familiarity with neural networks (forward pass, loss, gradients) and high-school mathematics are enough to follow the formulas below.

Goal: Keep outputs of the quantized network close to the fp32 baseline for the same inputs while shrinking model size and speeding up Linear / Conv2d core ops.

Deep Dive — Bonus Q&A
Bonus Question

If int8 gives a 4× size reduction over float32, why not go further to int4 or even int2?

Fewer bits means fewer representable values, so quantization error rises steeply. With only 4 bits you get 16 codes; with 2 bits just 4 codes. Additionally, most CPU/GPU hardware has fast paths for 8-bit but not for exotic widths like 4 or 2 — you may lose the speed benefit entirely. In practice, 4-bit quantization (e.g. GPTQ, AWQ) is used for LLMs with careful calibration, but going below that typically requires specialized methods.

Bonus Question

On hardware with no floating-point unit at all, is quantization optional or mandatory?

Mandatory. Some embedded microcontrollers lack FP hardware entirely. Integer-only inference paths are the only way to run a neural network on those devices — quantization isn't just an optimization, it's a requirement.

01

Fixed-width bits, integers, and IEEE 754 float32

Hardware stores numbers in fixed bit widths. An n-bit pattern represents at most 2**n distinct values. With three bits, for example, you can represent 2³ = 8 distinct codes, conventionally the integers 0 through 7, each with a binary expansion. You recover the decimal value by summing each bit multiplied by the corresponding power of two—same idea as base ten, but with base two weights.

Signed integers on most CPUs use two’s complement: the high bit is the sign (0 positive, 1 negative); the remaining bits encode magnitude (or complement form for negatives). A practical payoff is a unique encoding for zero: +0 and -0 are not two different bit patterns, which simplifies arithmetic hardware.

Arbitrary-precision integers: CPython stores large integers as limb arrays (conceptually base 2**30), so expressions like 2**9999 work in Python even though they exceed fixed 64-bit hardware integers. That is interpreter machinery, not native ALU width.

Separately, “decimal” real numbers in everyday notation are still positional: 85.612 means 8×10¹ + 5×10⁰ + 6×10⁻¹ + 1×10⁻² + 2×10⁻³—integer powers for the whole part and negative powers for the fractional tail. IEEE 754 float32 uses the same structural idea in binary: one sign bit, eight exponent bits (bias 127), and 23 fraction bits. Assembling (−1)^sign × 2^(e−127) × (1 + f), with f built from negative powers of two for the mantissa, lands on concrete values; a canonical textbook-style bit pattern decodes to 0.15625 in decimal—illustrating how a finite bit string pins down a representable real on the grid.

Narrower float16 / bfloat16 trade exponent and mantissa bits for half the footprint; you get fewer representable values between neighbors. int8 quantization goes further: store integers plus small per-tensor metadata s, Z, and rehydrate to approximate fp32 only when needed.

02

Quantization at a Linear layer

A torch.nn.Linear computes y = x @ W.T + b with fp32 (or bf16) tensors. The parameters are two tensors people usually call weight and bias, historically stored as floats. Quantization’s job is to represent W, b, and (when possible) x with integers so the core multiply-add runs as integer matmul, then dequantize the result so the next layer receives a float tensor as if nothing odd happened—the downstream module should not “see” quantization artifacts beyond small numerical drift.

PTQ freezes W and b after training, estimates ranges, and stores int8 weights plus scale / zero_point. Activations x change every forward pass. One approach is dynamic quantization: for each incoming batch you observe the min and max of x on the fly, compute α and β, quantize x, and run integer matmul. The output Y of that integer product is not yet on the original float scale of the unquantized network. Calibration means running representative inputs through the trained model, recording typical ranges of those outputs, and choosing α/β (hence s and Z) so you can dequantize Y back to a float that tracks the full-precision baseline. Static PTQ bakes those statistics with observers during a dedicated calibration pass.

After integer matmul, the partial sum is dequantized so the next layer still sees a float-like tensor. The pipeline is only useful if dequant(requant(·)) approximates identity on typical activations.

Shapes and the quant round-trip

Per-tensor scale s is a scalar (or shared vector broadcast). q holds int8 codes; x_hat = s * (q - Z) for affine, or x_hat = s * q for symmetric. MSE(x, x_hat) measures quantization error.

03

Affine (asymmetric) quantization

Let α = max(x), β = min(x), and n bits. For unsigned q ∈ [0, 2**n - 1]:

s = (α − β) / (2n − 1),   Z = round(−β / s)   (Z is an integer; s stays float)
q = clamp( round(x / s) + Z , 0 , 2n − 1 )   where n is the bit width (e.g. n = 8 → codes 0…255)
x̂ = s · (q − Z)

The inner round implements “round down or up to the nearest integer” before clamping. Any result below 0 clamps to 0; any result above 2n − 1 clamps to 2n − 1. After dequantization, the largest original value should map back to the top bin and the smallest to 0; real zero should coincide with Z when the rounding aligns.

Symmetric signed grid:   qmax = 2n−1 − 1,   s = max(|tensor|) / qmax   (α = largest magnitude entry)
q = clamp( round(x / s), −qmax, qmax ),    x̂ = s · q

Z aligns real 0 with an integer bucket—critical for skewed tensors (mostly positive activations after ReLU). Clamping saturates outliers to 0 or qmax; the demo below visualizes that saturation on a number line.

Picture a hidden weight matrix as, say, 5×5 floats: today each entry might occupy four bytes (fp32). Quantization tries to cover the same dynamic range with eight bits per entry. Raw two’s-complement int8 spans −128…127, but symmetric schemes often sacrifice the −128 code so the quantized range stays symmetric around zero (e.g. −127…127). The forward map is still called quantization; the inverse is dequantization. After a round trip, some entries match the original float exactly; others land nearby but not identically—quantization error is unavoidable when you squeeze a wide fp32 range into a coarse integer lattice.

Bonus Question

Why is the zero point Z forced to be an integer rather than a float?

Keeping Z as an integer guarantees that real 0.0 maps to an exact integer code with no rounding error. This matters because zero-valued activations (from ReLU, padding, masking) are extremely common in neural networks — exact zero representation avoids systematic bias in the quantized output.

Bonus Question

What happens to values that fall outside the [β, α] range after clamping?

They saturate — values below β clamp to integer code 0, values above α clamp to code 255 (for uint8). Those outlier values all reconstruct to the same boundary float, losing all distinction between them. This is why calibration range choice matters so much.

04

Symmetric vs asymmetric; range mapping

Consider a ten-element tensor used pedagogically with extremes β = −44.93 (minimum) and α = 43.31 (maximum). Asymmetric uint8 maps that float interval onto integers 0…255. After the affine map, the largest float tends to land on 255, the smallest on 0, and the real zero lands on the integer zero-point Z—in one worked example Z = 130 when you evaluate round(−β/s) with the corresponding scale. Every other value occupies some interior code between the ends.

Symmetric signed int8 often uses q ∈ [-(2**(n-1)-1), 2**(n-1)-1] (e.g. -127…127), with s = max(|α|,|β|) / qmax and q = clamp(round(x/s), -qmax, qmax), x̂ = s*q. Real zero maps to q=0, which simplifies kernels. Dequantization is simply x̂ = s · q. The pedagogical tensor above is not symmetric around zero (43.31 versus 44.93 in magnitude), yet symmetric quantization still allocates scale from absolute max—here the larger magnitude is 44.93, so the implied symmetric float band is roughly −44.93…44.93 even though your actual maximum is only 43.31. Codes that would represent floats between about 43.31 and 44.93 are never used by data in that tensor, so part of the integer grid is wasted. A concrete round-trip symptom: the original value 43.31 may dequantize to about 43.6—close, but not exact.

If the tensor is not symmetric around zero (e.g. β=-50, α=150), symmetric quantization wastes levels on an empty side and often raises MSE versus asymmetric packing. For a vector spanning −50…150, symmetric scale is driven by max(|−50|, |150|) = 150, so you effectively reserve room for −150…150 even though no element actually occupies the negative tail down to −150. That mismatch inflates the scale s, makes every bin wider, and hurts reconstruction for the bulk of the values.

Bonus Question

After ReLU, activations are always ≥ 0. Which scheme wins and why?

Asymmetric. ReLU outputs are non-negative — using symmetric would waste half the integer codes on negative values that never appear. Asymmetric packs the full 0…255 grid across the actual [0, max] range, giving much tighter bins and lower error.

Bonus Question

Why do symmetric kernels often sacrifice the −128 code in int8 (using −127…127 instead of −128…127)?

Keeping the range symmetric around zero (−127…127) means the positive and negative sides have equal span. This simplifies hardware — the same scale applies identically in both directions, and you avoid edge-case behavior from the asymmetric two's-complement range. The cost is losing one representable value out of 256, which is negligible.

05

Per-tensor vs per-channel granularity

Per-tensor quantization shares one (α, β) (or s, Z) for the entire W: tiny metadata, simple kernels. Per-channel (per output channel of Conv2d / row of Linear) assigns separate scales when channels have different ranges—common in CNNs where filters specialize.

Convolution stacks illustrate the point. Different filters (kernels) often live on different numeric ranges—one might sit roughly in −5…+5, another in −10…+10, another in −6…+6. A single global α/β forces all of them to share one wide bucket; some channels then use only a thin slice of the available integer codes while others could have used a tighter scale. Channel-wise (per-kernel) quantization computes separate α and β per filter, improving code utilization and usually lowering precision loss for the same bit width.

Hardware alignment: CPUs expose fast paths for a few widths—typically 8, 16, 32, and 64 bits. Picking an exotic width like 11 bits may save a little metadata in theory but often forfeits accelerated kernels; the practical compromise matches the integer types your stack actually vectorizes.

Bonus Question

Per-channel gives tighter fits — why not go even finer, like per-element scale?

Per-element would mean storing one float32 scale per weight — you'd end up using more metadata than you saved by quantizing. Per-channel is the sweet spot: typically a few hundred or thousand extra floats (one per output channel), which is negligible compared to millions of int8 weights. Going finer yields diminishing accuracy gains with disproportionate metadata and kernel complexity costs.

Bonus Question

Why is per-channel common for weights but per-tensor more common for activations?

Weight ranges are fixed after training — you compute per-channel scales once and store them. Activations change every forward pass; computing per-channel stats on the fly would add latency to every inference call. Per-tensor keeps it to just one min/max observation per layer, which is fast enough for real-time use.

06

Calibration: min–max, percentiles, and outliers

Min–max (sometimes written minmax) sets α to the tensor maximum and β to the minimum—simple, deterministic, and sensitive to outliers. With values mostly between −50 and +50 but one spike at 1000, that spike stretches s; after quantize–dequantize, ordinary entries drift—for example 43.31 reconstructing near 45.8.

Percentile strategies set α and β to high/low percentiles of the empirical distribution—e.g. the 99th percentile tail, or in code experiments the 99.99th percentile—instead of literal min/max. Values beyond the clipped range saturate, but the bulk of the tensor gets a finer step size. The outlier itself may reconstruct poorly, yet mean-squared error averaged over non-outlier entries often drops sharply compared with min–max.

Beyond min–max and percentiles, practitioners sometimes choose α and β by minimizing mean squared error between originals and their dequantized counterparts—often with a small grid search over candidate ranges because there is no closed form in general. For language-model heads, where the last Linear feeds a softmax and decoding uses greedy or top‑p sampling, what matters is less each logit’s absolute value than the ordering and shape of the probability mass. A cross-entropy objective between the full-precision distribution and the distribution induced after quantize–dequantize can therefore be a better calibration target than raw MSE on logits: you want the largest logit to stay largest and intermediate ratios to distort as little as possible.

Calibration in PTQ: run unlabeled inputs through the model (you are measuring activation statistics, not updating weights), let MinMaxObserver (or HistogramObserver) record ranges per tensor, then bake s, Z into convert()’s quantized modules.

Deep Dive — Bonus Q&A
Bonus Question

If a single outlier at 1000 stretches your scale by 5×, what happens to the MSE of the other 9,999 values?

It jumps dramatically. With min-max, s ≈ 4.12 instead of ≈ 0.784 — each integer bin is 5× wider. A value like 43.31 that would normally reconstruct within ~0.4 error might now be off by ~2.5. The outlier is fine, but the bulk of the distribution pays the price. Percentile calibration (e.g. 99.99th) clips that spike and keeps bins tight for everyone else.

Bonus Question

Why might cross-entropy calibration be better than MSE for language model heads?

The last Linear feeds a softmax — what matters isn't each logit's absolute value but the ordering and shape of the probability distribution. MSE might perfectly reconstruct a logit that doesn't affect the top token, while distorting one that does. Cross-entropy directly penalizes probability-distribution mismatch, keeping the largest logit largest and preserving sampling behavior.

07

Integer matmul and int32 accumulators

Each output element of y = x @ W.T is a sum of products. With int8 operands, each product needs up to 16 bits; summing k terms needs a wider accumulator, typically int32. Biases are often folded in accumulator space before rescaling to the next layer’s scale.

At a micro-architectural level, think of a multiply–accumulate lane that loads short vectors from a row of X and a column of W—a toy picture uses length‑four chunks: multiply x₁₁·w₁₁, x₁₂·w₁₂, and so on, then add into an accumulator register. The operands are int8 after quantization, but their products are wider; the accumulator therefore sits at 32 bits. The bias term is often injected by initializing the accumulator with the (appropriately scaled) bias so the same inner loop finishes the affine form XWᵀ + b. Thousands of such blocks run in parallel across rows and columns—this is the mental model behind accelerated int8 GEMM.

GPU kernels tile work into blocks; each thread maintains an accumulator register. Libraries like cuBLASLt expose mixed-precision GEMM with int8 inputs and int32 accumulation. For algorithmic detail on low-precision matrix multiply, see Google’s open-source https://github.com/google/gemmlowp project—documentation and kernels walk through how reduced-width products widen into int32 accumulators. Picking 8/16/32-bit widths that match hardware beats arbitrary bit depths with no fast path.

Deep Dive — Bonus Q&A
Bonus Question

Why does the accumulator need to be int32 instead of staying int8?

Each int8 × int8 product can be up to 16 bits wide. When you sum hundreds or thousands of such products (one per element in the dot product), the total can easily exceed 16-bit range. int32 accumulators give enough headroom to sum many 16-bit products without overflow. The final result is then rescaled back to the output layer's scale.

Bonus Question

How is the bias typically handled in integer matmul?

The bias is often folded into the accumulator — the int32 accumulator is initialized with a pre-scaled version of the bias before the multiply-accumulate loop begins. This way the same inner loop computes XW^T + b without a separate addition pass, and the bias stays in the wider int32 space where precision is fine.

Post-training quantization (PTQ) pipeline

Train fp32 MNIST MLP (or any small classifier), then torch.quantization.quantize_dynamic is a shortcut—but the full torch.ao.quantization story is: QuantStub at input, DeQuantStub before loss, prepare with qconfig, copy pretrained weights into the prepared skeleton, calibrate with a data loader (forward-only—no backward), then convert to QuantizedLinear. QuantStub and DeQuantStub bracket the region PyTorch rewrites; observers sit on activations (and weight hooks) recording running minima and maxima per layer.

A minimal three-layer ReLU MLP for MNIST might land near 360 KB serialized at full width (parameter bytes only land in the same ballpark as 368640 bytes when you tally numel × element_size). Train that baseline for several epochs (a reference notebook uses on the order of five epochs) before exporting weights. After static conversion, the same checkpoint often shrinks to on the order of 94 KB because weights become int8 plus sidecar scale and zero-point metadata (PyTorch also stores auxiliary fields). That is roughly a fourfold reduction—not magic, just one byte per weight instead of four, with small overhead.

Test accuracy before quantization might sit near 96 % on such a toy model; after PTQ it can remain essentially unchanged on an overparameterized baseline—though on harder tasks you should expect some drop. The important engineering pattern is unchanged: observers first, convert second, then validate on your metric.

Bonus Question

How many calibration samples are typically needed for good PTQ results?

Surprisingly few — often 100–1000 representative samples are enough for activation range statistics to stabilize. The observers are computing simple running min/max or histograms, not fitting complex models. More data rarely hurts but gives diminishing returns. The key is that samples are representative of real inference inputs.

Bonus Question

What's the difference between dynamic PTQ and static PTQ in terms of when quantization happens?

Dynamic PTQ: weights are pre-quantized to int8, but activations are quantized on the fly each forward pass (compute min/max per batch, then quantize). Static PTQ: both weights and activations use pre-baked scales from calibration — no per-batch computation. Static is faster at inference; dynamic is simpler to set up and handles distribution shifts better.

Quantization-aware training (QAT) and STE

prepare_qat inserts FakeQuantize / weight_fake_quant style modules that apply quantize → dequantize in the forward path while weights remain trainable floats. The rounding step is piecewise constant, so its derivative is zero almost everywhere—vanilla backprop would stall. Practitioners therefore use the straight-through estimator (STE): treat the forward as if quantization happened, but in the backward pass approximate ∂q/∂x as 1 for values whose pre-activation sits strictly between the calibrated β and α, and as 0 outside that band (where clamping would have pinned the code). PyTorch’s fused kernels implement the same idea with careful numerics; didactic code often writes x + (x_hat - x).detach() so the residual is treated as constant during autograd.

Why should fake quantization help at all? Picture training without it: gradient descent finds a sharp minimum of the loss in weight space. PTQ then nudges every weight onto a coarse grid; a tiny move on the lattice can hop across a steep wall of the loss. Quantization-aware training exposes those jumps during optimization, nudging solutions toward wider, flatter basins where a small quantization displacement barely moves the loss. In a one-weight cartoon: float training might park w on a narrow cusp; after rounding, w_q sits on the wrong side and loss spikes. QAT encourages a basin where both neighbors on the grid stay cheap.

In a representative MNIST QAT run you might train only one epoch with MinMaxObservers inserted up front; before any step their buffers read like ±∞ sentinels until forward passes populate extrema. After training plus convert, accuracy might land near 0.952—not automatically better than PTQ on every architecture (simple models can look unchanged), but on harder networks QAT usually recovers more accuracy than aggressive PTQ alone.

After QAT epochs, call convert like PTQ; observers updated during training supply ranges. On difficult models, QAT typically beats raw PTQ when PTQ alone bleeds accuracy.

Deep Dive — Bonus Q&A
Bonus Question

The STE approximates ∂round/∂x as 1 inside [β, α] and 0 outside. Why 0 outside?

Values outside [β, α] are clamped — they saturate at the boundary integer code regardless of how much the input changes. Since the clamped output is constant w.r.t. the input, the true gradient is zero there. The STE is actually exact at the boundaries; the approximation is only in the interior where it replaces the staircase derivative with 1.

Bonus Question

Does QAT always beat PTQ?

Not always. On simple, overparameterized models (like a small MNIST MLP), PTQ can maintain accuracy with zero drop — the loss landscape is already flat enough. QAT shines on harder tasks and tighter models where PTQ causes noticeable degradation. QAT also costs more: you need to retrain (or fine-tune) with fake-quant nodes, which adds engineering effort and compute.

Bonus Question

During QAT, are the observers updating their ranges throughout training or fixed from the start?

They update throughout training. As weights shift during backprop, activation ranges change too. The observers track running statistics so that s and Z adapt. At convert time, the final observer states are frozen into the deployed int8 model.
