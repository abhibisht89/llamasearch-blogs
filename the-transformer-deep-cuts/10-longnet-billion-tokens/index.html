LongNet: Scaling Transformers to a Billion Tokens

A Transformer-compatible attention pattern that grows linearly with sequence length while letting nearby tokens interact densely and distant ones interact through shorter, sparser bridges—so context can scale to roughly 10⁹ tokens without the usual quadratic cost.

dilated attention multi-scale segments O(n) · d FLOPs distributed blocks causal masking
00 · Overview

Why extreme context matters

LongNet (Microsoft Research Asia) targets a regime that sounds almost abstract until you put numbers on it: on the order of one billion tokens in context—roughly three orders of magnitude beyond many earlier long-context proposals. For orientation, autoregressive models such as early GPT variants often sat near 512 tokens; other “long sequence” systems in the literature climbed through 12,000, 64,000, 262,000, and into the millions—still 1,000× smaller than a billion-token horizon. At that scale you can think in terms of feeding something on the order of all of Wikipedia’s text into a single forward pass and still reasoning with an attention mechanism that has been reshaped so it does not explode like dense n × n attention.

In autoregressive language modeling, the sequence length sets how many past tokens can jointly influence the next one. Short windows forget earlier clauses, citations, and structure; long windows are desirable—but standard dot-product attention materializes an n × n affinity structure, whose cost scales like n² in the sequence length n.

The paper states the governing idea in one line: attention allocation decreases exponentially as the distance between the tokens grows. Nearby tokens receive many direct, high-resolution comparisons; far-apart tokens meet only through a short chain of intermediates, so their coupling is deliberately weaker.

The authors treat sequence length as one of the central bottlenecks for language models and argue that scaling context should be a first-class research priority. LongNet’s pitch is not merely “longer windows,” but fewer floating-point operations per attention layer relative to vanilla self-attention while preserving a usable path for information to flow across the entire sequence.

LongNet keeps the familiar Transformer stack (projections to Q, K, V, softmax-weighted values, output projection) but replaces dense global attention with a family of dilated, windowed attentions at increasing segment sizes, then mixes those views. Nearby positions see each other often; far positions meet through a small number of hops, so influence weakens with distance in a controlled way.

Properties emphasized in the original work:

  • Linear compute in n (for fixed hyperparameters) when geometric schedules are summed—contrasted with O(n²) vanilla attention.
  • Logarithmic interaction depth between arbitrary token pairs: direct attention is local or dilated, but graphs of allowed edges stay connected with short paths.
  • Embarrassingly parallel segment blocks: disjoint windows do not exchange messages, so blocks map cleanly to multi-GPU or multi-node layouts.
  • Drop-in attention module: swap the attention kernel while keeping embeddings, FFNs, and training recipes largely intact.

Dense global attention relates any two tokens in one layer but costs Θ(n²) in sequence length (see Chapter 01 and the HBM view in Chapter 09). LongNet trades some direct all-pairs coupling for a sparse pattern where distant tokens connect through intermediates, so per-layer work can scale closer to n.

01 · The context-length bottleneck

Vanilla self-attention cost

As in Chapter 01, each head forms scores Q K⊤ with n² pairwise dot products of length dhead. Chapter 09 shows why materializing that n × n grid in HBM becomes the bottleneck at long context. LongNet’s complexity table contrasts vanilla attention at Θ(n² · d) with dilated attention at Θ(n · d) under its geometric schedule—same d, very different scaling in n.

Dense causal self-attention (scores + softmax-weighted values):   O(n² · d)

Memory traffic for materializing full n × n score matrices also becomes prohibitive for large n. LongNet avoids a single dense n × n interaction by decomposing attention into many smaller structured blocks.

Vanilla self-attention

Builds a full pairwise score structure (subject to masking). Operation count commonly summarized as n² · d up to constants: n² cells, each involving a length-d dot product story.

LongNet (dilated multi-scale)

Replaces one dense grid with many windowed, dilated attentions at geometrically increasing w and r, then fuses them. Total attention work scales linearly in n and d in the paper’s accounting—not quadratically in n.

02 · Dilated attention

Segments, windows, and dilation rate

Take a concrete toy length n = 16. The causal mask still blanks the entire upper triangle: position 8 may attend backward to position 1, but position 1 never attends “forward” to 8. LongNet then splits the sequence into segments—contiguous blocks of length w. With w = 4 and n = 16, you get four segments; with w = 8, you get two; with w = 16, the whole sequence is a single segment. At the finest scale you can afford dense-ish attention inside each small segment; at coarser scales the physical window doubles, but you do not keep doubling the number of pairwise dot products without bound.

Partition the sequence into contiguous segments of length w. Inside each segment (with causal masking so a position only attends backward), not every past token inside the window is used: with dilation rate r, you attend on a strided subset—skipping intermediate positions so that the number of keys queried per query stays bounded even when the physical window grows. For example, when you “skip every rth” token in the segment’s indexing story, you are effectively leaving r − 1 positions between each attended neighbor along that residue class. At r = 1 you skip nothing inside the pattern; when the schedule moves to wider windows, you might relate token 0 to token 4, skip three interior indices, relate the next allowed pair, and so on—so the physical window can be large while the active query–key grid stays small.

A useful invariant for dilated causal windows: the number of dot products inside each segment, at a fixed design, can be held constant across scales. In a tiny causal triangle inside a segment of side w, the count of allowed pairs along one residue class behaves like a triangular number—for example 4 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 10 dot-product “slots” in the story told for the first scale—and the construction is arranged so that when you widen w and increase r together, you do not blindly inflate to a full w × w dense block. That is the whole trick: w grows, but the effective side length of the active grid is on the order of w / r.

A useful order-of-magnitude for dot products inside one window is proportional to (w / r)² (each side of the effective grid shrinks by the stride). Disjoint segments under a fixed (w, r) pair can be processed in parallel because no attention edge crosses segment boundaries for that scale. Each segment runs its own softmax normalization over its allowed keys; at inference or training time, one GPU can own one segment’s block while another owns a different segment, because the attention computation for that scale does not need embeddings from tokens outside its segment.

Implementation reality: if you materialize a dense w × w tensor in NumPy or PyTorch and simply zero out forbidden entries, you still pay storage and often time as if the block were dense. The pattern is sparse; production systems store only the allowed edges (or use kernels that never touch the zeros). The LongNet authors discuss custom CUDA attention implementations oriented around that sparsity—same mathematical mask, far less wasted work.

Per-segment attention (one scale):   ∝ (w / r)² · d   ·   (n / w) segments

To see why (w / r)² shows up, suppose w = 8 and r = 2. The window’s side length is eight, but you attend every other index along your residue class, so the effective active grid is about 8 / 2 = 4 by 4—not 8 × 8. Push to w = 16, r = 4: the physical window spans sixteen positions, yet the dot-product grid you actually evaluate is on the order of 4 × 4, because you skip three tokens between consecutive attended keys along that pattern. Multiply by d for the vector depth of each dot product, and multiply by the number of segment tiles n / w (e.g. 16 / 4 = 4 tiles when w = 4, or 16 / 8 = 2 when w = 8) to cover the full sequence at that scale.

A single dilated scale can leave large gaps—token 1 may never score token 16 directly—but overlap across scales reconnects the graph. The union of edges from multiple (w, r) levels yields paths through intermediates (breadth-first search on that union graph makes the routes easy to inspect at n = 16).

03 · Multi-scale mixing

Geometric schedules for w and r

A single (w, r) pair would either miss long-range structure or reintroduce heavy compute. LongNet therefore stacks multiple scales: segment length and dilation grow in a geometric progression (commonly ratio α = 2), e.g. start at w₀ = 4, r₀ = 1, then double w and double r until the window covers the full sequence. Local scales keep fine-grained dependencies; wider, more dilated scales carry broader context cheaply.

Intuitively: a paragraph ties words tightly; a chapter ties sections more loosely; a book ties themes even more loosely—the multi-scale pattern mirrors that hierarchy without dense all-pairs attention at every length.

Local scales (small w, small r) spend budget on tight pairwise coupling; wider scales use larger windows with coarser dilation so compute stays bounded while still capturing chapter- or document-level structure. The geometric ladder of (w, r) pairs implements fine coupling nearby and progressively sparser coupling at longer spans.

04 · Combining scales

From parallel softmaxes to one mixed view

Each scale produces its own causal attention output inside each segment. Rather than keeping them separate forever, LongNet reweights and fuses the scale-specific contributions. The paper’s presentation computes the usual softmax-based attention inside each segment and each scale, then combines scales using the softmax denominators—the partition functions that normalize each local attention distribution—as positive weights in a weighted average (a mixture) of the per-scale outputs. Intuitively, scales whose local distribution was “more peaked” or “more spread out” contribute differently; the denominators encode part of that geometry. The combined representation is a weighted mixture of the per-scale attention maps, conceptually similar to attention-over-attention stories, but grounded in the normalization constants you already computed for numerical stability.

Multi-head variants offset dilated patterns across heads (e.g., phase shifts so different heads sample complementary strides within the same (w, r)), increasing coverage without multiplying window area. Concretely, with four heads, segment length w = 8, and dilation r = 2 (attend every second token along a residue class), head 1 might start at offset 0 and use tokens 0, 2, 4, 6, … while head 2 starts at offset 1 and uses 1, 3, 5, 7, …. When the dilation stride is smaller than the number of heads, different head indices can implement identical sparsity patterns—in the worked example, heads 1 and 3 coincide for that reason. When the stride grows to be at least the number of heads, you can obtain four distinct phase patterns—four genuinely different sparsity masks—so the head dimension buys diversity instead of redundancy.

Presentation detail: some expositions describe the multi-scale outputs as a concatenation followed by a projection; the paper’s mixture view using softmax normalization constants is the same family of idea—combine per-scale segment attentions into one wide context vector—with weights tied to the local partition functions rather than a fresh learned gate (implementation variants may differ in how fusion is packaged).

05 · Complexity

Why the sum of scales stays linear in n

For one scale, work per layer is roughly (n / w) · (w / r)² · d = n · w / r² · d (up to constants). Along a geometric ladder where both w and r scale by α, the per-term w / r² stays constant across levels when w and r share the same ratio progression; only the O(log n) number of levels enters, giving overall attention FLOPs often summarized as O(n · d · log n) across levels—or, when depth is treated as constant in fixed schedules, O(n · d) in coarse big-O comparisons to O(n² · d) dense attention. The key qualitative point from the source narrative: combined FLOPs grow linearly with n and d, not quadratically in n like dense attention.

Another way to say the same thing: the expensive piece is always “how many dot products per segment” times “how many segments.” Per segment you target Θ((w/r)²) dot products at depth d; per scale you have Θ(n/w) segments. The paper fixes w and r along geometric sequences with ratio α—commonly α = 2 starting from w₀ = 4 and r₀ = 1, then doubling both each level until the window covers the sequence. Summing those level costs is where the linear-in-n story closes: you are not stacking independent quadratic regimes; you are stacking a bounded “local grid” cost per level and only O(log n) levels.

One-scale attention work (order of magnitude):   (n / w) · (w / r)² · d  =  n · w / r² · d

06 · Connectivity & implementation

Reachability, parallelism, and kernels

Even when two positions never share a direct attention edge at any scale, overlapping multi-scale windows create paths through intermediates: early tokens connect to mid-sequence anchors, which connect forward under wider dilations. The maximum hop count to link arbitrary pairs grows like O(log n), matching the intuition that long-range influence is present but exponentially weaker than short-range coupling—exactly the desired inductive bias for billion-token contexts.

Worked example on n = 16 (indices 0 … 15): there is no single scale where token 0 directly scores token 15, yet you can route information 0 → 12 → 15. Token 12 always has a self-attention dot product in the causal setup; if 12 attends to 15 at some scale, then 0’s representation—after layers and residual paths—can incorporate content that passed through 12. The paper’s claim is systematic: every position remains reachable from every earlier position through some finite chain, and the length of the shortest such chain grows only logarithmically in n—so if you make the sequence ten times longer, you do not need ten times more hops in the worst case.

For n = 32, the same idea appears when linking 0 to 17: there may be no direct edge, but 0 → 16 → 17 works when 16 lies in a wide enough dilated window to touch 17 while 0 reaches 16 through coarser structure. That is exactly what “logarithmic dependency between tokens” buys you algorithmically: global connectivity without global dense attention.

In a multi-colored overlap diagram for n = 32, you can read off local cliques directly: token 0 shares a segment with token 3 at the finest scale, while 4 and 5 sit in the same small box when their attention block is computed—those are the cheap, dense interactions. Tokens outside that immediate clique still end up reachable from every other token, but the shortest path length varies; the spread of those path lengths is what people summarize as logarithmic in n.

Interpreting hops as strength: if two tokens share a direct attention score in a given layer, the model can relate them in one attention step. If they relate only through intermediates, optimization must spread credit across multiple layers and residual updates—the effective coupling is weaker. The LongNet narrative matches the paper here: attention mass is arranged so that influence weakens sharply with graph distance, analogous to an exponential falloff in allocation even though the computational budget stays linear in n.

Implementation notes: naive dense tensors inside each window would still square with window side length; production systems exploit sparsity patterns (store only allowed edges) and often custom GPU kernels that skip zero pairs. For analysis and debugging, a breadth-first search over the union of allowed attention edges visualizes shortest informational paths between token indices—useful didactically even when training uses optimized code paths.

Distributed training: at a fixed scale, segment blocks are independent: to compute attention inside the block covering tokens 0–3 you only need those four embeddings; a dilated block might touch indices 0, 2, 4, 6 without needing the full dense neighborhood of unrelated tokens. Because the number of dot products per block can be held near constant by design, you can shard segments across GPUs or nodes with predictable per-device work—parallelism is not an afterthought but a consequence of the mask geometry.

Empirical runtime curves in the paper show attention cost tracking roughly linearly with sequence length for LongNet, while vanilla Transformers curve upward consistent with quadratic growth—same hardware, different scaling exponent in n. Exact wall-clock numbers depend on kernels, batch size, and width; the qualitative crossing point is what matters for “billion token” ambitions.

Reference implementations evolve quickly.
